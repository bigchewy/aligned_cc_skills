## Who

Damon Centola is the Elihu Katz Professor of Communication, Sociology and Engineering at the
University of Pennsylvania, and Director of the Network Dynamics Group at the Annenberg School. ✅
(Penn Press/MIT Press author bio, 2026.) He is a 2026 Guggenheim Fellow. ✅ His work is mostly
controlled experiments on online social networks, plus field studies in health and agriculture.

## Selected Slot

How a costly, risky, or norm-breaking practice spreads through an organization, and in what order
to seed it. He answers: which group goes first, which groups go next, how the groups must be
connected for the practice to cross between them, and how many committed people a group needs
before its norm flips.

The slot excludes org structure and role fit (Lex Sisney), validated learning and pivots (Eric
Ries), the content of the practice itself (for conscious leadership, Diana Chapman and Jim
Dethmer), individual habit design (Kristen Berman), and pilot design as a sales motion (Danny Iny).

## Master Concepts (encode these)

- **Simple vs complex contagion.** ✅ "Simple (or viral) contagions, such as the transmission of the
  flu or measles, spread through a single activated contact. Complex contagions, such as the
  adoption of new behaviors, require multiple sources of exposure." (MIT SMR 2018)
- **Why a contagion is complex.** ✅ Four mechanisms in the MIT SMR article: strategic
  complementarity (value rises with the number of adopters), credibility, legitimacy, emotional
  contagion. In Change, these appear as four barriers: coordination, credibility, legitimacy,
  excitement. 🟡
- **Multiple sources, not multiple exposures.** ✅ "Whereas multiple exposures to the same individual
  may be sufficient for simple contagions to spread, multiple sources of exposure are needed to
  transmit complex contagions."
- **Redundancy, not reach.** ✅ "Redundancy, not reach, is the mantra for diffusing complex
  contagions."
- **Narrow vs wide bridges.** ✅ Bridges can be measured by length and by width, "the number of ties
  they contain." A narrow bridge is one tie between two groups. "A signal that travels across a
  narrow bridge arrives alone, without any social reinforcement." A wide bridge is several people
  on each side with contacts in common. "Wide bridges endure even as individuals come and go."
- **Brokers are poor carriers of practices.** ✅ Three reasons in MIT SMR: a single broker is not
  necessarily trusted; one group's practice may not seem useful to another; a single broker is a
  fragile bridge.
- **Networking across silos can hurt adoption inside the team.** ✅ If a team spends its ties outside,
  "an initiative to spread a new innovation within their team may fail."
- **The 25% tipping point.** ✅ Committed minority; experimental; not universal. Centola: "When a
  community is close to a tipping point to cause large-scale social change, there's no way they
  would know this." ✅ (ScienceDaily quote)
- **Three seeding strategies.** 🟡 Shotgun (seeds spread as far apart as possible), silver bullet
  (all effort on one well-connected influencer), snowball (seeds placed together in one cluster so
  they reinforce each other). The Malawi pit-planting field study is the case; summaries report
  snowball seeding beat the others.
- **The influencer myth.** 🟡 Highly central people face the most countervailing pressure and often
  adopt late. Change starts at the periphery. Twitter spread geographically before Oprah's tweet ✅
  (MIT SMR).
- **Relevance.** 🟡 Three principles (quoted consistently across summaries): similarity to early
  adopters matters when people need proof it works for them; similarity matters when the change
  needs excitement or loyalty; diversity among adopters matters when the change turns on
  legitimacy, the belief that it is widely accepted.
- **Protect early adopters.** 🟡 Listed among the seven strategies in Change: keep the first
  adopters from facing too much opposition until they reinforce each other.
- **Fishing nets vs fireworks.** 🟡 Tight, redundant networks vs loose, one-link networks.

## Voice

- Scientist who talks in experiments. "In one experiment..." "We tested this." Numbers, then the
  lesson.
- Starts by knocking down a common belief: go viral, find the influencer, make the message sticky.
- Repeats a few paired contrasts: simple vs complex, reach vs reinforcement, narrow vs wide,
  influencers vs periphery, information vs behavior.
- Friendly and plain. Uses stories (Twitter, hybrid corn, Malawi farmers, Sweden's H-Day switch).
- Cautious about his own numbers. He says the 25% figure varies and that people near a tipping
  point cannot see it.
- Thinks in pictures of networks: who is connected to whom, how many ties cross between groups.

## Known critiques (for the Failure Modes section)

- The tipping-point experiment used a low-stakes naming game with strangers online. Real norms are
  entrenched and carry real costs. Centola himself says the number varies with "memory length." ✅
  Critics (for example James Ozden, 2023 essay) argue social tipping points are overstated. 🟡
- Some large field studies find weak ties do carry real outcomes (for example job mobility in
  LinkedIn experiments). The complex/simple split is a spectrum, and it takes judgment to say which
  kind a given practice is. 🟡
- The theory tells you where to seed and how to connect. It says little about what makes the
  practice itself good, or about the manager's support, which transfer-of-training research says
  matters most (Blume et al. 2010, from the brief).
- Network maps inside real companies are rough guesses unless someone collects the data.
