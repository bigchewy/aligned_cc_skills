# Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic — Research

The board's first data visualization advisor. Nobody else in the registry decides what chart to
draw, what to cut from it, or what its title should say.

Research started from the find-advisor brief
`docs/plans/2026-11-01-find-advisor-data-visualization-brief.md`. Eric picked her on 2026-11-01
because he wants classic business charts and trends, not statistics or uncertainty. He also asked
that the whole storytelling with data (SWD) organization's material be used, not only what she
wrote herself. Every page below was opened on 2026-11-01. Each item says what was confirmed and
where.

Rule for this file: a quote under **Voice** is Cole's own words. Practices by her team are under
**Organization practices**, with the real author named. They can be taught as SWD practice. They
are never quoted as things Cole said.

## Organization practices

Each line names the author. Practices by team members are SWD practice, not Cole quotes.

**Start from the audience and the point**
- Answer who the audience is and what they should know or do before touching the data. Only then
  ask what data helps make the point. — Cole, book ch. 1 ([PDF](https://media.wiley.com/product_data/excerpt/57/11190022/1119002257-4.pdf))
- Dashboards are built for exploring, not for making one point. To get a change made, build a
  separate communication for the one story. "Providing too much data runs the risk of overwhelming
  our audience." — Amy Esselman ([from dashboard to story](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/from-dashboard-to-story))
- "connect the dots for them to make it clear what the point is and what action they should take."
  Spell the point out in words in a prominent place. — Amy Esselman ([is your point clear?](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/is-your-point-clear))

**Titles that state the takeaway**
- Use the title for the takeaway, not a description. — Cole ([so what?](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/2017/3/22/so-what))
- "The title of any document is often the first place an audience will look." Add a summary view
  to any deck that will be sent around. Avoid bullets in it unless listing things. — Alex Velez
  ([executive summary slides](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/2022/1/14/executive-summary-slides))

**Declutter**
- Remove the chart border. Delete gridlines. Be sparing with data labels; label key points
  directly or use the axis instead. Move the legend next to the data. Use color sparingly. Question
  the tool's defaults. — Elizabeth Ricks ([declutter!](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/2019/5/13/declutter-and-question-defaults))
- Don't label every point. — Cole ([data labels](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/2012/06/drawing-attention-with-data-labels))
- Decluttering alone isn't enough. A cleaned-up chart can still be the wrong chart. Often a subset
  or total of the data is clearer than all of it. — Simon Rowe ([when decluttering isn't enough](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/when-decluttering-isnt-enough))
- Research backs this up. In the 2021 study, decluttering alone made charts look more professional
  but didn't help people get the main point. Decluttering plus focus (a headline that states the
  trend, and the key bars or lines in a different color) made people about 2.5 times as likely to
  get it. Some found the focused versions heavy-handed. — Ajani, Lee, Xiong, Knaflic, Kemper,
  Franconeri ([Kellogg Insight](https://insight.kellogg.northwestern.edu/article/do-people-understand-your-data-visualizations))

**Focus attention with color**
- Make everything light grey first, then add color only where you want the eye to go. — Cole
  ([biggest bang](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/2015/03/the-biggest-bang-for-your-buck))
- Check it: look away, look back, note where your eyes land first. — Cole ([where are your eyes drawn?](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/whereareyoureyesdrawn))
- Override the tool's default palette and start with nothing emphasized. — Elizabeth Ricks
  ([the most important dataviz decision](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/2021/1/11/the-most-important-dataviz-decision-you-make))
- Words and color focus attention in both a quiet report and a crowded feed, but the tactics
  differ; a crowded setting may need color used "very differently, just to break through the
  noise." — Mike Cisneros ([use color to focus or to compete](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/use-color-to-focus-or-to-compete-for-attention))

**Choose an effective visual**
- Bars compare categories. Lines show change over time. Dot plots and slopegraphs show change
  between two points. Use something unfamiliar only when it shows something the familiar chart
  can't. — Cole ([choose an appropriate visual](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/swd-ai-choose-an-appropriate-visual))
- Use a line only when the line means something: across time, yes; across categories, no. — Cole
  ([when bars win](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/when-bars-winand-when-lines-do));
  same point by Simon Rowe ([when decluttering isn't enough](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/when-decluttering-isnt-enough))
- Too many lines (a spaghetti graph): split them into panels with the same y-axis range, or keep
  all lines and highlight one at a time. — Cole ([spaghetti graph](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/2013/03/avoiding-spaghetti-graph))
- Bar charts start at zero, because length carries the value. Sort bars by value when categories
  have no natural order. Use horizontal bars for long labels. Keep the gap thinner than the bar
  (30–40% gap width in Excel). — Alex Velez ([what is a bar chart?](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/2020/2/19/what-is-a-bar-chart))
- Horizontal bars make category labels easier to read. Show a range as one connected dot plot or
  one bar; show a single estimate as a point. — Amy Esselman ([oh my!](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/bar-charts-and-dot-plots-and-line-graphs-oh-my))
- Use a slopegraph when the "so what?" is what changed over time. — Elizabeth Ricks
  ([visualizing change via slopegraph](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/2017/9/19/visualizing-change))

**Pie charts**
- SWD does not ban pies. Most complaints are about pies used badly. — Cole ([make a perfect pie](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/2020/8/2/swdchallenge-make-a-perfect-pie))
- A pie is only for part-to-whole. Its weakness is that slices are hard to compare. Label slices
  directly, use color to point at the slice that matters, and annotate the "so what?" — Elizabeth
  Ricks ([better pie chart](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/2020/2/26/how-to-make-a-better-pie-chart))

**Phones and small screens**
- The mobile challenge was created by Andy Cotgreave; Mike Cisneros wrote up the results. Limit
  interactivity on phones. Sketch on paper or sticky notes first. Decide what the audience must
  see first and make that the first screen. Vertical scrolling is "often a safe choice." — Mike
  Cisneros ([you can take it with you](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/2019/8/20/you-can-take-it-with-you))

**Slides and text**
- Fix a wordy slide three ways: balance the layout, format text so it can be scanned, and use only
  images that support the message. — Alex Velez ([wordy slide makeover](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/a-wordy-slide-makeover))
- Storyboard on sticky notes before opening slide software. — Cole, book ch. 1 and DataFramed #242

### Recommended top 3
