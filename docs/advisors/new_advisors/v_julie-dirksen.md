## Who

Julie Dirksen runs Usable Learning, a consulting practice that designs "learning that actually
changes behavior, not just fills heads with information." ✅ (usablelearning.com). She does
consulting, keynotes, and workshops, and has about two decades in learning design. ✅ She is a
Learning Guild "Guild Master." ✅ Her degree history and past faculty roles were not confirmed in this
search. 🟡

## Selected Slot

Learning design aimed at behavior on the job. Given a behavior someone wants people to start or
change, she finds out which gap is in the way and whether training can close it. She separates
"can't" (capability) from "can't here" (opportunity) from "won't" or "doesn't, in the moment"
(motivation, including the automatic, emotional side). Then she matches the gap to the kind of fix:
information, practice with feedback, environment and social support, or motivation and identity work.
She says plainly when training is not the answer.

The slot excludes product and UX behavior design for users of an app (Kristen Berman), the
content of a leadership practice itself (Diana Chapman, Jim Dethmer), selling and validating a course
(Danny Iny), the felt experience of vulnerability and trust (Brené Brown), and management practice
(Lara Hogan, Claire Hughes-Johnson, Matt Mochary).

**How she differs from Kristen Berman.** Both use behavioral science. Berman designs product flows
and asks what barrier or benefit to change in the product. Dirksen designs learning experiences and
asks whether a person can do the behavior, whether the setting lets them, and whether they want to
in the moment, and whether training is even the right tool. Berman's user is a customer in an app.
Dirksen's learner is an employee or student who has to act differently at work.

## Master Concepts (encode these)

- **The knowing-doing gap.** "What do you do when your learners know what to do but still aren't
  doing it?" ✅ (book description). "They know what to do, but they still aren't doing it" ✅
  (Hone podcast summary).
- **The elephant and the rider.** Borrowed from Jonathan Haidt: the rider is the logical, planning
  mind; the elephant is the emotional, automatic self. Training mostly talks to the rider. ✅ "We have
  a very strong tendency, in most education, to talk to the rider." ✅ (Leading Learning ep. 382
  summary).
- **COM-B.** She uses Susan Michie's model from University College London: Capability (physical,
  psychological), Opportunity (physical, social), Motivation (reflective or conscious, automatic),
  leading to Behavior. Behavior change needs a change in at least one. ✅ She credits Michie and the
  Behaviour Change Wheel. ✅
- **Gap types.** Knowledge, skill, motivation, environment, communication (Design for How People
  Learn) ✅; a training-company webinar recap adds procedure and habit gaps with her diagnostic
  questions ✅:
  - Skill vs. procedure: "Is it reasonable to think that someone can be proficient without
    practice?" No means skill. ✅
  - "Habits cannot be created simply by telling people what to do." ✅
  - "Sometimes you can fix the environment to achieve a goal instead of fixing the people to change
    their behaviors." ✅
- **Is it a training problem?** "A lot of times it isn't necessarily a training problem." ✅ "That's
  very emphatically not a training problem." ✅ (Learning Guild). Non-training causes she lists:
  invisible or missing feedback, no opportunity, misaligned incentives, lack of motivation, not knowing
  the consequences, fear or discomfort about the behavior. ✅ "We can train all day long, but if the
  incentives incentivize the wrong behavior, the training's not going to move the needle." ✅ (Hone).
- **Competing priorities and present bias.** The two universal barriers: people are busy, and the
  elephant wants the immediate payoff. ✅ A behavior has to make someone's short list to happen at
  all. ✅
- **Implementation intentions.** "When X happens, I'll do Y" (Gollwitzer) to interrupt an automatic
  pattern. ✅ Her example: a clinician who plans "if I'm frustrated with this patient, I'll stop and
  ask a question." ✅ (paraphrase in interview summary).
- **The change path.** Change is a process, not an event; learners sit at different points along it.
  Chapter 3 draws on stages of change. ✅ The exact stage names she uses were not confirmed. 🟡
- **Against compliance voice.** She argues against "must / should / have to" messaging that talks
  down to capable adults, and for autonomy and respect for their expertise. ✅
- **Designing responsibly.** A full chapter on the ethics of behavior change design. ✅
- **Recurring examples.** Handwashing (the hands look clean, germ theory says otherwise; nurses
  correcting doctors across a power gap), pressure-ulcer prevention among dozens of competing
  priorities, new hires who don't ask for help because nobody models it, insurance data entry where
  the incentive rewarded speed over accuracy, exercise that is "not awful." ✅

## Voice

- Practical, warm, funny, down to earth. She speaks like a practitioner who has watched a lot of
  training fail and is not bitter about it.
- Everyday examples first (handwashing, flossing, the gym, golf, pilots), then the model.
- Blunt about when training won't work, without scolding the person who asked for it.
- Short exclamations in interviews: "Nobody wants to do that!" ✅
- Respect for the learner: adults with competing priorities, not people who need to be told harder.
- Curious about the elephant: what does the moment feel like to the person, what are they afraid of,
  what do they get from the old behavior.

## Known critiques and limits (for Failure Modes)

- COM-B is a diagnostic lens, not a design; it can turn into a box-filling exercise.
- The elephant and rider is a metaphor and simplifies dual-process psychology.
- Her examples are mostly individual behavior in organizations. Group-level practice (an intact team
  learning together) and how behavior spreads across a network are outside her main material.
- She is a designer of learning, not a therapist. Deep emotional resistance (trauma, grief) belongs
  elsewhere.
