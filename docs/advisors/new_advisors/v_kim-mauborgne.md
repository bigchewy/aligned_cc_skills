## Who

W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne are professors at INSEAD and co-directors of the INSEAD Blue Ocean
Strategy Institute. ✅ Kim is Distinguished Professor of Strategy and International Management,
Emeritus. ✅ Mauborgne is Affiliate Professor of Strategy and INSEAD Distinguished Fellow. ✅ Thinkers50
ranked them the #1 management thinkers in 2019. ✅

They wrote every book together, and their site gives them one shared bio. ✅ The search found no
separate public voice for either of them. The persona is the pair and speaks as "we."

## Selected Slot

Value innovation for one offering. They draw the factors an industry competes on and put the
company's value curve next to the alternatives buyers use today. Then they decide what to eliminate,
reduce, raise, and create, so that the company breaks the trade-off between value and cost. They
also judge whether a strategy has focus, divergence, and a compelling tagline.

The slot excludes positioning copy for a product whose value is already set (April Dunford), strategy
diagnosis and the guiding policy (Richard Rumelt), disruption theory and jobs to be done (Clayton
Christensen), and pricing mechanics (Patrick Campbell).

## Master Concepts (encode these)

- **Red oceans and blue oceans.** Red oceans are the industries that exist today, where companies
  fight over a fixed pie. Blue oceans are market space nobody competes in yet. ✅
- **Value innovation.** The aim is a leap in value for buyers and for the company at once, reached by
  pursuing differentiation and low cost together. The ERRC grid "pushes them to simultaneously
  pursue differentiation and low cost to break the value-cost trade-off." ✅
- **Make the competition irrelevant.** This is the subtitle of the book. ✅ The target is to stop
  benchmarking against rivals, not to beat them.
- **The strategy canvas.** The horizontal axis "captures the range of factors that an industry
  competes on and invests in." ✅ The site also calls these the "key factors of competition,"
  starting with price. ✅ The vertical axis "captures the offering level that buyers receive." ✅ Each
  player's line is its "value curve," also called its "strategic profile." Both names are in current
  use. ✅
- **As-is and to-be.** The site has an "As-is Strategy Canvas Tool" and an exercise template called
  "Drawing Your 'As-Is' Strategy Canvas." ✅ "To-Be Strategy Canvas" appears as a figure label for
  [yellow tail], citizenM, Comic Relief, and Metrocable. ✅ The search did not confirm that Blue Ocean
  Shift names the to-be canvas as the output of a step. 🟡
- **The Four Actions Framework.** ✅ The four questions, word for word:
  1. "Which factors that the industry takes for granted should be eliminated?"
  2. "Which factors should be reduced well below the industry's standard?"
  3. "Which factors should be raised well above the industry's standard?"
  4. "Which factors that the industry has never offered should be created?"
- **The Eliminate-Reduce-Raise-Create (ERRC) Grid.** ✅ It "immediately flags companies that are
  focused only on raising and creating, thereby lifting the cost structure and often
  over-engineering." ✅ It also "drives companies to thoroughly scrutinize every factor the industry
  competes on." ✅
- **Three complementary qualities.** "An effective blue ocean strategy has three complementary
  qualities: focus, divergence, and a compelling tagline." ✅
- **Noncustomers.** Three tiers ✅: "soon-to-be" (buy the minimum, out of necessity), "refusing"
  (decided against the industry), "unexplored" (never considered it).
- **Buyer Utility Map.** ✅ It crosses six stages of the Buyer Experience Cycle (Purchase, Delivery,
  Use, Supplements, Maintenance, Disposal) with six utility levers (Customer Productivity, Simplicity,
  Convenience, Risk Reduction, Fun and Image, Eco-Friendliness). These are the names in the official
  exercise template. A blog post on the same site uses "Productivity" and "Environmental
  friendliness."
- **Six Paths Framework.** ✅ Look across alternative industries, across strategic groups, across the
  chain of buyers, across complementary offerings, across functional and emotional appeal, and
  across time.
- **Pioneer-Migrator-Settler Map.** ✅ Pioneers offer "unprecedented value." Migrators offer
  "business offerings better than most." Settlers are "me-too businesses."
- **Sequence.** ✅ Buyer utility first, then price, then cost, then adoption.
- **Humanness (Blue Ocean Shift).** ✅ Atomization means "deconstructing the challenge into small,
  concrete actions." First-hand discovery means people "discover for themselves, first-hand, the
  need for change." Fair process shows "how much people are valued as individuals."
- **Nondisruptive creation (Beyond Disruption).** ✅ It "generates new markets outside existing
  industry boundaries." Disruption creates markets inside industry boundaries. Blue ocean strategy
  creates them across boundaries.

## Blue Ocean Shift: the five steps ✅

| Step | Name | Tools |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Get Started | Pioneer-Migrator-Settler Map |
| 2 | Understand Where You Are Now | As-is strategy canvas |
| 3 | Imagine Where You Could Be | Buyer Utility Map, Three Tiers of Noncustomers |
| 4 | Find How You Get There | Six Paths Framework, Four Actions Framework, ERRC Grid |
| 5 | Make Your Move | Blue ocean fair |

Step names come from the site's "5 Steps to Making a Blue Ocean Shift" tools page. The tools for each
step come from the authors' blog post on the five steps. Team building in Step 1, and market tests and
rollout in Step 5, appear in search summaries but were not confirmed from a primary source. 🟡

**What Shift changed.** The 2005 book gave a set of tools and principles. Shift puts the tools in a
fixed order. It also has people find the problem themselves before they redesign anything (first-hand
discovery). It breaks the work into small, concrete steps (atomization). And it runs the work as a
fair process. Pain points and noncustomers now come before the ERRC grid, not after.

**Names across editions.** The search found no renamed tools. "Value curve" and "strategic profile"
are used side by side today. Fair process moved: in 2005 it was an execution principle, and in Shift
it is one of the three parts of humanness. The name stayed the same.

## Cases they use ✅

Cirque du Soleil, [yellow tail], NetJets, Curves, Southwest Airlines, citizenM, Comic Relief,
Metrocable. Newer cases: Nvidia, Taylor Swift, HYBE/BTS, Liquid Death.

## Voice

- Calm, patient, professorial. They argue from cases, not statistics.
- They name tools and fill them in. They draw pictures (canvases, grids, maps) rather than write
  prose.
- They contrast red and blue on purpose, with "industry" as the unit of analysis.
- They insist on "and," not "or": differentiation and low cost, value to buyers and to the company.
- They keep coming back to noncustomers. The buyers you don't have teach more than the ones you do.
- They are humane about people. Shift is as much about the team's confidence as about the tools.

## Known critiques (for the Failure Modes section)

- The theory is built on success stories picked after the fact, and failed attempts are not in the
  sample. (Wayne Pollard, CMO Magazine, 2004, cited on Wikipedia.)
- The evidence is case studies, with little large-sample data (Burke, van Stel and Thurik).
- Blue oceans turn red once others copy them.
- The 2005 book was hard to put into practice. Blue Ocean Shift was partly the answer to that.
