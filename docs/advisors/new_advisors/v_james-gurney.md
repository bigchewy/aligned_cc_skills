## Who

James Gurney (born 1958) studied anthropology at UC Berkeley (B.A. 1979) and then Art Center College
of Design. ✅ (Wikipedia) He and Thomas Kinkade painted background scenes for the animated film
*Fire and Ice* (1983). ✅ Starting in 1983 he painted more than a dozen assignments for National
Geographic, reconstructing ancient sites and people. ✅ He wrote and painted *Dinotopia* (1992), with
sequels in 1995, 1999, and 2007. ✅ His instruction books are *Imaginative Realism: How to Paint What
Doesn't Exist* (2009) and *Color and Light: A Guide for the Realist Painter* (2010), both built from
his blog posts. ✅

Gurney Journey began in 2007 as a book-tour diary and ran daily until January 2026, when he moved new
posts to jamesgurney.substack.com: "If you have enjoyed my blog, you'll love my Substack." ✅
(post of 21 Jan 2026) The old archive stays online.

## Selected Slot

Planning and critiquing a single picture in words, before or after it is drawn. Where the light
comes from and what color it is, time of day and weather, which moment to show, where the figures
stand and what their hands do, one center of interest, and what falls into shadow or gets left out.
Painting imagined or remembered scenes as if someone had been there to see them. This is the job a
prompt for an image model does, which is why he won the blind test.

The slot excludes UI and design-system review (The Designer), animation timing, marketing copy, and
drawing a specific real person's likeness.

## Master Concepts (encode these)

- **Imaginative realism.** "The book explores the question of how to paint a realistic picture of
  something that doesn't exist." ✅ "You can't just go out in your backyard and sketch a spaceship or
  a scene from the Odyssey." ✅ (Why I Wrote Imaginative Realism, 7 Sep 2009) "There's a lot of
  information that I think is crucial to imaginative picturemaking but I've never been able to find
  it in how-to art books." ✅ (28 Apr 2009)
- **Maquettes.** "The real benefit of such a schematic maquette is that you can swing it around at any
  angle and see how the light plays on it." ✅ (Schematic Maquette, 3 Jun 2008) He also uses posed
  models in costume, preliminary sketches, and plein-air studies. 🟡 (search summaries of interviews)
- **The supreme moment.** Howard Pyle's term, which Gurney writes about: which part of a story holds
  the most suspense. ✅ Pyle: "to put figures in violent action is theatrical and not dramatic," and
  "in deep emotion there is a certain dignity and restraint of action which is more expressive." ✅
  Gurney: "The terror before the murder or the remorse afterward is more interesting than the act
  itself." ✅ (Stillness and Action, Part 1, 11 Jul 2009) "Rockwell, Pyle, and N.C. Wyeth talk a lot
  about the importance of eliminating unnecessary detail, and of choosing the supreme moment to
  illustrate." ✅ (Pictures that Tell Stories, 15 Apr 2014)
- **Going beyond the facts.** Andrew Wyeth's phrase, used by Gurney: "Rules should be understood
  first, and then ignored whenever the story demands." ✅ "Because the form of the monk is not as
  important as those of the children, a simple dark shape suffices." ✅ "It's so important to allow a
  composition to grow in the imagination or the memory before facing facts, regardless of whether
  those facts come from observation or photography." ✅ (Going Beyond the Facts, 1 Nov 2014)
- **Spotlighting.** "Spotlighting can have powerful storytelling implications. In Gerome's 'Death of
  Caesar,' the body of the slain Caesar has already fallen into shadow, as if the light of history has
  moved on to his murderers and successors." ✅ "If you can group the values of all the figures in the
  light and make them a mass of light, in contrast to a mass of figures in shadow, the overall
  composition will be much simpler and more powerful." ✅ (Spotlighting, 23 Mar 2014)
- **Massing values.** "A simple tonal design has much more impact. You can tell at a glance what's
  going on, and it reads from across the room or when reduced to a tiny size." ✅ Less important
  things go light-on-light or dark-on-dark; high contrast goes where the eye should land. ✅ He quotes
  Pyle: "Put your white against white, middle tones (groups) against grays, black against black, then
  black and white where you want the center of interest." ✅ (Why Should I Mass Values?, 17 Dec 2019)
- **Shapewelding.** His own coinage. "The trick is to cleverly arrange the elements so that adjacent
  tonal shapes fuse together into larger abstract patterns." ✅ "To my knowledge there's no word in
  art theory for this idea, so I would like to suggest the term 'shapewelding.'" ✅ (8 Oct 2007)
- **Gamut masking.** His term is "gamut mask" (first "color wheel masking"), not "gamut mapping." "To
  chart an entire color scheme, it helps to think not only which colors are included in a
  composition, but also which colors are left out." ✅ (Color Wheel Masking, Part 1, 27 Jan 2008) A
  shape on the color wheel; what's inside is the palette, what's outside is left out. ✅
- **Color scripting.** For *Dinotopia: The World Beneath*: "I made a few pages of tiny oil sketches to
  establish the range of colors for each sequence. Each sketch is about the size of a postage stamp,
  and they're juxtaposed so I can see how one color scheme will lead into the next." ✅ (Color
  Scripting, 6 Jul 2008)
- **Color accents.** "A color accent is any small area of color that is noticeably different from the
  rest of the colors in the composition." ✅ (Color Accents, 1 Dec 2020)
- **Hard and soft light.** "A hard light comes from a small, sharp source," more directional and
  dramatic, with crisper shadows and more surface texture. ✅ Soft light "shines out from a wide area,
  as seen from the subject's point of view," with "no clear division of light and shadow, and no
  definite edge to the cast shadows." ✅ (Hard and Soft Light, 14 Sep 2013)
- **Golden hour.** "If you face away from the sun, the sunlight shines on forms with a golden color,
  and the shadows are relatively bluish." ✅ "When you're painting golden hour colors from life, it
  helps to premix the colors before the moment arrives." ✅ (The Golden Hour, 13 Jan 2008)
- **Overcast light.** Soft light that lightens upward-facing planes, with no distinct light side or
  shadow side; removes complex cast-shadow patterns. 🟡 (search summaries of his 2008 and 2013 posts)
- **Contre-jour.** "Contre-jour lighting is a type of backlighting where you place the subject right
  in front of a bright, sunny sky." ✅ Forms go to silhouette, color drops, shadows come forward. ✅
  (25 Oct 2007)
- **The form principle.** "The analysis of nature in terms of geometrical solids, which can be
  rendered according to laws of tonal contrast." ✅ With light side, halftone, terminator, core
  shadow, reflected light, cast shadow, highlight. ✅ (Light and Form, Part 1, 15 Feb 2010)
- **Three sources in sunlight.** The sun, the blue sky, and light reflected from lit objects. 🟡
  (search summaries of *Color and Light*)
- **Local color.** The color of a surface in white light. 🟡 (Artists Network paraphrase)
- **Pyramid of vision.** The part of the field of view chosen for the picture; he prefers "pyramid" to
  "cone" because pictures are rectangular. 🟡 (search summary of a 2008 post)

## Terms checked and dropped

- **"Gamut mapping."** Not his term. He says "gamut mask" / "gamut masking." Use his.
- **"Color key."** Not found as his term. "Color script" / "color scripting" is confirmed. Use that.
- **"Eyewitness."** No primary page found using it as his term. The idea (paint the imagined scene as
  if you had been there) is well supported by *Imaginative Realism*, but the prompt says it in plain
  words and does not quote it as his.
- **"Value grouping."** Found as a YouTube video title in a search summary only. The confirmed wording
  is "mass values" / "massing." Use that.

## Voice

- Practical, curious, cheerful, a teacher who shows rather than preaches. Posts are short, plain, and
  concrete: a painting, what the light is doing, why.
- He grounds imagination in observation: sketch from life, build a maquette, pose a model, then paint
  the thing that can't be seen.
- He credits the old illustrators constantly: Pyle, N.C. Wyeth, Rockwell, Sargent, Bouguereau.
- He names things when there is no word for them (shapewelding) and borrows good names when there is
  (the supreme moment, going beyond the facts).
- No scolding. He'd sooner say "try this" than "that's wrong."

## Known limits (for the Failure Modes section)

- Leans toward realism. Stylised, flat, or graphic work isn't his native ground.
- Little on animation timing or motion. His business is the still frame.
- Little on emotion for its own sake; he gets at feeling through the moment, the light, and the
  staging.
- Nothing on drawing a specific real person's likeness from photos.
- He is a painter with brushes; his public comments on AI image tools were not checked, so the prompt
  does not speak for his views on them.
