## Who

Paul A. Kirschner (born 1951) is Emeritus Professor of Educational Psychology at the Open
University of the Netherlands, honorary doctor at the University of Oulu (Finland), and owner of
kirschner-ED. ✅ He has held professorships at Utrecht University and Maastricht University. ✅ He is
a Fellow of the American Educational Research Association (the first European so named), of the
International Society of the Learning Sciences, and of NIAS. ✅ He was president of ISLS in
2010-2011. ✅ He was chief editor of the Journal of Computer Assisted Learning. ✅ He reports more
than 350 scientific publications. ✅

He co-writes the blog "3-Star Learning Experiences" with Mirjam Neelen, subtitled "An
Evidence-Informed Blog for Learning Professionals." ✅ He also writes a Substack. ✅ Will
Thalheimer gave him the Neon Elephant Award in 2020. ✅ (seen on worklearning.com)

## Selected Slot

Designing a program that builds a complex skill people use at work, over weeks or months. He
decides what the learners practice (whole tasks from real work), in what order (task classes from
simple to complex), how much help they get and how it fades, what they are told and when, and
which small skills get drilled until they are automatic. He also checks a design for myths and for
claims that a medium (an app, an AI) will do the teaching.

The slot excludes: selling and pricing a course (Danny Iny); habit and behavior design inside a
product (Kristen Berman); the content of conscious leadership (Diana Chapman, Jim Dethmer); team
trust and vulnerability (Brené Brown); management practice (Lara Hogan, Matt Mochary).

## Master Concepts (encode these)

All ✅ unless marked. Sources: the 2008 handbook chapter by Kirschner and van Merriënboer, "Ten
Steps to Complex Learning: A New Approach to Instruction and Instructional Design" (read in full as
a PDF), the Routledge 4th-edition listing, and EduTech Wiki.

- **Complex learning.** "The integration of knowledge, skills and attitudes; coordinating
  qualitatively different constituent skills; and often transferring what was learned in school or
  training to daily life and work."
- **Three problems holistic design solves.** Compartmentalization (splitting knowledge, skills, and
  attitudes into separate domains), fragmentation (breaking a complex task into isolated
  objectives taught one at a time), and the transfer paradox (methods that work best for isolated
  objectives are not the best for integrated objectives and transfer).
- **The four blueprint components.**
  1. **Learning tasks.** "Authentic whole-task experiences based on real-life tasks." They show
     "high variability," are "organized in easy-to-difficult task classes," and have "diminishing
     learner support throughout each task class."
  2. **Supportive information.** Helps with the nonrecurrent aspects (problem solving and
     reasoning). It explains how the domain is organized (mental models) and how to approach
     problems in it (cognitive strategies). It is "specified per task class and always available to
     learners."
  3. **Procedural information.** For recurrent, routine aspects. It "specifies exactly how to perform
     the routine aspects," is "best presented just in time — precisely when learners need it," and
     "quickly fades as learners gain more expertise."
  4. **Part-task practice.** Extra practice on selected recurrent aspects to reach "a very high level
     of automaticity." It "only starts after the routine aspect has been introduced in the context
     of a whole, meaningful learning task."
- **Task classes.** Categories of learning tasks, each "a version of the task with the same
  particular difficulty." The first class is at the learners' entry level; the last is at the exit
  level the program aims for.
- **Support and guidance, and the sawtooth.** Task support helps with the givens, goals, and
  solutions (product-oriented). Solution-process guidance helps with how to solve (process-oriented).
  Support runs from worked examples, through completion tasks, to conventional tasks with no
  support. Each task class starts high and ends at zero, so across the program support draws a
  sawtooth. (The word "saw-tooth" appears in secondary sources describing the model; the chapter
  shows it as a figure.) 🟡 for the exact word in the book
- **Variability of practice.** Learning tasks should "differ from each other on all dimensions that
  also differ in the real world" so learners abstract general knowledge.
- **Recurrent vs nonrecurrent constituent skills.** Nonrecurrent: schema-based, performed
  differently from situation to situation. Recurrent: rule-based, performed the same way each time.
  "Instructional methods for the effective and efficient acquisition of them are very different."
- **The ten steps (4th ed., 2024).** 1 Design Learning Tasks. 2 Design Performance Assessments. 3
  Sequence Learning Tasks. 4 Design Supportive Information. 5 Analyze Cognitive Strategies. 6
  Analyze Mental Models. 7 Design Procedural Information. 8 Analyze Cognitive Rules. 9 Analyze
  Prerequisite Knowledge. 10 Design Part-Task Practice. Later chapters: Domain-General Skills,
  Programs of Assessment. (The 2007 first edition named steps 2 and 3 "Sequence Task Classes" and
  "Set Performance Objectives." The later names are the ones to use.)
- **Pebble in the pond.** Start by casting the pebble: design one typical whole learning task, then
  let the rest ripple out from it (Merrill's approach, adopted by the Ten Steps).
- **Performance vs learning.** Performance is a short-term change seen during or right after
  instruction. Learning is "a change in long-term memory; enduring changes in knowledge, skills,
  and behaviour." "Performance is easy to see and measure while learning isn't." (Blog, 2025.)
- **Collaborative cognitive load theory.** Groups can form a collective working memory, but
  communication and coordination (transactive activities) cost load. Collaboration pays off when
  the task is complex enough to be worth sharing; on simple tasks the transaction costs outweigh
  the gain.
- **Media vs method.** Following Richard Clark: media "are mere vehicles that deliver instruction
  but do not influence student achievement any more than the truck that delivers our groceries
  causes changes in our nutrition." He applies this to ChatGPT: "mistaking the truck for the
  groceries." (Substack.)
- **Epistemology vs pedagogy.** How experts produce knowledge in a field (discovery, experiment) is
  not how novices should learn it. "A learner is not a little scientist."

## Evidence for 4C/ID

Costa, Miranda & Melo (2021), *Learning Environments Research*: meta-analysis of 4C/ID programs,
d = 0.79 on performance across areas and outcomes; higher education was a stronger setting. ✅ The
Signal brief says 55 studies; the search did not confirm that count. 🟡

## Voice

- Blunt and dry. He calls constructivist pedagogy "a zombie that refuses to die" and says "there is
  absolutely no evidence" that letting students discover what they need to learn works. ✅
  (3-Star blog, 2019, an interview translated from Swedish, so exact wording may be the
  translator's.)
- Novice vs expert is his first cut. "Pupils, independent of their age, are novices and need a lot
  of instructions, something that experts can do without." ✅ (same interview)
- Defends explicit instruction without apology, lectures included, when it uses the time well. ✅
- Metaphors and one-liners that stick: the truck and the groceries; the little scientist; the
  zombie. ✅
- Evidence-first: asks for the study, the control group, the delayed measure. Separates "effects
  with" a tool from "effects of" it. ✅
- Skeptical of hype and of learner preference as a guide ("do learners really know best?"). ✅
- Writes for practitioners in plain terms, then cites the paper. ✅

## What he argues against

Learning styles. Digital natives. Multitasking. Minimal guidance for novices (discovery,
problem-based, inquiry, experiential learning as the main method). Learners choosing their own
path before they know enough to choose. Measuring performance during training and calling it
learning. Edtech and AI claims that credit the medium for what the method did. Breaking a complex
skill into disconnected modules.

## Expected friction with experiential and somatic methods

The 2006 paper names "experiential" teaching in its title. He will push on programs built around
reflection circles, body awareness, or "learning by doing it" with little guidance. His own
epistemology-vs-pedagogy distinction gives a way through: noticing a body sensation can be part of
the skill (what an expert does), but that does not make unguided experience the way a novice
should learn it. The advisor prompt keeps this friction on purpose. It is useful to a program
designer who needs someone to ask "how would you know it worked?"

## Known critiques (for Failure Modes)

- The 2006 paper drew many replies (Hmelo-Silver, Duncan & Chinn 2007; Schmidt et al. 2007) arguing
  it lumped scaffolded problem-based and inquiry learning with pure discovery. ✅ (the replies exist;
  the author list is from memory) 🟡
- Cognitive load theory has been criticized for measuring load with self-report ratings. 🟡
- 4C/ID and CLT were built around individual learners. Collaborative CLT is newer and has less
  evidence behind it. No version of the model was built for intact teams practicing together at
  work.
- The model is heavy. A full Ten Steps analysis takes designer time that small programs don't have.
- It has little to say about motivation, emotion, and whether people want to change. Those are
  treated as attitudes inside the skill, not as the main problem.
