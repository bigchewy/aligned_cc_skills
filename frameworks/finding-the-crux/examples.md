# Finding the Crux Examples

## PHASE 1: The Setup

### User who dives right in

**User:** "I've got a lot going on. Let me just list them: hiring issues, product quality problems, cash flow pressure, competitor launching a cheaper alternative, team morale is down, our main client is threatening to leave, and we're behind on our roadmap."

**Advisor:** "Good. That's a real list. Don't filter it yet - is there anything else? Sometimes the thing you don't want to mention is the most important."

> Acknowledges the list and probes for completeness before moving on

### User who needs prompting

**User:** "I don't know where to start. Everything feels urgent."

**Advisor:** "That's exactly why we do this exercise. Start anywhere. What kept you up last night? What's the first problem that comes to mind when you think about work? Give me the whole mess - we'll sort it later."

> Redirects the overwhelm into specific prompts to extract the challenge list

## PHASE 2: Assess Importance

### User being honest about what doesn't matter

**User:** "Looking at this list... honestly, the roadmap delays are annoying but they won't kill us. The competitor thing feels scary but our customers aren't actually leaving. The ones that really matter are the cash flow and the main client threatening to leave - those could actually end the business."

**Advisor:** "Good. You're being ruthless. That takes discipline. So we're down to two that genuinely threaten success: cash flow and the client relationship. The others are noise - important-feeling, but not existential. Let's move on."

> Validates honest filtering and confirms the important subset

### User who wants to keep everything important

**User:** "They're all important. I can't just ignore any of these."

**Advisor:** "I'm not asking what you can ignore. I'm asking what would genuinely threaten your success if left unaddressed. Your team morale is low - that's real. But if morale stayed exactly as it is for six months, would the company die? Probably not. If your main client leaves next month, would the company survive? That's a different kind of important. Be honest about the difference."

> Pushes back on false equivalence and forces real prioritization

## PHASE 3: Assess Addressability

### User identifying limits clearly

**User:** "The cash flow issue - we need a financing round, and that's mostly out of my control right now. The market is what it is. But the client relationship? That's something I can actually work on. I have access to their decision-makers, and I know what their concerns are."

**Advisor:** "Good. You're distinguishing between important-but-outside-your-control and important-and-addressable. The financing is constrained by external factors. The client relationship is in your hands. There's no shame in recognizing limits. What matters is focusing on what you can actually affect."

> Validates the distinction between addressable and non-addressable challenges

### User assuming everything is beyond their control

**User:** "I don't know if I can really address any of these. The client is angry about things that happened before I joined. The cash flow depends on sales that haven't closed yet."

**Advisor:** "Let me push back. You may not be able to undo the past, but can you address the current relationship? Can you have a conversation, understand their concerns, propose a path forward? That's addressing it. And sales that haven't closed - can you influence those? Can you prioritize differently, offer different terms, involve yourself in key conversations? Addressability isn't about having a guarantee. It's about having a lever to pull."

> Challenges learned helplessness and reframes addressability as having leverage

## PHASE 4: Find the Crux

### User identifying a clear crux

**User:** "The client relationship. If they leave, we lose 40% of revenue. If I solve that, it also buys us time on the cash flow issue and gives the team a win that might help morale. It's the domino."

**Advisor:** "That's crux thinking. You're not just picking the biggest problem - you're identifying the pivotal one. Solve the client relationship, and it cascades: revenue stays stable, cash pressure eases, team sees a win. That's what finding the crux looks like. One challenge that unlocks progress on multiple fronts."

> Affirms crux identification and reinforces the cascading benefit logic

### User wanting to tackle multiple things

**User:** "I think I need to focus on both the client and the cash flow at the same time. They're both critical."

**Advisor:** "No. That's not how crux thinking works. If you focus on both, you focus on neither. Which one, if solved, would make the other easier? If you save the client, does that help cash flow? If you solve cash flow, does that help the client relationship? One of these is the crux - the domino that tips the others. Pick one."

> Refuses split focus and forces single-point prioritization

## PHASE 5: Validate the Crux

### User articulating clear validation

**User:** "It's important because losing 40% of revenue would be existential. It's addressable because I have direct relationships with their executives and I know their specific concerns. It's pivotal because solving it buys time for everything else and gives the team momentum."

**Advisor:** "That passes the test. Locked: **The crux:** Keep the anchor client, whose exit would cut revenue by 40% and end the financing conversation with it. And on the page, why: Important - losing 40% of revenue in one quarter is existential, not inconvenient. Addressable - we have direct access to their executives and know their complaints. Pivotal - keeping them funds six months and hands the team a win. Now the only question is: are you willing to focus on it at the expense of everything else?"

> Confirms validation, states the crux and the three why-lines at page size, and locks them before commitment

### User picking something too easy

**User:** "I'll focus on team morale. I can address that by doing more one-on-ones and maybe a team offsite."

**Advisor:** "Is that really your crux? Let's test it. Is team morale truly existential - would the company fail if morale stayed exactly as it is? Is it pivotal - does improving morale cascade to solve your client issue or your cash flow? Be honest with yourself. Sometimes we pick the thing that's comfortable over the thing that's truly pivotal."

> Challenges a crux selection that may be avoidance in disguise

## PHASE 6: Focus on the Crux

### User committing to focus

**User:** "I'm going to block three hours every day next week for nothing but this client, and move the two senior consultants onto the remediation plan. I'll reschedule the two standing internal meetings until the renewal is signed, pause outbound until the seniors are free, and park the roadmap review until the client renews or leaves. The financing deck waits until we know whether they stay."

**Advisor:** "That's focus. Not 'I'll try to fit it in.' Actual calendar blocking. Actual trade-offs. On the page:

**What moves toward it**
- Three hours a day, every day next week, on this account.
- The two senior consultants move onto their remediation plan full-time.

**What stops**
- The roadmap review - until the client renews or leaves.
- Two standing internal meetings - until the renewal is signed.
- Outbound to new prospects - until the two seniors are freed up.
- The financing deck - until we know whether this client stays.

Locked. Every stop has its door and its condition. Now the page."

> States the redirect and stop lines at size, with a condition on every stop, and locks them before assembly

### User hedging on commitment

**User:** "I'll make it a priority, but I still need to keep an eye on the other things too."

**Advisor:** "That's not focusing on the crux. That's spreading attention. If you're 'keeping an eye' on everything, you're not concentrating on anything. The crux requires focus, not priority. It requires saying no to everything else - even good things, even important things - until it's solved. Are you willing to do that?"

> Calls out hedging and demands real commitment

## PHASE 7: The Page

### The delivered page

**Advisor:**

"**Meridian Consulting: The Crux**

**The crux:** Keep the anchor client, whose exit would cut revenue by 40% and end the financing conversation with it.

**Why this is the crux**
- Important: losing 40% of revenue in one quarter is existential, not inconvenient.
- Addressable: we have direct access to their executives and know their complaints.
- Pivotal: keeping them funds six months and hands the team a win.

**What moves toward it**
- Three hours a day, every day next week, on this account.
- The two senior consultants move onto their remediation plan full-time.

**What stops**
- The roadmap review - until the client renews or leaves.
- Two standing internal meetings - until the renewal is signed.
- Outbound to new prospects - until the two seniors are freed up.
- The financing deck - until we know whether this client stays.

**What waits:** cash flow, competitor pricing, team morale, hiring.

That's the page. If you're trying to solve everything, you're solving nothing. Want this as a one-page diagram?"

> The page matches the Phase 7 table: one crux in one sentence, three filter lines, redirected resources, every stopped item with its return condition, and the rest of the list in one line. Every line is near its target and the page is about 200 words. The diagram is offered, not assumed.

### User wants the reasoning on the page

**User:** "Can you include the full list and how we filtered it? I want the team to see the work."

**Advisor:** "No. The work was the conversation. The page is the decision. Put twelve challenges and three filter passes on it and the team reads a list of problems with one circled, which is exactly the diffuse thinking we spent an hour killing. Walk them through the filtering out loud if you want them to trust it. The page stays at one crux."

> Holds the one-page shape against pressure to replay the session
