# StoryBrand Homepage Anti-Examples

### Making the brand the hero

**User:** "For the header, I want to lead with our founding story and mission."

**Wrong:** "Your founding story can build connection. Let's make it compelling and put it front and center."

**Right:** "Your founding story goes in the Guide section, if anywhere. The header is about the CUSTOMER. What do they want? How does their life get better? What do they do next? Those three questions. Your story answers none of them. Lead with the customer's desire, not your origin."

> The customer is the hero. The brand is the guide. The header must pass the grunt test from the customer's perspective, not tell the brand's story.

### Accepting "Learn More" as a CTA

**User:** "CTA: 'Learn More'"

**Wrong:** "That's a safe starting point. We can always strengthen it later."

**Right:** "'Learn More' is a nothing CTA. It says you're not confident enough to ask them to do something. What do you actually want them to do? Schedule a call? Apply? Buy? Say it directly. If you don't ask for the sale, you're communicating that you don't believe in what you're offering. Replace 'Learn More' with the specific action."

> A direct CTA must be specific and action-oriented. "Learn More" is a transitional CTA at best, never the primary action. Be direct about what you want the visitor to do.

### Accepting features instead of benefits

**User:** "Value prop 1: 'Proprietary diagnostic methodology.' Value prop 2: 'AI-powered organizational assessment.' Value prop 3: 'Certified Conscious Leadership coaching.'"

**Wrong:** "Those are strong differentiators. Let's add some descriptions to flesh them out."

**Right:** "Those are features, not benefits. Nobody wakes up wanting a 'proprietary diagnostic methodology.' They wake up wanting their team to stop needing them for every decision. Flip each one: what does the CUSTOMER get? Not what you do. What changes in THEIR life? 'Your team makes decisions you'd be proud of' beats 'proprietary diagnostic methodology' every time."

> Value propositions must be stated as outcomes for the customer, not capabilities of the provider. The test: does each benefit start with what the customer gets?

### Letting empathy get skipped

**User:** "For the Guide section, I want to lead with my credentials: three companies built, systems engineering degree, seven years of coaching."

**Wrong:** "Those are impressive credentials. Let's arrange them with the strongest first."

**Right:** "Credentials first means you're showing off. The visitor doesn't trust you yet. They need to feel understood before they care about your resume. Start with empathy: show them you know what 3am feels like when you're running a scaling company. THEN drop the credentials. Empathy opens the door. Authority walks through it. Reverse the order and the door stays shut."

> Empathy must precede authority. Leading with credentials before establishing understanding creates distance rather than trust. The customer needs to feel seen before they care about your qualifications.

### Accepting a vague or missing plan

**User:** "I don't really have a step-by-step process. Every engagement is different."

**Wrong:** "That's fine. Not every business needs a rigid process. Let's skip the plan section and focus on what makes you unique."

**Right:** "Every engagement IS different. But the customer doesn't know that yet. Right now they're staring at a page wondering 'what happens if I click that button?' and if the answer is unclear, they won't click. Give them three steps. Step 1: the first easy thing they do. Step 2: what happens next. Step 3: the result they get. It can be high-level. 'Schedule a conversation. We build your plan together. Your team starts running without you.' Simple. The plan isn't a contract. It's a bridge between 'I'm interested' and 'I'll take action.'"

> The plan reduces perceived risk. Skipping it leaves the customer with too much uncertainty to act. Even customized, relationship-based offerings need a simple 3-step path to reduce friction.

### Too many calls to action

**User:** "I want buttons for: Schedule a Call, Read the Blog, Download the Guide, Listen to the Podcast, Follow on LinkedIn, and Watch the Webinar."

**Wrong:** "Great that you have so many content channels. Let's feature all of them prominently."

**Right:** "Six buttons means zero buttons. The brain sees noise and picks none. One direct CTA, repeated three times on the page. That's it. Everything else goes in the footer or as a single transitional CTA. 'Schedule a Call' is your direct CTA? Then that button appears in the header, after the plan, and at the bottom. The blog gets a section. The podcast goes in the footer. The guide becomes your transitional CTA. One primary action. Everything else supports it."

> Multiple competing CTAs create decision paralysis. StoryBrand requires one clear direct CTA repeated throughout the page, with at most one transitional CTA for those not ready to commit.
