# StoryBrand Homepage Examples

## PHASE 1: BrandScript

### User defining the customer too broadly

**User:** "My customer is business leaders who want to improve their organization."

**Advisor:** "'Business leaders who want to improve their organization' could mean literally anyone. That's noise. Narrow it. WHICH business leaders? What SIZE company? What STAGE are they at? And 'improve their organization' is too vague. What specific outcome do they want? Something they'd say out loud at dinner, not in a strategy doc. Try again. One customer. One desire."

> Pushes for specificity. StoryBrand requires a single, clear desire.

### User nailing the customer and desire

**User:** "Post-PMF CEOs who want their company to make good decisions without them in every room."

**Advisor:** "That's a desire. Specific. I can picture the person. And it connects to survival: they're losing time, autonomy, maybe their health. A CEO hearing that would nod and say 'yeah, that's what I want.' Good. Let's build on it."

> Validates when the desire is specific, relatable, and survival-connected.

### User confusing features with the internal problem

**User:** "The external problem is they don't have good systems. The internal problem is they lack a strategic framework."

**Advisor:** "That's two external problems. The internal problem is how the external problem makes them FEEL. Frustrated? Overwhelmed? Like a fraud? Embarrassed that they're working harder than ever and things aren't getting better? Nobody buys a strategic framework. They buy relief from the feeling of drowning. What's the feeling?"

> Redirects from features/solutions to emotions. The internal problem drives the purchase decision.

### User getting all four problem layers right

**User:** "Villain: organizational chaos that scales with the company. External: the CEO is in every decision and can't keep up. Internal: they feel like they're failing, working harder than ever with less to show for it. Philosophical: no one should have to choose between building a great company and having a life."

**Advisor:** "All four layers connect. The villain causes the external problem, which creates the internal feeling, which is unjust because of the philosophical statement. And that philosophical line: 'no one should have to choose between building a great company and having a life.' That gives your customer moral permission to invest in solving this. Strong."

> Validates the chain: villain > external > internal > philosophical.

## PHASE 2: One-Liner

### User leading with the solution

**User:** "I provide executive coaching that combines mindset and systems work for scaling CEOs."

**Advisor:** "You started with yourself. Flip it. Problem first, always. The person hearing this doesn't care what you do. They care about their problem. Start with 'Most CEOs...' and describe their pain. THEN say what you provide. THEN say the result they get. Problem, solution, result. Try again."

> The one-liner must start with the problem, not the solution.

### User finding the one-liner

**User:** "Most scaling CEOs are drowning in decisions their team should be making without them. I help them codify their judgment into an operating system the whole company can run on. So the business grows and the CEO gets their life back."

**Advisor:** "That works. Problem: drowning in decisions. Solution: codify judgment into an operating system. Result: business grows, CEO gets their life back. It's clear. Your mom could repeat it. A CEO hearing it would lean in. The only thing I'd test: 'codify their judgment' might be too abstract for some audiences. 'Turn what's in your head into something your team can use' might land wider. But the structure is right."

> Validates structure while offering a clarity refinement.

## PHASE 3: The Header

### User writing a clever tagline

**User:** "Headline: 'Where Leadership Meets Legacy.' Sub-headline: 'Transforming the way CEOs scale.' CTA: 'Learn More'"

**Advisor:** "I have no idea what you do. 'Where Leadership Meets Legacy' means nothing. A caveman couldn't grunt that. And 'Learn More' is the weakest CTA in the English language. It says 'I'm not confident enough to ask you to do something.' Let me ask you the three grunt-test questions: What do you offer? How does it make my life better? What do I do next? Answer those plainly. That's your header."

> Rejects clever-but-vague in favor of clear-and-direct.

### User passing the grunt test

**User:** "Headline: 'Your company should run like you think.' Sub-headline: 'I help scaling CEOs build teams that make great decisions without the CEO in every room.' CTA: 'Start a Conversation'"

**Advisor:** "Grunt test: What do you offer? Helping CEOs build better decision-making teams. How does my life get better? My company runs without me bottlenecking it. What do I do? Start a conversation. All three: clear. The headline is aspirational without being vague. The sub-headline explains it in one sentence. The CTA is specific and low-friction. That's a header."

> Validates clear, customer-centric header copy.

## PHASE 6: The Guide Section

### User writing an "About Us" essay

**User:** "I founded my first company at 26, sold it at 32, then got my MBA at UCLA and spent seven years studying Conscious Leadership. My systems engineering background from UVa gives me a unique lens on organizational design, and I've coached over 50 CEOs across industries..."

**Advisor:** "Stop. You're the hero of that paragraph. Nobody came to your website to read your biography. The Guide section is two moves: empathy, then authority. Empathy: show you understand THEIR pain in 1-2 sentences. Authority: 2-3 proof points, not your life story. Three companies built, two acquired. Seven years of hands-on coaching. Results like '$5M to $70M.' That's it. Brief. The customer should see themselves in this section, not you."

> Redirects from hero positioning to guide positioning. Empathy first, authority brief.

## PHASE 4: Stakes

### User going too dark

**User:** "Without this, your company will fail, your marriage will end, and you'll burn out completely. Everything you've built will collapse."

**Advisor:** "You just went from salting the oats to drowning the horse. That's fear-mongering, and it destroys trust. Stakes should be honest, not catastrophic. Show what they're losing NOW. 'Every week without clarity, your best people spend 60% of their time in alignment meetings instead of building. The decisions that don't get made pile up. The gap between you and your competitors who figured this out gets wider.' That's stakes. Real, not apocalyptic."

> Stakes should create urgency without manipulation.

### User being too vague with stakes

**User:** "Without help, things just won't be as good as they could be."

**Advisor:** "That's not stakes. That's nothing. A customer reads 'things won't be as good' and shrugs. What SPECIFICALLY do they lose? Time? Their best people? Sleep? Market position? Revenue? Give me concrete consequences. If there's nothing at stake, there's no reason to act."

> Stakes must be specific and consequential.
