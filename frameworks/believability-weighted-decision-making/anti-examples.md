# Believability-Weighted Decision Making Anti-Examples

### Conflating status with believability

**User:** "My investor is really successful. I should probably just do what he says."

**Wrong:** "Successful investors often have valuable perspective. It makes sense to give his opinion significant weight."

**Right:** "Successful at what? Investing? Operating companies? Making the specific type of decision you're facing? Believability is topic-specific. Your investor might be brilliant at spotting good companies, but has he ever run one? Has he made decisions like yours from the inside? Being generally successful doesn't make someone believable on your specific question."

> Status and general success don't equal believability. Always ask: believable about WHAT?

### Treating all opinions as equally valid

**User:** "I asked five people and got five different answers. I guess I just need to make a call."

**Wrong:** "When experts disagree, ultimately you have to trust your own judgment. Go with your gut."

**Right:** "Wait - five opinions doesn't mean five equal opinions. Who among those five has actually done the thing you're deciding about? Who has a track record of good outcomes in similar situations? Who can explain their logic clearly? Don't count votes equally. Weight them by believability. The answer isn't 'just make a call' - it's 'figure out whose call should count more.'"

> Equal weighting ignores the fundamental insight of the framework. Not all opinions are equal.

### Skipping the reasoning to count votes

**User:** "Three out of five people said I should do it. Majority rules, right?"

**Wrong:** "Three out of five is a clear majority. That's a strong signal you should move forward."

**Right:** "You just told me the conclusions, not the reasoning. Why do those three say yes? Why do the two say no? What are they each weighing? Understanding the WHY behind the opinions matters more than counting them. And are those three more believable than the two, or less? A believability-weighted minority might be more informative than an equal-weighted majority."

> Understanding reasoning matters more than counting votes. And believability weighting can flip the apparent consensus.
