## Is this his own method?

Yes. "Power requires a benefit and a barrier" and "So benefit and a barrier, we call it the to be or not to be test." ✅ [LENNY-T]. The research file lists `benefit-and-barrier-test` as the first framework to build. The same test is in the book's Introduction 🧠 (not read).

## Phases

- Phase 1, The Claimed Advantages: Added: a framework needs the person's own claims on the table before testing them. The routing to sibling frameworks follows his spoken advice: branding and process power "only are really available in when you reach a stability phase" and "almost every startup that you want to deal with starts with counter positioning" ✅ [LENNY-T].
- Phase 2, The Benefit: "you have to have something that you do that gives you a better outcome than your competitors, lower cost or higher price" and "there's something in what you do that gives you either a cost or price advantage over others" ✅ [LENNY-T].
- Phase 3, The Barrier: "something that makes it impossible for somebody else to mimic that" and "the competitors can't take away from you" ✅; the to be or not to be test ✅; moat is only the barrier, "you can have a moat around a very undesirable piece of property" ✅. Per-type barrier questions:
  - Operational excellence: "The things that drive operational excellence can be mimicked." and the Blockbuster UI story ✅; Porter, "operational excellence is not strategy" ✅ [LENNY-T].
  - Brand: "you can get brand recognition by buying an ad in the Super Bowl. That's not power, that you paid for it." ✅ The price-premium question is Added: it applies the benefit test to brand. The book's brand benefits ("uncertainty reduction", "affective valence") 🧠 are not used.
  - Process: "process power is really operational excellence on steroids and usually is imitable" ✅. The one-good-consultant question is adapted from Lenny's heuristic, which Helmer agreed with ✅; the words are Lenny's, so they are not quoted as Helmer's.
  - Data: "The curve flattens in other words" ✅.
  - Cornered resource: "if you transferred it to somebody else it would be of value to them, but you own the rights. So the barrier is law for example" ✅. The end-date question is Added: a license that expires is a time-limited barrier.
  - Scale: Netflix spreading its content fixed cost over more subscribers ✅.
  - Switching costs: named as a power in his spoken power progression ✅; the specific questions (data, retraining, integrations, a competitor's discount) are Added, from the book's idea of switching costs 🧠.
  - Network effects: see Phase 4.
- Phase 4, Is It Material?: "The key thing here is materiality, not whether the flywheel exists, but whether the effect is strong enough to really tilt returns." / "it's not an impact, it's a material impact" / "If it's a penny to your bottom line, that's one thing if it's a billion dollars or something else." / "network effects but not network economies" ✅ [LENNY-T]. The bound-it fallback (small, medium or large) is Added, for users with no number.
- Phase 5, Power or Treadmill: "castle and not a shack" ✅; "You're on a treadmill and if you stop running on that treadmill, you get creamed, but it's not power." ✅; in takeoff "operational excellence is everything" ✅. The three-way verdict (Power / treadmill / not yet) is Added: it names the honest outcome the research file requires ("The persona must not invent a moat to be helpful."). "founders have to be optimistic" ✅; "I don't want to be unkind" ✅; "tilting probabilities" ✅.
- Phase 6, The Table: Added: the one-page table and one next-step line are the deliverable; his spoken "action is the first principle of business" ✅ supports ending on one action.
- The Key Rule that a short conversation can rule things out but not prove Power: "Actually understanding whether or not there is a type of power in place is hard." and "it might take us weeks to answer that question for a single company." ✅ [LENNY-T].
- Hand-offs: `power-progression-check` (origination, takeoff, stability ✅) and `counter-positioning-test` (counter-positioning as the start-up's "refuge" ✅). The idea that an incumbent won't copy because of damage to its existing business is the book's "collateral damage" 🧠, taught as a concept only in the hand-off line, never quoted.
