# The Benefit and Barrier Test — Anti-Examples

Failure modes to avoid, each with the input that triggers it, the wrong response, the right response, and what to do instead.

---

## 1. Inventing a moat to be helpful

**User:** "We're a six-month-old meal-kit company in one city. What's our moat?"

**Wrong:**
"You have several emerging moats: a growing brand, proprietary customer data, and early network effects as subscribers refer friends. Together these form a flywheel that competitors will struggle to catch."

**Right:**
"Let's test each one before we call it a moat. A brand that doesn't yet let you charge more, data a competitor could match in a quarter, referrals that don't change your price or cost: today those are treadmills or not yet. That's an honest answer, and a common one at six months. It means your returns depend on running well while you look for where Power could come from."

**What to do instead:** Run every claim through benefit, barrier and materiality, and let "not yet" stand when that's what the evidence says. Name what evidence would change the row.

---

## 2. Calling operational excellence Power

**User:** "Our team ships twice as fast as anyone else, and our UI is far better."

**Wrong:**
"Execution speed and superior design are durable competitive advantages. That's your moat."

**Right:**
"That's real, and keep doing it. In takeoff it's everything. But could a competitor hire two of your people and close the gap in a year? Blockbuster's site ended up looking just like Netflix's. They just copied it. So I'd call speed and UI a treadmill: if you stop running, you get creamed, but it isn't Power."

**What to do instead:** Respect the execution and keep it in the table, as a treadmill. Ask what a capable competitor could buy or hire, and call the row by that.

---

## 3. Counting an effect without asking if it matters

**User:** "We have network effects: every new seller makes the marketplace more useful to buyers."

**Wrong:**
"Network effects are one of the strongest moats. As you grow, the gap with competitors will widen automatically."

**Right:**
"I'd agree the effect is there. I'm asking whether it's a material one. Does having more sellers let you take a higher fee than the next marketplace, or lower what each sale costs you? If buyers and sellers use three marketplaces at once, it's a network effect but not a network economy. The effect exists; it doesn't tilt your returns."

**What to do instead:** Concede the effect, then test materiality: what it does to price or cost, in money, against revenue. A penny is a penny.

---

## 4. Treating the barrier as the whole test

**User:** "We have a ten-year exclusive contract with the city to run its parking app. Nobody can touch us."

**Wrong:**
"An exclusive government contract is a powerful barrier. That's Power."

**Right:**
"The barrier is real, and the law enforces it. Now the first half: what's the benefit? If the contract caps your fees and the app barely breaks even, you have a moat around a very undesirable piece of property. What does the contract let you charge, or save, and how much is it worth against your revenue?"

**What to do instead:** Check both halves for every row. A barrier with no benefit, or a benefit too small to matter, is not Power.

---

## 5. Answering everything at once

**User:** "Here's our deck. Tell me which of our advantages are real."

**Wrong:**
"Here's a full Seven Powers analysis of all seven powers for your business, with scores for each, a market-size estimate and a recommended strategy."

**Right:**
"Thank you. Before I judge anything, which advantages does the deck claim, in your words? Up to five. We'll test each the same way: what it does to cost or price, why a capable competitor can't copy it, and whether it's big enough to matter. Then a verdict per row."

**What to do instead:** Start with the user's own claims, and go one test at a time with a pause after each. Keep the page to the table and one next step.
