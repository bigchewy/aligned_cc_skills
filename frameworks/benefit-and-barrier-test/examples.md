# The Benefit and Barrier Test — Examples

The businesses below are made up to show the moves: a regional cold-chain trucking firm, a developer API company, a direct-to-consumer mattress brand, a freelance design marketplace, a specialty coffee roaster, and a medical-device maker. The coaching lines are written for this framework. Lines given in quotation marks as Hamilton Helmer's own words are listed in `sources.md` with their source.

---

## Phase 1: The Claimed Advantages

### Golden path

**User:** "We run refrigerated trucking for grocery chains in three states, about $60M revenue, 6% operating margin. We compete with two national carriers and a lot of small owner-operators. Our advantages: route density in our region, a 98% on-time record, our dispatch software, and long relationships with the buyers."

**Say something like:**
"Thank you, that's a clear list. So we're testing four: route density, the on-time record, the dispatch software, and the buyer relationships. Each one has to pass the same tests. Let's start with what each does to your cost or your price."

**Why it works:** The rows are read back in the user's own words, numbered, before any judgment. The revenue and margin are on the table, so materiality can be checked later against something real.

### Struggle: a slogan instead of a list

**User:** "Honestly, our moat is just that we're better. Customers love us."

**Say something like:**
"I believe you, and that's a good place to start. Let's break 'better' into the parts you'd point to. When a customer picks you over the alternative, what are the two or three reasons they give? Each of those becomes a row."

**User:** "Faster onboarding, our support team, and we integrate with their billing system."

**Why it works:** The claim isn't argued with; it's split into testable rows. "Customers love us" becomes three things that can each be checked for a benefit and a barrier.

### Edge case: a business-model edge

**User:** "We're a mattress brand that sells only online, no stores, so we price 40% below the big chains. They can't match it."

**Say something like:**
"That one is a different question. The chains could open a website tomorrow; the real question is why they won't, given what it would do to their stores. We'll keep it as a row and mark it for `counter-positioning-test`, which is built for exactly that. What else do you lean on besides the model?"

**Why it works:** The model-based edge is routed to the sibling framework instead of being forced through a copy test that misreads it. The session keeps going with the other claims.

## Phase 2: The Benefit

### Golden path

**User:** "Route density: our trucks run 30% fewer empty miles than the national carriers in our region, so our cost per load is about 12% lower."

**Say something like:**
"Good, that's a benefit in the plain sense: a lower cost than the competitor you named, with a number on it. Row one: lower cost per load, about 12%."

**Why it works:** The answer is already in cost terms, so the advisor states it back in one line and moves on.

### Struggle: delight instead of numbers

**User:** "For the API company, our benefit is developer experience. Our docs are the best in the category."

**Say something like:**
"That may well be true, and it matters. Where does it show up in the numbers? Do you charge more than the competitor for it, or does it lower what it costs you to win a customer, say, fewer sales calls?"

**User:** "We don't charge more. But we spend almost nothing on sales; developers sign themselves up."

**Say something like:**
"Then that's the benefit: a lower cost to acquire a customer. We'll test next whether a competitor could write docs as good as yours."

**Why it works:** "Best docs" is translated into the only two forms a benefit takes, cost or price. The user finds the real benefit themselves.

### Edge case: no benefit at all

**User:** "Our brand is well known in the coffee scene, but we price the same as everyone else and our costs are the same."

**Say something like:**
"Then this row stops here, and that's useful to know. A brand that doesn't let you charge more or spend less isn't doing the work of Power yet. It goes in the table as 'not yet'."

**Why it works:** The row ends honestly without a lecture. The person learns the brand isn't yet earning anything measurable, which is the point.

## Phase 3: The Barrier

### Golden path

**User:** "Route density: a national carrier could add trucks in our region, but they'd run them half empty for years until they won our customers' loads. They'd lose money every month doing it."

**Say something like:**
"That's a real barrier. The copier's cost per load stays higher until they have your volume, and they can't get your volume without first running at a loss. That is scale working for you. Row one has a benefit and a barrier."

**Why it works:** The barrier is something a capable, funded competitor can't simply buy: the volume itself. The advisor names why, so the user can repeat the reasoning to an investor.

### Struggle: effort mistaken for a barrier

**User:** "Our dispatch software took us four years to build. Nobody could copy that easily."

**Say something like:**
"Four years is real work, and it shows. But picture a competitor who hires two of your engineers and licenses a good routing package. How long would they need? Blockbuster's site ended up looking just like Netflix's. In his words: 'They just copied it.' The question isn't how long it took you; it's what stops them."

**User:** "Probably eighteen months. Maybe less."

**Say something like:**
"Then it's operational excellence. Valuable, keep doing it, but it isn't the barrier."

**Why it works:** Effort is separated from what can't be bought. The Blockbuster story makes the point concrete, and the user does the estimate.

### Edge case: a cornered resource with an end date

**User:** "The medical-device business holds an exclusive license on the sensor patent. It runs four more years."

**Say something like:**
"That's a real barrier, and the law enforces it. Two questions. If you handed that license to a competitor, would it be worth as much to them? And what happens in year five? If the answer to the first is yes, it's a barrier for four years. Let's write the end date into the row."

**Why it works:** A legal barrier is taken seriously, and its time limit becomes part of the row instead of being hidden.

## Phase 4: Is It Material?

### Golden path

**User:** "Route density saves us about $4M a year on $60M revenue. Our margin is 6%, so that's most of it."

**Say something like:**
"That's real money. It's more than the difference between a good year and a bad one for you. Material: yes."

**Why it works:** The figure is compared with revenue and margin, and the verdict on materiality follows from the user's own numbers.

### Struggle: a real effect that's a penny

**User:** "Our freelance design marketplace has network effects. More designers attract more clients."

**Say something like:**
"I'd agree the effect is there. Here's the finer line. In his words, there are 'lots of things that I would say have network effects but not network economies.' Does having more designers let you take a bigger fee than the next marketplace, or lower what each sale costs you?"

**User:** "Not really. Clients use three marketplaces at once and our fee is the same as theirs."

**Say something like:**
"Then it's a network effect, but not a network economy. The effect exists; it doesn't tilt your returns. Material: no, for now."

**Why it works:** The advisor concedes the effect before drawing the distinction, and multi-homing, the user's own fact, decides it.

### Edge case: no number

**User:** "I have no idea what the buyer relationships are worth."

**Say something like:**
"Let's bound it instead. If your two best buyer relationships walked out tomorrow, what would change next year in your price or your cost? Small, medium or large?"

**User:** "Small. They'd keep us for the on-time record."

**Why it works:** A rough bound is enough to rule a row in or out. And the answer points back to which row really carries the business.

## Phase 5: Power or Treadmill

### Golden path

**Say something like:**
"Here's how I'd call it. Tell me where I'm wrong.

| Claimed advantage | Benefit | Barrier | Material? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Route density | ~12% lower cost per load | A copier runs half-empty trucks at a loss for years | Yes, ~$4M on $60M | Power |
| On-time record | Wins contracts, no price premium | Better dispatch and drivers, both copyable | Some | Treadmill |
| Dispatch software | Fewer empty miles | ~18 months for a funded copier | Folded into density | Treadmill |
| Buyer relationships | None we can name | People move | Small | Not yet |"

**User:** "That's fair. I'd have put the software higher, but I see it."

**Why it works:** Every row is at table size before the final page. The verdicts use the framework's three words and the user is asked to correct them.

### Struggle: pushback with conviction only

**User:** "No, our brand is definitely a moat. Everyone in specialty coffee knows us."

**Say something like:**
"Founders have to be optimistic, and I'd rather you were. But I can't see the barrier yet, and I don't want to be unkind by pretending I do. Here's what would show me one: a price you hold above the roasters next to you on the shelf, for years, without losing volume. If you have that number, we'll change the row."

**Why it works:** The row stays put, the pushback is met with courtesy, and the user gets the exact evidence that would change the verdict.

### Edge case: no Power anywhere

**User:** "So all five rows are treadmill or not yet?"

**Say something like:**
"Today, yes, and that's common at your stage. It doesn't mean the business is bad. It means your returns depend on running well. You're tilting probabilities now, not proving anything. The work is finding where Power could come from next."

**Why it works:** The honest answer is said plainly and kindly, with no moat invented to soften it.

## Phase 6: The Table

### Golden path

**Say something like:**
"Here's where you stand. One row is Power: route density. Two are treadmills, and one isn't there yet.

The one thing to do next: add density where you already lead, before a national carrier decides to pay for it in your region."

**Why it works:** One next step, tied to the strongest row. The table is unchanged from Phase 5.

### Edge case: early stage, brand and process claimed

**Say something like:**
"Two of your rows were brand and process, and you're eighteen months in. Those usually come late, at the stability phase. Run `power-progression-check` next. It'll show which powers are within reach for you now."

**Why it works:** The hand-off names the sibling framework and why, instead of stretching this test to answer a question it isn't built for.
