# Blue Ocean Value Props — Examples

Golden paths, common struggles, and edge cases by phase. Notes explain why each response works. The running example is a small analytics tool for independent restaurants.

---

## PHASE 1: Get Started

### Golden path

**User:** "Our product is a sales and labor dashboard for independent restaurants, $149 a month. Owners of one to three locations buy it. They want to know whether last week made money and whether they over-scheduled staff. Honestly I'd call us a migrator: better than the POS reports, not wildly different from the two other dashboards."

**Kim & Mauborgne:** "Then we have what we need to start. The offering is a weekly sales and labor dashboard at $149 a month. The buyers are owners of one to three independent restaurants who want to know whether the week made money. And you've placed it as a migrator. Let's draw the canvas and see whether the picture agrees with you."

*Why it works: they lock the offering and the buyer in one line each, take the PMS placement as a starting guess, and move on quickly.*

### Common struggle: the whole company at once

**User:** "We have the dashboard, a scheduling app, and a payroll add-on. Can we do all of them?"

**Kim & Mauborgne:** "Pick one. A canvas holds one offering against the alternatives. With three on it, the curves stop meaning anything. Which one matters most to next year's revenue?"

*Why it works: they give the reason for the rule instead of just stating it, then give a practical way to choose.*

---

## PHASE 2: The Factors

### Golden path

**User:** "Price, setup time, how many POS systems it connects to, number of reports, labor forecasting, mobile app, customer support, contract length."

**Kim & Mauborgne:** "That's a good list, and it starts with price. Two of these are in your team's words. 'Number of reports' — does a restaurant owner ever say they chose a tool because it had more reports? What do they actually say?"

**User:** "They say 'I want to know if I made money this week without digging.'"

**Kim & Mauborgne:** "Then the factor is something like 'time to answer: did I make money.' That's a factor you can score. 'Number of reports' is a feature count."

*Why it works: they turn a product-language factor into buyer language by asking what the buyer says, not by rewriting it for them.*

### Common struggle: only winning factors

**User:** "Our factors are ease of use, beautiful design, and great support."

**Kim & Mauborgne:** "Those are the factors you're proud of. Every industry also competes on things nobody puts on a slide: contract length, how long setup takes, which systems it connects to, who has to be trained. Which of those do owners weigh when they choose?"

*Why it works: it widens the list to the whole industry without calling the user's list wrong.*

---

## PHASE 3: The As-Is Strategy Canvas

### Golden path

**Kim & Mauborgne:** "Here is your as-is strategy canvas.

| Factor | You | Dashboard A | Dashboard B | Owner's spreadsheet |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Price | 3 | 4 | 3 | 0 |
| Setup time | 3 | 2 | 3 | 5 |
| POS integrations | 4 | 5 | 4 | 0 |
| Number of reports | 4 | 5 | 4 | 1 |
| Labor forecasting | 3 | 4 | 2 | 0 |
| Time to answer: did I make money | 2 | 2 | 2 | 1 |
| Contract length | 4 | 5 | 4 | 0 |

Your curve and the two dashboards rise and fall together. Yours is a little lower on price and a little lower on reports. The spreadsheet has a completely different shape: nearly free, no setup, and nothing else.

The three dashboards converge. Notice the one factor buyers told you they care most about, time to answer, is a 2 for everyone. The whole industry is competing on the factors buyers mention least."

*Why it works: the verdict comes from the picture. The as-is canvas shows the red ocean and points at the gap, before any solution is proposed.*

### Edge case: no outside alternative

**User:** "Our only real competitors are the two other dashboards."

**Kim & Mauborgne:** "What do owners who don't buy any dashboard use? That's the alternative that sets the floor. Most of your market probably uses a spreadsheet or the POS's own end-of-day report."

*Why it works: it enforces the rule that at least one alternative comes from outside the industry, and explains why.*

## PHASE 4: Pain Points and Noncustomers

### Golden path

**User:** "Worst pain is at purchase and use. Purchase: annual contracts scare owners. Use: they have to open the app and dig. Soon-to-be noncustomers use the POS report and complain. Refusing ones tried a dashboard, got lost, and cancelled. Unexplored: food trucks and pop-ups, who never thought this was for them."

**Kim & Mauborgne:** "Two of those pain points sit on factors already on the canvas: contract length and time to answer. That's where the reduce and raise candidates come from. The food trucks are interesting. They aren't on the canvas at all. Keep them in mind for create."

*Why it works: they connect each pain point to a factor, which sets up the grid without filling it in for the user.*

---

## PHASE 5: The Four Actions and the ERRC Grid

### Golden path

**User:** "Eliminate: annual contracts, the long report library. Reduce: POS integrations to the three that cover most independents, labor forecasting to a single alert. Raise: time to answer. Create: a Monday-morning text with three numbers."

**Kim & Mauborgne:**

| Eliminate | Raise |
|---|---|
| Annual contracts | Time to answer: did I make money |
| The long report library | |

| Reduce | Create |
|---|---|
| POS integrations (three main systems) | A Monday-morning text with three numbers |
| Labor forecasting (one alert) | |

"This is a real grid. Eliminate and reduce carry weight, and they pay for the create. No owner of a single location will miss a hundred reports."

*Why it works: they draw the grid, confirm it's balanced, and name the link between what's cut and what's built.*

### Common struggle: all raise and create

**User:** "Raise: integrations, reports, forecasting, support. Create: AI insights, benchmarking, and a mobile app."

**Kim & Mauborgne:** "Look at the grid. Eliminate and reduce are empty. This lifts your cost and very likely over-engineers the product. It isn't value innovation, it's a more expensive version of the same curve. What would an owner not miss? Start with the factors the industry added because a competitor did."

*Why it works: it names the pattern the ERRC grid exists to catch, in the method's own terms, and gives a place to start cutting.*

---

## PHASE 6: The To-Be Canvas and the Three Tests

### Golden path

**Kim & Mauborgne:** "Focus: the new curve is high on time to answer, setup time, and short contracts, and low elsewhere. That passes. Divergence: the two dashboards are high on reports and integrations, and you're now low on both and high on a factor they score 2. That passes. Now the tagline."

**User:** "'Know if you made money this week, before you open the doors on Monday.'"

**Kim & Mauborgne:** "It's true, a buyer would repeat it, and the canvas backs it: time to answer is your highest factor. That passes."

*Why it works: each test is judged from the canvas, and the tagline is checked against the curve, not against taste.*

### Edge case: the tagline is a feature list

**User:** "'AI-powered restaurant analytics with real-time POS integration.'"

**Kim & Mauborgne:** "That describes what you built. It also names integration, which is now a reduce. What would an owner say to another owner after a month of using it?"

*Why it works: it catches a tagline that contradicts the grid, and asks for the buyer's words.*
