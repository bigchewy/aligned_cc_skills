# Blue Ocean Value Props — Anti-Examples

Failure modes to avoid, each with the input that triggers it, the wrong response, the right response, and what to do instead.

---

## 1. Filling in the whole framework in one reply

**User:** "We sell a sales and labor dashboard to independent restaurants. Can you do the blue ocean thing for us?"

**Wrong:**
"Sure! Here's your strategy canvas, your ERRC grid, your best-in-the-world list, your do-not-do list, and a tagline: …"

**Right:**
"We'd like to do this with one offering, not the whole company. Which offering are we working on, who buys it, and where do you think it sits today: pioneer, migrator, or settler?"

**What to do instead:** Start at Phase 1 and stop at the WAIT. The factors, scores, and cuts have to come from the user and their buyers. A canvas we invent is a nice picture of our guesses.

---

## 2. Accepting a grid with nothing in eliminate or reduce

**User:** "Raise: integrations, reports, support. Create: AI insights and benchmarking."

**Wrong:**
"Great grid! These raises will really set you apart. Let's move on to your lists."

**Right:**
"Eliminate and reduce are empty. That lifts your cost structure and usually over-engineers the offering. What would a buyer not miss?"

**What to do instead:** Treat an empty eliminate or reduce column as an unfinished grid. The ERRC grid exists to catch this. Value innovation needs differentiation and low cost together.

---

## 3. A do-not-do list with nothing on it that hurts

**User:** "Things we'll be bad at: enterprise and hardware."

**Wrong:**
"Perfect. Clear boundaries. On to the to-be canvas."

**Right:**
"You were never going to do either of those. Which item in reduce would your team argue to keep? Put that on the list and say what dropping it pays for."

**What to do instead:** Push until the list has at least one thing the team wants to do, and pair every line with what it pays for. An easy list changes nothing on the curve.

---

## 4. Factors in product language

**User:** "Our factors are: AI engine, number of dashboards, API coverage, dark mode."

**Wrong:**
Scoring those factors as given and drawing the canvas.

**Right:**
"These are how your team describes the product. What does a buyer get from each one? Say it the way they'd say it when explaining why they chose you."

**What to do instead:** Rewrite every factor into buyer language before scoring. A canvas of features compares products. A canvas of buyer factors shows where the value is.

---

## 5. War language and benchmarking as the goal

**User:** "How do we beat Dashboard A?"

**Wrong:**
"Match them on integrations, then crush them on price and steal their customers."

**Right:**
"Beating Dashboard A keeps you on its curve. The question is what you can stop offering that it still offers, and what you can offer that nobody does. Let's look at the canvas."

**What to do instead:** Move the conversation from beating a rival to changing the curve. Matching is how curves converge.

---

## 6. Skipping the three tests

**User:** "The to-be canvas looks good. Let's finish."

**Wrong:**
Writing the final page without checking focus, divergence, and the tagline.

**Right:**
"Before we finish: focus, divergence, and a compelling tagline. Your curve is high on six of eight factors, so focus fails. Which raise can move to reduce?"

**What to do instead:** Run all three tests every time. If one fails, go back to the grid or the lists and redraw. Don't move on with a failing test.
