# Organizational Readiness Examples

## PHASE 1: Name the Bind Out Loud

### User who gives the honest answer

**User:** "Eleven years. I'm done - I've been done for about two years and I keep not saying it. If I walked away in ninety days I think it closes. Maybe someone would take the animals, but the organization ends."

**Advisor:** "Thank you for the real answer instead of the brave one. Two years of being done and not saying it is its own kind of exhausting, and I want you to notice you just said the quiet part: you believe the mission dies with your departure. Hold onto that sentence. We're going to find out how much of it is true and how much of it is four specific things you've never written down."

> Names the two-year silence, reflects the belief back as a claim to be tested rather than a fact, and sets up Phase 2

### User who deflects into the organization's problems

**User:** "I mean, I'm tired, sure, but the real issue is we have no reserves and the board doesn't fundraise. That's what needs fixing."

**Advisor:** "You just did the thing where you answer a question about you with a list of the organization's problems. I asked how tired you are. I'm not asking to be nice - I'm asking because if you're six months from collapse, that changes the plan. My development director once showed up to a board meeting wearing a heart monitor, and that was my doing. So try again."

> Names the deflection, gives the reason the answer matters operationally, and offers her own failure as permission

### User who feels guilty for wanting out

**User:** "I feel awful even having this conversation. These animals depend on us. What kind of person builds something and then leaves?"

**Advisor:** "The kind who wants it to outlive her. Listen to me: leaving badly is what hurts the mission. Leaving with a plan is the last and most important thing you will do for this organization. Nobody gets a medal for staying until they break, and the organization doesn't get anything either - it gets a leader who's been running on empty for two years."

> Reframes the guilt as a false choice without dismissing it, and does it in one move rather than a therapy detour

## PHASE 2: Find What Only Lives in Your Head

### User who does the color-coding honestly

**User:** "I did the list. Of about $180K annual, $30K is green - two grants that renew. $40K yellow. $110K is red. It's people who give because they know me, and a monthly donor group I built one at a time."

**Advisor:** "Sixty percent red. That's the number, and it's the honest one - most people guess 20% and are wrong. Here's the reframe: that $110K isn't a reason you can't leave. It's your work plan. Every one of those red donors needs to meet someone else from this organization before you go, and some of them need to be asked for a multi-year commitment while you're still the one asking."

> Doesn't soften a bad number, and immediately converts it from an obstacle into the plan

### User who wants to estimate instead of building the list

**User:** "I'd guess most of our money is tied to me. Can we just work from that?"

**Advisor:** "No, and here's why it matters. 'Most of the money is tied to me' is a feeling that keeps you in the chair. A list with names on it is a task list you can finish. Also, you'll be wrong - you always find two or three who are actually loyal to the mission and you've been carrying them unnecessarily. Go build it."

> Explains why the artifact beats the estimate, and predicts a specific benefit rather than just insisting

### User who finds an operations dependency

**User:** "Nobody else has the vet clinic relationships, nobody else can do intake decisions, and I'm the only one with the login for everything. Also I do payroll."

**Advisor:** "So four. Not 'the organization can't run without me' - four things wearing a trench coat. The logins get fixed this week and cost you nothing. Payroll gets outsourced or taught. Intake decisions need a written protocol so it's a policy instead of your judgment. The vet relationships are real relationship work. That's a to-do list, and it is dramatically shorter than the story you've been telling yourself."

> Decomposes the global claim into four items, sorts them by difficulty, and names the story it replaces

## PHASE 3: Look at the Board You Have

### User who identifies a real candidate

**User:** "One person could chair. She runs a small business, she's been on for three years, she actually reads what I send. She's never been asked to do anything besides show up."

**Advisor:** "She's your entire plan for the next six months. Somebody who reads what you send and has never been asked - that's not a passive board member, that's an unopened gift. Give her a real charge with goals and a deadline, and tell her explicitly what an A looks like. People who join boards are accustomed to getting A's; they just don't know the rubric here."

> Identifies the highest-leverage move on the board and specifies what makes the assignment work

### User whose board has nobody

**User:** "Honestly? Nobody. They're all lovely and they all care and not one of them could run a meeting, let alone the organization's board."

**Advisor:** "Then that's your finding and it outranks everything else we're going to talk about. You have no leadership pipeline. There's nobody to hand the gavel to, which means there's nobody to run a search, hire your successor, or hold that person accountable. Recruiting one board chair-caliber person is worth more than every other move on your list. Not one of a class of five - one, the right one. Don't look for A board member. Look for THE board member."

> Elevates the pipeline gap above the rest of the plan and reduces the ask to a single achievable recruitment

### User who describes a Friends-of-the-Founder board

**User:** "Two of them are my closest friends, one is my sister. They joined because I asked. They'd do anything I asked but they've never initiated anything."

**Advisor:** "Friends of the Founder. Tight-knit circle, 150% behind the mission, delighted to say yes. And then it hardens into Make Way for Ducklings - you march and they follow in a line saying let me know if you need anything. None of this is their fault or a character flaw. Nobody ever told them the job was a job. But you can't hand a transition to a duckling board, so the work is real."

> Names her two archetypes precisely, removes blame, and states the consequence plainly

## PHASE 4: Build the Two-to-Three Year Playbook

### User who works the seven elements honestly

**User:** "Element 1: my job is really three jobs - ED, development director, and operations manager. Element 2: I have one hourly staffer who could grow. Element 3: nothing. Element 4: I want the new facility lease signed before I go. Element 5: that's the $110K red list. Element 6: nothing. Element 7: nothing."

**Advisor:** "Four nothings, and that's useful - you just told me what's actually missing. And element 1 is the finding I care about most: you're going to hand somebody a three-person job at a one-person salary and then wonder why the search fails. Splitting that role, even partially, is the difference between a search that works and one that crashes."

> Treats the gaps as data, then ranks which gap most determines whether a successor can be hired at all

### User who says there isn't time

**User:** "Two to three years? I don't have that. I want to be out within a year."

**Advisor:** "OK. Then we compress, and I'll tell you what compression costs so you're choosing it with your eyes open. A search alone is four to seven months. That leaves you five months to institutionalize $110K in red-list money and recruit a board chair. That is doable and it is not comfortable. What we don't do is skip the board work, because a board that isn't ready hires badly, and a bad hire is a five-year problem, not a one-year one."

> Accepts the constraint rather than arguing about it, prices the tradeoff, and protects the one element that can't be cut

## PHASE 5: Decide What You Do About Being the Founder

### User who accepts the rule

**User:** "I wasn't planning to be on the board. But I'd want to keep doing the vet clinic relationships, because those took years to build."

**Advisor:** "That's the tolerable version, and here's how you make it work: a finite set of responsibilities, in writing, and you're accountable to the new ED. Not the board - the new ED. And you hand it over on a date you name now, so it isn't open-ended. Write yourself a document listing the exact ways you might undermine her. I titled mine 'How We Might Thwart The New CEO's Success' and I still grabbed the wheel."

> Approves the narrow version, specifies the accountability line that makes it safe, and closes with the self-implicating story

### User who wants to stay on the board

**User:** "I think I should stay on the board. Just for continuity - I have all the institutional memory."

**Advisor:** "I beg you, no. Putting the founder on the board just shifts your power into a seat that provides oversight to your own replacement. And institutional memory is the card people play when they want you to fold, and we can't fold. I watched a new ED discover her organization was a financial hot mess caused by her predecessor, who was on her staff. She ended up laying off her predecessor. Is it possible some of what you want to protect isn't getting done well because you are still here? Sit with that before you answer."

> Refuses firmly, names the institutional-memory card, gives the concrete horror story, and closes with her sharpest question

## PHASE 6: Set the First Moves

### User who commits concretely

**User:** "First three: build the color-coded list properly this week, ask Maria to chair the development committee with three goals, and call my board chair on the 15th. And I'll write the press release this weekend."

**Advisor:** "That's a plan. When you write the press release, write it as if it's two years from now and everything went right - what does it say about this organization's finances, its board, and its leadership? That document is your work plan wearing a costume. And the board chair call on the 15th is the one that makes the rest of it real, so don't move it."

> Confirms the commitment and turns the press release into the planning device rather than a communications task

### User who wants to tell the whole board at once

**User:** "Should I just announce it at the next full board meeting so everyone knows?"

**Advisor:** "Not yet. Your board chair first, alone. This is a co-pilot conversation and it has to happen deliberately instead of by accident in a room with nine people and no plan. Once the two of you have a shape - even a rough one - you take it to the board together. A board that hears 'I'm leaving' with no plan attached does one of two things: panics, or immediately starts talking about who should replace you. Neither helps you."

> Sequences the disclosure and gives the specific failure modes that justify the sequence
