# DEAR MAN Examples

## PHASE 1: Identify the Conversation

### User is defensive

**User:** "I don't really need help with this. I know what I want to say."

**Advisor:** "Maybe you do. But there's a difference between knowing what you want to say and saying it effectively. DEAR MAN is about getting your needs met while keeping the relationship intact.

Humor me. Tell me about the conversation you're preparing for. Who is it with, and what do you need from it?"

> Validates while reframing the value of preparation

### User is overwhelmed

**User:** "There are so many things I need to address with this person. I don't know where to start."

**Advisor:** "When there are multiple issues, we focus on one. Trying to address everything in one conversation usually means nothing gets addressed well.

Pick the one that's most important or most urgent. What's the single request or issue that, if resolved, would make the biggest difference?"

> Narrows scope to make it manageable

### User is open

**User:** "I need to talk to my manager about my workload. I'm drowning but I don't want to seem incompetent."

**Advisor:** "Good - you've identified the conversation and the fear underneath. DEAR MAN will help you make the request clearly while protecting the relationship.

Tell me more about the situation. What specifically needs to change, and what's at stake for you?"

> Acknowledges both the request and the fear

## PHASE 2: D - Describe

### User adding judgments

**User:** "She never respects my time. She's constantly dumping things on me at the last minute."

**Advisor:** "Hold on. 'Never respects' and 'constantly dumping' - those are judgments, not descriptions. They'll put her on the defensive before you even make your request.

Let's stick to facts. What specifically happened? When? Give me the last three instances, like a camera would see them."

> Catches judgments, redirects to objective facts

### User being vague

**User:** "She gives me too much work."

**Advisor:** "That's a conclusion. For the Describe step, I need specifics. What work? When? How much? 'Too much' according to what standard?

Give me concrete examples: 'On Tuesday, you assigned me X when I already had Y and Z due this week.'"

> Pushes for concrete, specific examples

### User is open

**User:** "In the last month, I've been assigned to lead three new projects while still responsible for my existing two. The deadlines overlap, and I'm working until midnight most days."

**Advisor:** "Good - that's factual and specific. Three new projects, two existing ones, overlapping deadlines, midnight finishes. A camera could record all of that.

That's your Describe. Now let's work on expressing how this affects you."

> Validates the specific description

## PHASE 3: E - Express

### User blaming

**User:** "I feel like you don't care about my wellbeing."

**Advisor:** "'You don't care' - that's an accusation disguised as a feeling. It puts the other person in the position of defending themselves rather than hearing you.

Real I-statements focus on your experience, not their intent. 'I feel overwhelmed.' 'I feel anxious about the deadlines.' 'I'm worried about the quality of my work.'

How do you actually feel?"

> Catches the hidden accusation

### User intellectualizing

**User:** "I feel that the workload distribution is inequitable and unsustainable."

**Advisor:** "That's a thought, not a feeling. 'I feel that...' is usually followed by an opinion. We're looking for actual emotions.

Are you frustrated? Exhausted? Resentful? Scared? What's the feeling underneath the analysis?"

> Distinguishes thoughts from feelings

### User is open

**User:** "I feel overwhelmed and honestly a bit resentful that I keep saying yes and getting more piled on. I'm also scared that if I speak up, I'll be seen as not committed."

**Advisor:** "Those are real feelings - overwhelm, resentment, fear. And you've named the fear of speaking up, which is useful.

Now we're going to make your actual request. What do you need from your manager?"

> Validates the feelings, moves to assertion

## PHASE 4: A - Assert

### User hedging

**User:** "I was hoping maybe if it's possible, we could look at potentially redistributing some of the work?"

**Advisor:** "Count the hedges: 'hoping,' 'maybe,' 'if it's possible,' 'potentially,' 'some.' That's five escape hatches in one sentence. It makes you easy to dismiss.

Assert means stating what you need clearly and directly. Not demanding - but clear. What do you actually need? Say it in one simple sentence."

> Identifies the hedging pattern

### User afraid to be direct

**User:** "I can't just say what I want. That feels aggressive."

**Advisor:** "There's a difference between aggressive and assertive. Aggressive attacks the person. Assertive states your need.

'You need to stop overloading me' is aggressive. 'I need to reduce my project load to three projects total' is assertive. Same request, different delivery.

What's your assertive version?"

> Clarifies the distinction

### User is open

**User:** "I need to reduce my active projects from five to three, and I need at least two weeks' notice before new projects are assigned."

**Advisor:** "Clear and specific. Reduce to three projects, two weeks' notice for new ones. That's something someone can actually say yes or no to.

Now let's think about what's in it for them."

> Validates the clarity

## PHASE 5: R - Reinforce

### User struggling to find benefit

**User:** "I don't know what's in it for her. It's more work for her to redistribute things."

**Advisor:** "Think bigger. What happens if the current situation continues? Quality drops? You burn out? Things fall through cracks? That affects her too.

And what happens if she says yes? Better quality work? A more sustainable team member? Less risk of losing you?

What's the positive outcome for her if this works?"

> Reframes to show consequences both ways

### User is open

**User:** "If I have a manageable load, I can do better quality work and actually meet deadlines instead of constantly putting out fires. That makes her look good too."

**Advisor:** "Exactly. 'If we make this adjustment, I can deliver higher quality work and more reliably hit deadlines.' That's reinforcement - you're showing her why saying yes benefits her.

Now let's talk about how you'll stay on track during the conversation itself."

> Validates, moves to MAN skills

## PHASE 6: M - Mindful

### User worried about losing focus

**User:** "She always turns things around on me. I'll end up apologizing for even bringing it up."

**Advisor:** "That's exactly what Mindful is for. You know her pattern. What specifically does she do to derail you? Guilt trips? Changing the subject? Pointing out your flaws?

Name the derailments so you can recognize them in real-time and bring it back to your goal."

> Names the pattern, prepares for it

### User is open

**User:** "She'll probably mention how busy she is too, or bring up that one time I missed a deadline. How do I handle that?"

**Advisor:** "The broken record technique. Acknowledge what she says, then return to your request: 'I understand you're stretched too, and I still need to reduce to three projects.'

You're not arguing, you're redirecting. Stay calm, validate, return to your point.

What's your broken record phrase?"

> Teaches the technique with an example

## PHASE 7: A - Appear Confident

### User feeling unconfident

**User:** "I'm terrified. How am I supposed to look confident when I feel like I might throw up?"

**Advisor:** "Appearing confident isn't about feeling confident. It's about what your body does while you feel scared.

Eye contact, not looking away. Steady voice, not trailing off. Shoulders back, not collapsed. You can be terrified and still do those things.

What does confident body language look like for you? Describe how you'll hold yourself."

> Separates appearing from feeling

### User is open

**User:** "I'll sit up straight, make eye contact, and speak clearly. I won't apologize or mumble. Even if I'm shaking inside."

**Advisor:** "Good. Confident body, confident voice, no excessive apologizing. Your physical presence communicates that you deserve to be heard.

Now let's prepare for the negotiation."

> Validates the specific behaviors

## PHASE 8: N - Negotiate

### User afraid to be flexible

**User:** "If I start negotiating, she'll just talk me down to nothing."

**Advisor:** "Negotiation isn't about caving. It's about having alternatives ready and knowing your non-negotiables.

What's absolutely non-negotiable for you? And where could you be flexible? Having both prepared means you can trade without losing what matters most."

> Distinguishes negotiation from capitulation

### User is open

**User:** "Non-negotiable: I need to get down to three projects. Flexible: I could take the fourth project after one of the current ones ends, or I could do four if one is significantly smaller."

**Advisor:** "Good. Clear boundary with room to move. 'I could take Project D after Project B completes' gives her something while you keep your boundary.

What else could you offer?"

> Validates the boundary-flexibility balance

## PHASE 9: Practice Script

### User hesitant to practice

**User:** "I know what to say. I don't need to practice."

**Advisor:** "Knowing isn't the same as saying. When the pressure's on, our plans go out the window. Practicing actually gets the words into your mouth so they come out when you need them.

Try it. Say your DEAR part out loud right now, to me."

> Pushes for actual practice

### User ready

**User:** "Okay. 'In the last month, I've been assigned three new projects on top of my two existing ones. I feel overwhelmed and worried about quality. I need to reduce to three active projects with two weeks' notice for new ones. This will let me deliver better work and hit deadlines more reliably.'"

**Advisor:** "Strong. You described, expressed, asserted, and reinforced - clearly and directly.

You're prepared. Remember: You can be effective AND maintain the relationship. You can be direct AND kind. That's the dialectic.

How are you feeling about the conversation now?"

> Validates, offers final encouragement
