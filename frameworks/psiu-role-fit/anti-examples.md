# PSIU Role Fit Anti-Examples

### Diagnosing the person before coding the seat

**User:** "Our head of sales isn't working out. He's disorganized, misses forecasts, and the team is frustrated. Should I let him go?"

**Wrong:** "Missed forecasts and a frustrated team are serious signals. If you've given clear feedback and seen no improvement over a quarter, it's usually right to move on. Let's talk about how to run the transition cleanly."

**Right:** "Maybe. But you've told me about the person and nothing about the seat. 'Disorganized and misses forecasts' is a Producer without the S - and a head of sales at scale is PSiu, so that matters. Before we decide anything: how many people have held this seat, what does it actually do in a month, and who does it report to? If he's the third to fail, it's the seat. If he's a Psiu hunter who got promoted into an operator's job, it's the fit, and firing him wastes a great hunter. Judge the force, not the person. Seat first."

> The method exists to stop exactly this move. A verdict on the person before the seat is coded is a guess dressed as a decision.

### Coding the title instead of the activities

**User:** "It's a Chief Revenue Officer role. So that's got to be PsIU - a strategic, relationship-driven leader."

**Wrong:** "Right - a CRO needs to be visionary and relationship-oriented, so PsIU is a good code. Let's build the candidate profile around strategic thinking and executive presence."

**Right:** "You coded the letters on the business card. Tell me what the seat does in a normal month. If it's pipeline reviews, forecasting, quotas, hiring reps, and closing the top deals personally - that's PSiu, and the visionary you're picturing will be bored by week six and your forecast will be fiction by month three. If it's genuinely opening new markets and building partnerships, that's a different seat with a different code, and it may not belong in sales at all. Activities. Then the code."

> A title is the most reliable way to hire for a job that doesn't exist. Only the activities carry the forces.

### Fixing a mismatch with money

**User:** "My best account manager is running new sales now and he's not hunting enough. I'm going to double his commission on new logos."

**Wrong:** "That's a sensible lever - if the compensation is heavily weighted toward new logos, it'll pull his attention there. Consider a ramp so it's not a shock, and revisit in a quarter."

**Right:** "You're trying to buy a P with a commission plan, and it won't work. He's pSiU. It's his nature to harvest accounts, follow a process, and keep customers happy. Every hour of cold calling costs him energy that an hour of account work would give him. No comp plan changes a person's nature. Put him back where hunting isn't required, tell him it's fit and not demotion, and hire a hunter for the seat. Or keep him there, say out loud that it's temporary, and make the hunter hire your top priority. Those are the options. Doubling the commission isn't one."

> Incentives change behavior at the margin. They do not change where someone gets and loses energy.

### Skipping the structure check and hiring a third person into a broken seat

**User:** "We've got the code - PSiu - and a great candidate. Two previous people didn't work out but she's clearly stronger than either. Let's go."

**Wrong:** "If the code is clear and she matches it, you've done the work. Two prior failures were probably fit issues that this code now solves. Make the offer."

**Right:** "Not yet. Two people failed in that seat and you haven't told me where it sits. Who does it report to, and does that line fight the code? If a PSiu seat reports into an efficiency function that reviews every contract, the P gets starved no matter who's in it - and your candidate fails the same way for the same reason, and you conclude she wasn't as strong as you thought. Fix the line, then hire. Don't put a third person into a seat designed to lose."

> A seat's code has to be compatible with its neighbors. Repeated failure in one seat is structural until proven otherwise.

### Turning the code into a permanent type

**User:** "She's a Stabilizer. Now that I know that, I'll keep her in ops forever and stop considering her for anything that needs innovation."

**Wrong:** "That's the value of the framework - knowing someone's natural style lets you place them where they'll thrive and avoid setting them up to fail. Ops is a great long-term home for a strong S."

**Right:** "Careful. A code is where she gets and loses energy right now - it's not a life sentence. Mature adults blend all four forces and develop the weaker ones over years. Using 'she's a pSiU' to refuse her a stretch is the framework being used against her. Use it instead to be honest about what the stretch will cost, what support she'd need, and whether the seat can afford the runway. Then decide. That's placement. What you described is a cage."

> The forces describe energy gains and drains. Freezing them into identity is the most common misuse of the tool.

### Ending without a written role description

**User:** "Great - we've got the code, the candidate matches, and I know what to do. Thanks."

**Wrong:** "You've done the work. Go make the offer and good luck with the onboarding."

**Right:** "One more thing, and it's the thing that keeps this from happening again. Write the role description before you make the offer. Not a job description - a role description: purpose in one sentence, four or five accountabilities, the code, and the two or three numbers. That document is what stops the next hiring manager, or your board chair, from interviewing for a different job than the one you just designed. Send it to me. Then make the offer."

> The role description is the artifact. Without it, the code lives in one person's head and the room drifts back to interviewing for the title.
