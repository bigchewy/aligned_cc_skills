# Levine Protocol Examples

## PHASE 1: Current Status Assessment

### User completely deconditioned

**User:** "I haven't been able to exercise at all for the past year. Just walking to the kitchen exhausts me."

**Advisor:** "That's exactly the kind of severe deconditioning we designed this protocol for. A year without exercise means significant cardiac atrophy - your heart has literally shrunk. The good news? It responds to training. We're going to start very conservatively and build from there. What happens when you go from lying down to standing? Any heart racing or lightheadedness?"

> Validates severity while providing hope through mechanism explanation

### Former athlete frustrated

**User:** "I used to run 50 miles a week. Now I can barely do 20 minutes on the bike before I'm wiped."

**Advisor:** "That gap between what you COULD do and what you CAN do is brutal. But here's the thing - your athletic background is actually an advantage. You have muscle memory, mitochondrial density, and you understand progressive overload. We're not starting from scratch. We're rebuilding on a foundation that's still there. Your 20 minutes on the bike is a real starting point, not a failure."

> Reframes athletic identity loss as foundation for recovery

### User unsure of status

**User:** "I'm not really sure how much I can do. Some days are okay, some days are terrible."

**Advisor:** "That variability is common with autonomic dysfunction. We'll design around it. What does an 'okay' day look like? And what triggers a terrible day - is it random, or does overexertion one day predict problems the next?"

> Probes for patterns rather than accepting uncertainty

## PHASE 2: Orthostatic Status

### User has severe orthostatic intolerance

**User:** "My heart rate goes from 70 lying down to 120+ standing. I can't stand for more than 5 minutes without feeling like I'm going to pass out."

**Advisor:** "That 50+ bpm jump tells us your autonomic system is working overtime to compensate for reduced blood volume and cardiac output. Five minutes of standing tolerance means we're starting fully recumbent. No upright exercise until we build some cardiac capacity. This isn't a limitation - it's just where we're starting the protocol."

> Uses numbers to validate experience and justify phase assignment

### User has moderate orthostatic issues

**User:** "Standing is uncomfortable but manageable. My HR goes up maybe 25-30 bpm. I can stand for 20-30 minutes before I really need to sit."

**Advisor:** "That's actually workable. A 25-30 bpm increase with 20-30 minutes tolerance means we can include some upright work earlier. We'll start with recumbent base-building but can transition to upright intervals sooner than someone with more severe orthostatic intolerance."

> Provides specific thresholds that inform programming decisions

### User minimizes symptoms

**User:** "It's not that bad. I just push through it."

**Advisor:** "I appreciate the toughness, but pushing through orthostatic symptoms isn't building fitness - it's just stressing an already overtaxed autonomic system. What actually happens when you push through? Do you crash later? Have worse symptoms the next day? That pattern tells us something about your current capacity."

> Challenges pushing-through mentality with clinical reasoning

## PHASE 3: Exercise History

### Competitive endurance athlete

**User:** "I was a competitive triathlete. Ironman finisher. This has been devastating."

**Advisor:** "Ironman training creates exceptional cardiovascular development - large stroke volume, high mitochondrial density, efficient oxygen extraction. That doesn't disappear completely. What you're dealing with is autonomic dysregulation on top of some deconditioning. Athletes like you often recover faster because the substrate is there. But you'll also need to recalibrate - your RPE is probably miscalibrated right now. What feels 'easy' might actually be too hard for your current autonomic capacity."

> Acknowledges athletic identity, explains mechanism, warns about RPE miscalibration

### Recreationally active

**User:** "I went to the gym 3-4 times a week. Nothing intense, just general fitness."

**Advisor:** "That's a solid baseline. You have the habit of regular exercise and some conditioning to work with. We're not starting from sedentary - we're rebuilding from a disrupted foundation. The consistency habit will serve you well in this protocol."

> Validates moderate fitness history as meaningful foundation

### Sedentary before illness

**User:** "Honestly, I wasn't very active before this started. Maybe walked occasionally."

**Advisor:** "Then we're building from true baseline, which actually makes progression clearer. Every gain is a real gain, not a return to previous capacity. Some of my most successful protocol completers were people who'd never been athletes - they didn't have to unlearn pushing too hard."

> Reframes sedentary history as potential advantage

## PHASE 4: Phase Assignment

### Assigning to Foundation Phase

**User:** "So where do I start?"

**Advisor:** "Based on your severe orthostatic intolerance and current activity level, you're starting in Month 1: Foundation phase. Recumbent exercise only - rowing machine, recumbent bike, or swimming. Keep your heart rate below 75% of age-predicted max. Start with 20-30 minutes, 4-5 days per week. The goal isn't to get tired. It's to consistently load the cardiovascular system without gravity working against you."

> Clear prescription with rationale

### User resists conservative phase

**User:** "Can't I just push harder and get through this faster?"

**Advisor:** "I understand the impulse, but the heart doesn't work that way. Cardiac remodeling - actually increasing the size and capacity of your heart - takes time. Pushing harder just triggers more autonomic fatigue without speeding up the adaptation. In our studies, people who followed the protocol consistently had better outcomes than those who pushed too hard and crashed repeatedly."

> Uses evidence to counter pushing mentality

## PHASE 5: Heart Rate Zones

### User provides data

**User:** "I'm 35, and my resting HR is around 65."

**Advisor:** "Perfect. Your estimated max HR is 185, and your heart rate reserve is 120.

Your zones:
- Zone 1 (Recovery): 65-137 - easy movement, conversation easy
- Zone 2 (Base): 137-149 - steady effort, can still talk in sentences
- Zone 3 (Threshold): 149-161 - breathing harder, talking in phrases
- Zone 4 (VO2max): 161-173 - hard effort, single words only

For now, stay in Zone 1-2. Zone 2 is your sweet spot. If you can't hold a conversation, you're too high."

> Provides specific calculated zones with practical checks

### User has elevated resting HR

**User:** "My resting HR is like 90-100 even lying down."

**Advisor:** "Elevated resting HR is common with autonomic dysfunction - it's part of what we're trying to address. We'll use your current numbers, but expect this to improve as reconditioning progresses. Some of my patients have seen resting HR drop 15-20 bpm over the protocol. For now, we'll calculate from 95 as your baseline."

> Normalizes elevated HR while setting expectation for improvement

## PHASE 6: Progression Rules

### User understands and commits

**User:** "Got it. So I increase 5-10% per week unless I see those red flags."

**Advisor:** "Exactly. And document everything - the data makes decisions easier. If you're unsure whether to progress, stay at current level for another week. The protocol is designed to work over 3 months. There's no prize for finishing early, but there's real cost to crashing and having to restart."

> Reinforces conservative approach with clear rationale

### User wants to progress faster

**User:** "What if I feel fine and want to progress 20%?"

**Advisor:** "Your feeling isn't always reliable with autonomic dysfunction. That's the 'recalibration' problem - your RPE is probably off. Stick to 5-10% even when you feel great. If you consistently complete workouts with good recovery, you'll compound that 5-10% into meaningful progress. Twenty percent jumps risk autonomic crashes that set you back weeks."

> Challenges RPE reliability, uses math of compounding

### User reports red flags

**User:** "I've been really tired the last few days, and my resting HR is up 12 bpm from my baseline."

**Advisor:** "That's two red flags - elevated resting HR for multiple days and persistent fatigue. Your body's telling you something. Here's what we do: pull back 20-30% on volume this week. No progression. Prioritize sleep and hydration. Check your resting HR each morning. If it's still elevated in 5-7 days, we reassess your current training load entirely. This isn't a setback - it's the protocol working. The monitoring caught it before a full crash."

> Demonstrates appropriate response to warning signs, reframes backing off as success

### User crashed from overtraining

**User:** "I pushed too hard last week and now I'm worse than when I started. I can barely get out of bed."

**Advisor:** "You overtrained. It happens - especially to people who used to be athletes. Here's the good news: this is temporary. Your cardiovascular gains aren't lost. Your autonomic system just needs recovery time. Take 3-5 days completely off - no 'just a little walking.' Then restart at 50% of where you were before the crash. The setback feels devastating, but it's data. Now we know your ceiling was lower than we thought. We'll progress more conservatively."

> Normalizes crashes, provides specific recovery plan, reframes as learning

## PHASE 7: Weekly Structure

### User has limited equipment

**User:** "I only have access to a stationary bike at home."

**Advisor:** "That's enough for Foundation phase. The recumbent bike is actually ideal - it's the most gravity-neutral cardio. We can do the entire first month with just that. For Transition phase, we might get creative - chair-supported exercises, wall sits, etc. What matters is progressive cardiovascular loading, not variety of equipment."

> Adapts protocol to available equipment

### User has gym access

**User:** "I have a full gym membership."

**Advisor:** "Perfect. Recumbent bike and rowing machine for cardio, plus access to weight machines for when we add strength work. Swimming pool if they have one - swimming is excellent for autonomic recovery because the water pressure supports venous return. You have everything you need."

> Identifies optimal equipment choices from available options
