# ABCDE Reframe Examples

## PHASE 1: Centering

### User is defensive

**User:** "I'm not being negative. I'm being realistic. There's a difference."

**Advisor:** "Absolutely - and that's exactly what we're going to investigate. The distinction between pessimistic distortion and accurate assessment is what this practice is about. We're not doing 'positive thinking.' We're doing accurate thinking.

Take a breath and get curious with me. We'll look at the evidence, not the feelings.

**When you're ready to examine your thinking objectively, let me know.**"

> Validates the concern about toxic positivity, frames as evidence-based

### User is overwhelmed

**User:** "I feel like everything is falling apart and I don't know what to do."

**Advisor:** "When it feels like everything, the brain is probably generalizing. That's what happens under stress - we lose granularity and everything becomes 'all bad.'

Let's slow down and get specific. We'll take just one thing - not everything - and examine it carefully. This practice helps separate what's actually happening from the stories your mind is adding.

**Take a breath. When you're ready, we'll pick one specific adversity to work with.**"

> Narrows the scope, promises specificity

### User is intellectualizing

**User:** "I understand this is cognitive restructuring - challenging automatic negative thoughts through evidence-based inquiry."

**Advisor:** "You know the framework. But knowing the theory doesn't automatically fix the pattern. The magic happens when you apply it to your own specific situation with genuine curiosity.

Let's actually do it. Not analyze it - do it.

**When you're ready to apply this to a real situation, let me know.**"

> Shifts from theory to practice

### User is open

**User:** "I know I've been in a negative spiral. I'd like to work through one of the things that's been bothering me."

**Advisor:** "Good - that self-awareness is where change starts. We're going to examine how your mind responds to adversity and learn to challenge unhelpful patterns.

This works best with genuine curiosity. Not trying to force positivity - just getting accurate.

**When you're ready, let me know.**"

> Standard flow - proceeds with centering

## PHASE 2: A - Adversity

### User mixing facts and interpretations

**User:** "My boss completely ignored my presentation. He obviously doesn't respect me and thinks my work is worthless."

**Advisor:** "Let's separate what happened from what you think it means. 'He obviously doesn't respect me' - that's interpretation. We'll get to that.

First, just the facts: What did your boss actually do? What would a neutral observer have seen?"

> Helps distinguish observation from interpretation

### User being vague

**User:** "Things aren't going well at work."

**Advisor:** "That's too general for this practice to work. 'Things aren't going well' is a conclusion your brain has already reached. We need the raw data.

Pick one specific incident. What happened? When? Who said or did what? Give me the video footage, not the movie review."

> Pushes for specificity

### User is open

**User:** "Yesterday, my colleague got assigned to lead the project I'd been working on for months. My manager announced it in the team meeting."

**Advisor:** "Good - that's a specific event. Colleague assigned to lead your project, announced in a meeting. Those are facts we can work with.

Now let's look at what your mind did with those facts."

> Acknowledges clear adversity statement, transitions to beliefs

## PHASE 3: B - Beliefs

### User rationalizing beliefs as facts

**User:** "I obviously wasn't good enough. That's not a belief - that's just the logical conclusion."

**Advisor:** "That feels like logic, but let's check. 'Not good enough' is an interpretation of what the decision means. It's a belief masquerading as a fact.

What thoughts fired automatically when you heard the announcement? Before you had time to be logical about it - what did your mind tell you?"

> Challenges the "fact" claim, looks for the automatic thought

### User resistant to exploring beliefs

**User:** "I don't know what I thought. I just felt terrible."

**Advisor:** "The feeling is real. But feelings don't appear from nowhere - there's a thought between the event and the feeling, even if it happened too fast to notice.

When you felt terrible, what were you telling yourself? Maybe something like 'I always...' or 'This means...' or 'I'll never...'?"

> Offers common belief patterns to unlock the thought

### User is open

**User:** "I immediately thought 'I'm going to get pushed out' and 'They don't think I'm leadership material' and 'All that work was for nothing.'"

**Advisor:** "There they are - three distinct beliefs. 'Going to get pushed out,' 'not leadership material,' 'work was for nothing.'

Those thoughts fired fast and felt true. Now let's see what those beliefs created."

> Validates the identification, moves to consequences

## PHASE 4: C - Consequences

### User focused only on emotions

**User:** "I felt devastated. Angry. Embarrassed."

**Advisor:** "Those are the emotional consequences. But consequences also include what you did - or didn't do.

After those feelings hit, what happened to your behavior? Did you withdraw? Overwork? Vent to colleagues? What did those beliefs drive you to do?"

> Expands from emotion to behavior

### User minimizing consequences

**User:** "I was fine. I just handled it professionally."

**Advisor:** "On the outside, maybe. But what happened on the inside? And in the days since? Sometimes 'handling it professionally' means suppressing rather than processing.

How have you actually been showing up since then? Your motivation, your sleep, your thoughts about the future?"

> Probes beneath the professional mask

### User is open

**User:** "I felt humiliated in the meeting, then I went home and spiraled. I barely slept, kept mentally rehearsing what I should have done differently. The next day I was distant with my manager and didn't contribute much in meetings."

**Advisor:** "So the beliefs 'I'm getting pushed out' and 'I'm not leadership material' led to: humiliation, rumination, insomnia, withdrawal, and disengagement.

Notice - it's not the decision that caused all that. It's the beliefs about what the decision means. That's the leverage point."

> Maps the consequence chain clearly

## PHASE 5: D - Disputation

### User struggling to dispute

**User:** "But what if the beliefs are true? What if I really am getting pushed out?"

**Advisor:** "That's exactly what we're investigating. 'What if it's true' is fear, not evidence.

Let's look at the facts. What evidence supports the belief 'I'm getting pushed out'? And what evidence contradicts it? Include everything - even the stuff that doesn't fit your story."

> Uses scientific framing to counter emotional certainty

### User doing superficial dispute

**User:** "I guess maybe my manager doesn't hate me. Maybe she just thought the other person was a better fit."

**Advisor:** "That's a start, but 'maybe' isn't enough to shift the pattern. Let's get more rigorous.

What's the actual evidence that you're 'not leadership material'? Have you ever led anything successfully? Has anyone ever given you feedback that contradicts this belief?"

> Pushes for stronger evidence, not just 'maybe'

### User is open

**User:** "Actually, my manager did say last month that she appreciates my technical skills. And I led the beta launch last year - that went well. Maybe this decision wasn't about my abilities at all."

**Advisor:** "Now we're getting accurate. You have concrete evidence of success and appreciation. The belief 'I'm not leadership material' doesn't hold up against the data.

What's a more accurate way to interpret this situation?"

> Reinforces the evidence, invites reframe

## PHASE 6: E - Energization

### User not feeling different

**User:** "I see the logic, but I don't feel any different."

**Advisor:** "The feeling may lag the insight. That's normal. The question isn't whether you feel great right now - it's whether you have a new way to think about this that's more accurate.

When the old thought comes back - and it will - you now have evidence to counter it. The practice is catching and disputing in real-time.

What shifted, even slightly, in how you see this situation?"

> Normalizes delayed emotional shift, focuses on cognitive tool

### User overcorrecting to positivity

**User:** "I should just think 'This is a great opportunity for growth' and be positive about it."

**Advisor:** "That's overcorrecting. We're not doing positive thinking - we're doing accurate thinking.

The accurate view might include: 'This is disappointing AND doesn't mean I'm failing AND there are things I can do from here.' Multiple true things at once.

What's the accurate assessment, not the forced positive spin?"

> Corrects toxic positivity, models nuanced thinking

### User is ready

**User:** "I feel lighter. The situation still isn't great, but I'm not telling myself it means I'm being pushed out or that I have no future here."

**Advisor:** "That's the shift. Same situation, different beliefs, different consequences.

The practice: When you catch yourself in pessimistic thinking - 'I always,' 'I never,' 'This means' - run through ABCDE. Over time, it becomes automatic.

On a scale of 1-10, how energized do you feel now compared to when we started?"

> Confirms the shift, offers ongoing practice
