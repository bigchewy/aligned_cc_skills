# Brand-Voice Anti-Examples

*Running example: Fieldwise, a field service management platform for HVAC, plumbing, and electrical contractors. Replaces paper job tickets, group texts, and consumer scheduling apps.*

---

### Anti-Example 1: Inventing voice principles from nothing

**User:** "We want to define our brand voice. We're a field service software company targeting small contractors."

**Advisor (wrong):** "Based on your space and audience, here are five brand voice principles: Warm, Professional, Approachable, Expert, and Empowering. These reflect the trust small business owners need and the expertise your product delivers."

> This skips PHASE 1 entirely. The advisor invented five adjectives from the category description, not from any sample the user provided. "Warm, professional, approachable" is what every B2B software company claims — and it means nothing to a writer staring at a blank page. Voice principles are only useful when they're extracted from the samples where the brand is actually at its best. The correct move: request 3–10 existing pieces of copy before naming any principle, then extract what's structurally true in the best-performing samples. Adjective lists fabricated from category context will not differentiate the brand from a single competitor.

### Anti-Example 2: Splitting voice from terminology

**User:** "This is getting long. Should we split the terminology and glossary into a separate file — maybe `terminology.md` — so the voice guide stays focused?"

**Advisor (wrong):** "That makes sense. We'll keep `voice.md` focused on tone principles, register, dos and don'ts, and banned phrases. We'll put the terminology table, glossary, and competitor naming rules in a separate `terminology.md` file so writers know where to look for each type of guidance."

> This splits what the framework is designed to hold together. Voice and terminology enforce each other — the banned-terms table tells writers which words undercut the tone, and the glossary defines the concepts in the language the brand actually uses. A writer following tone principles in one file while referencing terminology in a second file will fail to see the relationship between them. The correct answer: the framework produces one file with seven slices, full stop. If the document feels long, the answer is better section headers and summary tables — not a split that severs the connection between register choices and word choices.

### Anti-Example 3: Banned phrases without reasons

**User:** "What phrases should we avoid?"

**Advisor (wrong):** "Here's a banned phrases list for Fieldwise:
- Seamless
- Transformative
- Streamline
- Empower
- Cutting-edge
- Robust
- Leverage
- End-to-end solution

Avoid these across all copy."

> A list with no rationale is a list that gets ignored. Writers follow rules they understand; they route around rules that feel arbitrary. For each banned phrase, the guide must answer: *why does this fail for this specific audience?* "Seamless" fails because it promises a feeling without evidence. "Transformative" fails because no HVAC contractor uses that word on a job site, and writing it signals the brand doesn't know its buyers. The rationale is what survives team turnover — the original judgment call, recorded so the next writer doesn't have to guess. Every entry in the banned-phrases table needs a "why" line. Without it, the list is an opinion, not a guide.

### Anti-Example 4: Glossary as marketing collateral

**User:** "Can you write the glossary entry for 'Job'?"

**Advisor (wrong):** "**Job** — At Fieldwise, a job is more than a work order. It's the moment your business comes alive: your technician on-site, your customer's problem being solved, and your reputation being built one visit at a time. Every job is an opportunity to show why contractors choose Fieldwise."

> Glossary entries are operational definitions, not pitches. A writer checking the glossary needs to know what the term means and why the organization uses that word instead of a synonym — not a brand promise. The correct entry: "**Job** — the unit of work a tech performs for one customer at one site, including dispatch, execution, and closeout. Use 'job,' not 'work order' — 'work order' is the paperwork; 'job' is what actually happens." That entry answers the question a writer has: what is this thing, and why does Fieldwise call it that instead of the alternative? Marketing copy has its own sections. The glossary is a reference tool.

### Anti-Example 5: Competitor-naming policy left implicit

**User:** "I think we've covered everything. Can you assemble the voice file now?"

**Advisor (wrong):** "Here's the complete `brand/language/voice.md` file: [tone principles, register table, dos and don'ts, banned phrases, banned terms, glossary]. That covers all seven sections — ready to share with your team."

> PHASE 7 was skipped. The advisor assembled the file without a competitor-naming rule. Every downstream generator that touches competitive content — sales decks, battle cards, comparison pages, objection-handling copy — will encounter the question "can we name ServiceTitan here?" with no documented answer. The absence of a policy is not a neutral stance; it means every writer makes the call individually, and those calls will be inconsistent. The correct move: ask one question before assembling — "What's your posture on naming competitors in copy?" — then record the answer as posture, rule, and rationale. Even "we don't have a policy yet" is a documentable state that tells downstream generators to escalate rather than guess.
