# Brand-Voice Examples

*Running example: Fieldwise, a field service management platform for HVAC, plumbing, and electrical contractors. Replaces paper job tickets, group texts, and consumer scheduling apps.*

---

## PHASE 1: Sample intake

### User shares scattered, inconsistent samples

**User:** "Here's some copy from our site: 'Fieldwise empowers contractors to streamline their field operations with cutting-edge technology.' And from our last email campaign: 'Get jobs done faster. Dispatch smarter. Win more bids.' We also have some LinkedIn posts — mostly updates about new hires."

**Advisor:** "I can see two very different voices competing here. The website line is corporate: passive construction, adjectives doing the heavy lifting ('empowering,' 'cutting-edge'), no sense of who's speaking. The email subject lines are completely different — short, active, verb-first, written like a contractor would talk. One of these is your actual voice. The other is what happens when someone writes for 'a software company' generically. Before we extract principles, tell me: which pieces were written by your founders or people who've spent time on job sites? I want to read those separately."

> Surfaces the voice-inconsistency immediately from the samples; names the structural difference without judgment; directs toward the authentic source

### User flags copy they know is off-brand

**User:** "The 'empowers contractors to streamline' line was written by an agency. The email copy was my co-founder. He ran HVAC crews for six years before we started this. Also — we have a blog post he wrote called 'Why I stopped using ServiceTitan.' That one got shared a lot."

**Advisor:** "That blog post is the reference sample I want to read most. Here's what I'm seeing across what you've shared: when your co-founder writes, sentences run short, there's no hedging, and the subject is almost always a person or a job — not an abstraction. When the agency wrote, the subject is the software. The software 'empowers.' The software 'streamlines.' Real field service contractors don't talk that way about tools — they talk about what the tool lets them stop doing. The off-brand line isn't wrong because it sounds corporate; it's wrong because the subject is backwards. Let's extract from the founder copy and the blog post. Anything else from his hand I should read before we start naming principles?"

> Identifies the root structural difference (tool-as-subject vs. human-as-subject); sets up the extraction phase with the correct source material

## PHASE 2: Tone principles

### User pushes back on a principle that doesn't fit

**User:** "You said 'short sentences, declarative, no setup.' But our About page is longer — it tells the origin story and it converted really well. I don't want to lose the ability to go long."

**Advisor:** "The principle isn't 'never go long' — it's 'earn the length.' Your origin story converts because it's a story, not a feature list. It has tension, a turning point, and a payoff. That structure earns the length. What kills you is setup on feature pages — three sentences explaining why a problem exists before you've said anything about how you solve it. The principle is: 'Open on action or outcome; earn the setup if you need it.' Does that hold across the samples you've shared? I want to check it against the email copy and the blog post before we confirm it."

> Refines an overly blunt principle after user pushback; distinguishes between length bias and narrative discipline; models verifying principles against samples

### User surfaces a principle the advisor missed

**User:** "One thing I've noticed — our best copy always speaks to contractors, not to business owners. Even when the buyer is an owner, we talk to the part of them that's still on the truck."

**Advisor:** "That's a real principle, and I missed it because I was looking at surface-level features. What you're describing is a register choice: your voice addresses the practitioner identity even when the economic buyer is also a manager. 'On the truck' is useful shorthand internally. The principle I'd write: 'Address the person who does the work, not the title they hold.' Implication: don't write 'for your team' — write 'for you and your techs.' Don't write 'optimize dispatch operations' — write 'stop texting your techs to find out where they are.' Does that match the instinct?"

> Accepts a user-generated principle rather than defending the initial list; translates the insight into a writeable rule; offers a concrete application

## PHASE 3: Register table

### User adds a missing content type

**User:** "You've got homepage hero, feature pages, proof, objection handling, about, and footer. But we have a job-status notification product — texts and emails sent to homeowners. That's a completely different register from everything else."

**Advisor:** "Add it. Customer-facing notifications are often the biggest voice problem in field service — they're either written like legal disclaimers or like consumer apps, and neither matches how a contractor actually communicates with a homeowner. For that row: formality is conversational but professional (you're representing the contractor, not their buddy), intensity is low-key (it's status, not sales), length bias is short (2–4 sentences max — homeowners don't read), person is third (the tech is the subject, not the brand). Flag that this row needs its own approval process — the homeowner's experience of the voice is the contractor's reputation, not yours."

> Accepts a content-type addition the user raised; fills in the register cells with reasoning; flags a process implication the user probably hadn't thought through

### User challenges the register for objection handling

**User:** "You put objection handling as 'direct / assertive.' Our team has been taught to be softer there — acknowledge the concern, then redirect. Is assertive right?"

**Advisor:** "There's a difference between assertive and aggressive. 'Assertive' in the register table means: don't apologize for your product's design choices. Don't write 'we understand this may feel like an adjustment' — that reads as insecurity, not empathy. Acknowledge the concern in one sentence, then state your position without hedging. The example: 'Some teams find the dispatch screen takes a week to learn. Most say by week two it's faster than what they had.' That's assertive — you're naming the real friction, not minimizing it, and you're making a claim about outcome without hedging. Does that match what your best salespeople do on calls?"

> Defends the cell value while redefining the term more precisely; uses a concrete example to show the difference between assertive and aggressive

## PHASE 4: Dos and don'ts

### User identifies a don't the advisor missed

**User:** "We have an internal rule: no exclamation points. Our industry uses them to signal enthusiasm but it reads as fake to our buyers. They're skeptical people."

**Advisor:** "Add it, and the reason matters for the guide: 'Contractors are trained to distrust sales energy. An exclamation point reads as a contractor supply-house ad, not a software recommendation. Reserve emphasis for specificity, not punctuation.' This is a category-specific rule — what signals enthusiasm in consumer apps signals desperation in B2B trades. The do side: 'Use specificity to carry weight. Instead of "dispatch faster!" write "dispatch in under 30 seconds."' That's the same energy without the punctuation that undercuts you."

> Accepts the user-generated don't; writes the rationale in terms of the buyer's specific psychology; provides the 'do' reframe

### Advisor surfaces a pattern from the samples

**Advisor:** "Here's one from the blog post your co-founder wrote that I want to turn into a rule. He wrote: 'You don't need a 47-step onboarding. You need to dispatch your first job today.' That sentence does three things: it identifies a real frustration, names the bad alternative with a specific detail (47 steps), and replaces it with the buyer's actual goal (dispatch a job). The pattern: 'Lead with the problem your buyer already knows they have; name the bad alternative specifically; state your outcome.' The failure mode on your current site is that you do the third step without the first two. Does this hold as a general rule?"

**User:** "Yes — but 47 steps was real. We counted ServiceTitan's onboarding. Can we just say that?"

**Advisor:** "Use 'a competitor's 47-step onboarding' rather than the name — lets the reader fill it in, and you're not exposed. If legal has cleared you to name them in direct comparisons, that's a separate conversation. The rule stands regardless of how you handle the attribution."

> Models extraction of a do/don't pattern directly from a user's sample; handles the user's competitive naming question as a downstream decision, not a blocker

## PHASE 5: Banned phrases

### User defends a phrase on the initial banned list

**User:** "You've got 'seamless' on the banned list. We actually use it in one specific way: 'seamless integration with QuickBooks.' That phrase tests really well with our buyers."

**Advisor:** "The exception is real: 'seamless integration' attached to a specific named integration is different from 'seamless experience' floating unanchored. The reason 'seamless' ends up on banned lists is that it's usually used to promise a feeling without evidence. 'Seamless integration with QuickBooks' is a specific claim — either it is or it isn't. Keep it, but add a note: 'Acceptable only when attached to a named integration. Never standalone.' That way the rule is precise enough to prevent drift without banning a phrase that's doing real work."

> Models the process for keeping a phrase with a specific acceptable scope rather than blanket banning; writes the nuanced note that prevents future misuse

### User adds an org-specific banned phrase

**User:** "We can't say 'transformative' or any variant. We tried it once in a press release and our co-founder's old crew made fun of us for it. Nobody in this industry talks that way."

**Advisor:** "'Transformative' goes on the list with that exact reason: 'sounds like a VC pitch, not a field service business — your buyers will read it as out of touch.' This is the best kind of banned-phrase entry: not abstract, but culturally grounded. The test for banned phrases in your industry: 'Would a 20-year plumber say this out loud?' If no, it doesn't belong in copy written to that plumber's identity. Other candidates from the same test: 'unlock the potential of,' 'reimagine your workflow,' 'future-proof.' Do any of those appear in your current site copy?"

> Accepts the user's banned phrase with their exact reasoning as the rationale entry; introduces a practical test for future additions that's calibrated to the specific buyer identity

## PHASE 6: Banned terms + glossary

### User clarifies their internal terminology

**User:** "We use 'techs,' not 'technicians.' And 'jobs,' not 'work orders.' Always. Our customers say that and we say that."

**Advisor:** "Both are clean: 'technicians' → 'techs' (reason: industry vernacular — your buyers say 'my techs,' not 'my technicians') and 'work orders' → 'jobs' (reason: 'work order' is the paperwork; 'job' is the human reality of what gets done). These go in the banned-terms table. Add to the glossary: **Job** — the unit of work a tech performs for one customer at one site, including dispatch, execution, and closeout. This matters because your product measures jobs, not work orders, and the distinction affects how metrics are reported."

> Accepts terminology corrections without interrogating them; adds a glossary entry that explains why the definition matters at the product level, not just the vocabulary level

### User surfaces a term competitors have poisoned

**User:** "'Field service management' — we're debating whether to even use it. Every competitor uses it and it's become meaningless. But buyers search for it."

**Advisor:** "This is the SEO-vs-voice tension and there's no perfect answer. Here's how to handle it: 'field service management' stays in the banned-terms table as 'use sparingly — SEO only, never in hero copy.' The glossary entry explains your position: 'We use the phrase where buyers are searching for it. We don't use it to describe ourselves because it describes the category at its most generic — what we do is run the jobs that field service management software usually just tracks.' That gives writers a rule they can apply: yes in metadata and comparison pages, no in positioning copy. Does that match how you'd want it to work?"

> Navigates the SEO-vs-voice conflict with a scoped usage rule rather than a binary decision; frames the glossary entry as a positioning statement, not just a definition

## PHASE 7: Competitor naming

### User is uncertain about their competitive posture

**User:** "We win a lot of deals against ServiceTitan and Jobber. Our salespeople name them constantly. But I'm nervous about putting that in writing."

**Advisor:** "The nervousness is worth naming: you're worried that naming them on the website elevates them or invites a legal or PR response. The question is whether your buyers already know the alternatives exist. In field service software, any contractor evaluating you has already Googled 'ServiceTitan vs. Jobber vs. Fieldwise.' The comparison exists — you're not creating it. The risk of not naming them is that you look evasive to a buyer who knows the market. My recommendation: name-by-category on the website ('built for small crews, not enterprise contractors') and name directly in sales decks and battle cards where the buyer has already named them first. Does your sales team hear both names in the first call?"

> Addresses the legal/PR nervousness directly; uses the buyer-knowledge test to frame the naming posture; distinguishes between public copy and sales material

### Advisor drafts the competitor naming rule

**Advisor:** "Based on what you've told me:

**Posture:** Conditional — name by category on public surfaces, name directly in sales materials.

**Rule:** On the website and in demand-gen content, reference competitor categories rather than company names: 'built for small crews, not the enterprise contractors ServiceTitan targets' is acceptable; 'better than ServiceTitan' is not. In sales decks, email sequences, and battle cards, name competitors directly when the buyer has already introduced them — your reps aren't raising the comparison, they're responding to it. Never pre-emptively compare in first-touch content.

**Rationale:** Your buyers are sophisticated enough to know the alternatives; the comparison is already in the room. Naming by category in public copy lets you make the contrast without the legal exposure or the positioning risk of seeming to define yourself against a larger competitor.

Does this match how you'd want it handled?"

**User:** "Yes — exactly. Can we add: never say 'unlike ServiceTitan' — that construction sounds defensive."

**Advisor:** "Add it: 'Avoid "unlike [competitor]" constructions — they make the competitor the reference point and position Fieldwise as the alternative rather than the category leader.' Rewrite: 'Built for crews that can't afford a six-month onboarding' does the same work without the defensive frame."

> Produces the full posture-rule-rationale structure; accepts and improves the user's editing note in real time

## PHASE 8: File assembly

### Standalone mode hand-off

**Advisor:** "The `brand/language/voice.md` file is written. Three sections worth a second look before you distribute it:

1. **Dos and don'ts, pair 4** — the 'specificity over enthusiasm' rule. This is the right principle but the example sentence needs updating once you have a real dispatch-time stat from your customers.

2. **Glossary, 'Job' definition** — I wrote this from the conversation. Have your co-founder read it; he'll know if the edge cases (multi-day jobs, warranty callbacks) need to be addressed in the definition.

3. **Competitor naming rule** — the 'Conditional' posture is right for now. Revisit if you ever run a direct comparison campaign — you'll want a tighter rule for that surface.

Everything else is stable. The tone principles and banned phrases are the core — if writers internalize those two sections, the rest is refinement."

**User:** "Should we share this with our agency? They write our ads."

**Advisor:** "Yes, but with a cover note. Agencies read voice guides looking for what they can't do. Lead with the tone principles and banned phrases — those are the actionable constraints. Tell them the dos and don'ts are the reference for when they're uncertain; the glossary and competitor naming rules are the guardrails they need to check before anything goes public. The register table is for your internal writers who need to calibrate across surfaces — agencies won't use it unless you walk them through it."

> Models the post-assembly conversation about distribution and usage; distinguishes how different audiences will use different sections of the voice document
