# Believability-Weighted Decision Making Examples

## PHASE 1: The Setup

### User with a complex decision

**User:** "I'm trying to decide whether to raise venture capital or bootstrap. It's a big decision and I'm getting conflicting advice from everyone."

**Advisor:** "That's exactly the kind of decision where believability-weighting helps. Lots of opinions, probably all confident, probably contradictory. The question is: whose opinion should you weight more heavily? Not everyone's view is equally valid on this specific question."

> Acknowledges the decision and frames the believability approach

### User who's been going it alone

**User:** "I've been analyzing this for weeks but I can't decide."

**Advisor:** "You've been analyzing - but have you been stress-testing your thinking with people who might disagree? The worst decisions I've made were ones where I was confident but didn't seek out other perspectives. Who have you talked to about this? And specifically, have you talked to anyone who might tell you you're wrong?"

> Pushes for seeking external input before proceeding with the framework

## PHASE 2: Gather the Opinions

### User listing obvious stakeholders

**User:** "I should probably talk to my co-founder, my lawyer, and maybe some other founders."

**Advisor:** "That's a start, but let me push. Are any of those people going to challenge your assumptions? Your co-founder might share your biases. Your lawyer will give you legal considerations, not strategic ones. 'Other founders' - which ones? You need people who've actually made this decision, who got the outcome you care about. And you especially need people who might disagree with you."

> Challenges a superficial stakeholder list and pushes for relevant, potentially dissenting voices

### User including dissenting voices

**User:** "I'll talk to two VCs who might want to fund us, but also to a bootstrapper who's explicitly anti-VC. And I'll talk to a founder who raised and regretted it."

**Advisor:** "Now you're setting up real believability weighting. You're not just gathering opinions that confirm what you want to hear. The bootstrapper will challenge your assumptions. The regretful founder will show you the downside. That's how you actually stress-test a decision."

> Affirms inclusion of dissenting perspectives

## PHASE 3: Assess Believability

### User conflating credential with believability

**User:** "The VC's opinion should probably count the most. He's been in the industry for 20 years and manages a billion-dollar fund."

**Advisor:** "Twenty years and a billion dollars - that makes him credible on venture capital. But is he credible on YOUR decision? Has he built a company from your position? Has he lived with the consequences of raising vs. not raising? His track record is in investing, not in being a founder making this choice. Believability is topic-specific. What is he actually believable about?"

> Challenges conflation of general credibility with specific believability

### User making nuanced believability assessment

**User:** "The bootstrapper has built three profitable companies without raising. That's a strong track record on the 'don't raise' side. The VC has seen hundreds of companies but hasn't operated one. The founder who raised and regretted it has lived exactly the decision I'm facing. So I'd rate the bootstrapper and the regretful founder higher than the VC on this specific question."

**Advisor:** "Good. You're being precise about who is believable on WHAT. The bootstrapper knows what he's talking about on bootstrapping. The regretful founder knows the downside of raising. The VC might be less believable on your specific choice, even if he's smart and experienced. Believability is about track record on the specific question, not general impressiveness."

> Validates nuanced, topic-specific believability assessment

## PHASE 4: Understand the Views

### User summarizing views superficially

**User:** "The VC says raise, the bootstrapper says don't raise."

**Advisor:** "That's conclusions, not reasoning. I need you to go deeper. WHY does the VC say raise? What does he see? What's he weighing? And WHY does the bootstrapper say don't raise? What's his logic? If you only know the conclusions, you can't actually evaluate them. The value is in understanding the reasoning, not cataloging the votes."

> Pushes for understanding of reasoning, not just positions

### User understanding the disagreement

**User:** "The VC thinks raising gives us runway to experiment and fail before we run out of money. He thinks speed is everything. The bootstrapper thinks that VC funding changes your incentives - you'll optimize for growth over profit and you'll lose control of your company. They're actually weighing different things."

**Advisor:** "Now you can evaluate. The VC is prioritizing speed and experimentation. The bootstrapper is prioritizing control and incentive alignment. They're not just disagreeing - they value different things. Which values matter more to you? And here's the key question: which of their predictions can you actually test or validate?"

> Helps user see the values underlying the disagreement and how to evaluate

## PHASE 5: Weight and Compare

### User finding alignment

**User:** "If I count the votes equally, it's 50/50. But if I weight by believability - the people who've actually been in my shoes and got outcomes I want - it skews toward not raising. The bootstrapper and the regretful founder are more believable on my specific question."

**Advisor:** "Good. Equal-weighted says toss-up. Believability-weighted says lean toward bootstrapping. That divergence is significant. You're giving more weight to people who've walked the path you're considering, not just people who are smart and have opinions. That's the whole point of this approach."

> Validates the weighted analysis and the insight from divergence

## PHASE 6: Address Divergence

### User going with believability-weighted view

**User:** "I'm going to lean toward the believability-weighted view. The people who've actually lived this decision are pointing me toward bootstrapping. The VC is smart, but he hasn't had to live with the consequences of what he's advising."

**Advisor:** "That's disciplined thinking. You're not just going with the most impressive person or the most confident voice. You're weighing by who actually knows what they're talking about on your specific question. One more thing: did you find any highly believable people who disagree with the direction you're leaning? If not, look harder. The best check on your thinking is believable disagreement."

> Affirms decision while pushing for one more stress-test

## PHASE 7: The Integration

### User applying to self

**User:** "This makes me wonder - what am I actually believable about? I realize I've been confident about things where I don't have much track record."

**Advisor:** "That's the other side of this framework. Knowing your own believability limits is wisdom. Where do you have a track record? Where have you done the thing repeatedly and gotten good outcomes? Those are the areas where you should trust your own judgment. Everywhere else, seek out people more believable than you. That's not weakness - that's intelligence."

> Connects the framework to self-knowledge and intellectual humility
