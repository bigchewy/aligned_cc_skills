# Movement System Impairment Examples

## PHASE 1: Establish the Problem

### User with common back pain

**User:** "I have low back pain that gets worse when I sit at my desk all day."

**Advisor:** "Back pain that worsens with sitting suggests flexion susceptibility - your spine moves into flexion too easily. But let me be clear: the pain is in your back, but the PROBLEM is probably not your back. Your back is moving too much because something else - likely your hips - isn't moving enough. Let's test which pattern you have."

> Immediately reframes from symptom to pattern

### User who has tried everything

**User:** "I've stretched, done yoga, seen chiropractors. Nothing helps my hip pain."

**Advisor:** "That's not surprising. Stretching addresses tissue length, but most movement dysfunction is motor control, not tissue length. You can stretch for years without changing how you actually move during daily activities. Let me ask: when do you feel the hip pain - with specific movements, or all the time?"

> Challenges stretching paradigm

### User focused on flexibility

**User:** "My hamstrings are super tight. I've been stretching them for months."

**Advisor:** "Let me challenge that. What makes you say they're tight? Can you touch your toes? If not, let's test whether it's actually hamstring length or whether your spine is flexing too much, making your hamstrings FEEL tight because they're constantly pulling against an already-flexed spine. True hamstring tightness is rarer than people think."

> Questions the diagnosis before accepting the treatment

## PHASE 2: Identify the Pattern

### Clear flexion pattern

**User:** "Definitely worse with sitting and bending forward. Standing feels better."

**Advisor:** "Classic flexion syndrome. Your spine habitually moves into flexion - when you sit, when you bend, when you reach. Each of those movements adds flexion stress to your lumbar discs. The pain is your tissue telling you it's had enough repeated flexion. Let's test specifically where your movement is breaking down."

> Confirms pattern and explains mechanism

### Clear extension pattern

**User:** "It hurts more when I stand for long periods or arch my back. Sitting actually feels better."

**Advisor:** "That's extension syndrome - opposite of the flexion pattern. Your spine habitually extends, arches. This might be because your hip flexors are stiff (pulling your pelvis forward), or because your abdominals aren't controlling extension. Let's test to see what's driving it."

> Identifies pattern and suggests mechanisms

### Mixed or unclear presentation

**User:** "It seems to hurt with both bending forward AND standing up. I'm not sure."

**Advisor:** "Combined patterns are common. One possibility: you have a flexion pattern that you're compensating for by extending when you stand. Let's test both - we'll see which direction shows more dysfunction. Sometimes one is primary and the other is the compensation."

> Acknowledges complexity, proposes testing

## PHASE 3: Test the Hypothesis

### User performs flexion tests

**User:** "When I bend forward, my back rounds immediately. And in the knee-to-chest test, my lower back definitely flattens and the other leg lifts a little."

**Advisor:** "Perfect data. Your spine participates in hip movements - it flexes when your hips should flex. This means every time you bend to tie your shoes, pick something up, or sit down, your lumbar spine is moving. Thousands of times a day. That's the source of your tissue stress. The solution isn't stretching - it's learning to hinge at your hips while your spine stays neutral."

> Interprets test results and explains mechanism

### User finds extension tests positive

**User:** "When I lie face down, there's a big gap under my low back. And when I lift my leg, my back definitely arches first."

**Advisor:** "Your spine extends before your hip extends. Every time you walk, stand up from a chair, or reach behind you, your lumbar spine is arching. The gap under your back when lying down shows your tissues have adapted to extension - they're at rest in that position. We need to teach your hip to extend while your spine stays neutral."

> Uses test findings to confirm pattern

### User's tests are inconclusive

**User:** "I'm not really sure what I'm seeing. The tests feel similar on both patterns."

**Advisor:** "That's okay - this is why clinicians spend years learning assessment. Let me ask a functional question: what single activity makes your pain worst? We'll reverse-engineer from there. The activity that causes the most pain reveals the directional susceptibility."

> Adapts when standard tests are unclear

### User finds rotation asymmetry

**User:** "Wow, I can rotate way more to the left than the right. And my pelvis drops a lot when I stand on my right leg."

**Advisor:** "There's your rotation pattern. Your spine is compensating for restricted right hip rotation, and your pelvis instability on the right confirms it. Every time you walk, reach, or twist, your lumbar spine is rotating to make up for what your right hip can't do. The asymmetry is the pattern - we need to address the restricted side while stabilizing your spine."

> Identifies asymmetry and explains compensatory mechanism

## PHASE 4: Find the Relative Stiffness

### User understands the concept

**User:** "So you're saying my back moves too much because my hips don't move enough?"

**Advisor:** "Exactly. Movement follows the path of least resistance. If your hips are stiffer than your lumbar spine, movement goes to the spine. But here's the key insight: the solution is NOT to stretch your hips until they're more mobile than your spine. The solution is to learn to DIRECT movement to your hips while STABILIZING your spine. That's motor control, not mobility."

> Confirms understanding and distinguishes from stretching

### User wants to stretch more

**User:** "So should I stretch my hips more?"

**Advisor:** "That's the common misconception. Stretching increases available range, but it doesn't change where movement GOES during function. You could have perfect hip mobility and still move from your spine if that's your habitual pattern. You need to change the PATTERN - how you recruit, how you coordinate. That happens through movement re-education during daily activities, not through stretching sessions."

> Corrects mobility assumption

### User confused by relative stiffness

**User:** "I don't understand. If my hips are stiff, why wouldn't I stretch them?"

**Advisor:** "Here's the distinction: your hips are stiffer RELATIVE to your spine, so movement goes to your spine. But the solution isn't making your hips looser than your spine - that's a mobility arms race. The solution is learning to DIRECT movement to your hips while KEEPING your spine stable. It's not about tissue length. It's about where your nervous system sends the movement. Motor control, not mobility."

> Explains relative stiffness vs. stretching paradigm

### User grasps the concept

**User:** "Oh - so it's not that my spine is weak, it's that it moves too easily compared to my hips."

**Advisor:** "Exactly. Your spine isn't weak - it's doing TOO MUCH work. It's moving when it shouldn't. The path of least resistance runs through your lumbar spine, so that's where movement goes. We're not going to strengthen your spine - we're going to redirect movement away from it and toward joints that should be doing the work."

> Affirms correct understanding

## PHASE 5: Movement Correction Strategy

### User ready to implement

**User:** "So how do I actually fix this pattern?"

**Advisor:** "The fix is simple but not easy: you need to change how you move ALL DAY, not just during exercises. Every time you bend to pick something up - that's practice. Every time you sit down - practice. Every time you get out of bed - practice.

For your flexion pattern: hips hinge FIRST, spine stays neutral. When you sit, maintain your lumbar curve. This will feel exhausting at first because you're fighting decades of habit. But within weeks, the new pattern becomes automatic."

> Emphasizes all-day practice, not just exercises

### User concerned about time commitment

**User:** "I don't have time for a bunch of exercises."

**Advisor:** "Good news: this isn't about adding exercise time. It's about changing how you do what you already do. You already bend over dozens of times a day. You already sit for hours. The 'exercise' is doing those things differently. No extra time - just different awareness."

> Reframes as pattern change, not exercise program

### User wants specific exercises

**User:** "Can you give me exercises to strengthen the right muscles?"

**Advisor:** "I can, but let me be direct: exercises won't fix this if your daily movement pattern doesn't change. I've seen people do perfect bird-dogs in the gym and then flex their spine every time they pick up their bag. The exercise is just practice for real life. Do bird-dogs and dead bugs - but ONLY if you can maintain perfect spine position. If you can't control it during an exercise, you won't control it during life."

> Provides exercises but emphasizes daily movement

### User skeptical of "all day" approach

**User:** "This sounds exhausting. I can't think about how I move every second of the day."

**Advisor:** "It feels that way at first because you're fighting a decades-old habit. But here's what happens: within a few weeks, the conscious effort becomes unconscious competence. Right now, your brain automatically moves you the dysfunctional way - that didn't require effort because you practiced it for years. Same thing will happen with the correct pattern. The first month is hard. After that, it's automatic."

> Addresses the sustainability concern

### User with extension pattern

**User:** "Okay, I have the extension pattern. What do I do?"

**Advisor:** "Your spine extends too easily - arches whenever you stand, walk, or reach back. The fix: every time you stand for more than a minute, slightly tuck your pelvis. Not aggressively - just enough to take out the excessive arch. When you walk, think of your pelvis as a bowl of water you don't want to spill forward. Stretch your hip flexors, but maintain neutral spine during the stretch - don't let your back arch to fake more range. And planks, but focus on preventing extension, not just holding position."

> Provides pattern-specific guidance
