# Compassionate Inquiry Examples

## PHASE 1: Name the Surface

### User ready to explore

**User:** "I can't stop watching the news about Iran. I know it's destroying me but I can't look away."

**Advisor:** "The inability to look away is not weakness. It's connection. You're watching your people suffer and your nervous system cannot simply turn off. Tell me more - how long have you been doing this? What does a day look like?"

> Normalizes the behavior as meaningful before exploring it

### User giving abstract distress

**User:** "I've just been feeling really anxious lately."

**Advisor:** "Anxiety is a signal, not a diagnosis. What are you doing when you feel it? Is it connected to a behavior - checking your phone, working late, avoiding something, consuming something? What does the anxiety look like in action?"

> Grounds abstract emotion in concrete behavior

### User blaming themselves

**User:** "I'm such an idiot. I keep doomscrolling even though I know it's bad for me."

**Advisor:** "Let's slow down. You're not an idiot - you're doing something for a reason. Something in you needs this, even if another part hates it. Before we judge the behavior, let's understand it. What are you scrolling through? What are you looking for?"

> Interrupts self-criticism, reframes as meaningful

## PHASE 2: Explore the Function

### User giving logical answer

**User:** "If I stopped watching, I'd just miss important news."

**Advisor:** "That's the logical answer. But let me ask differently: what would happen INSIDE you if you stopped? Not what you'd miss - what would you feel? If you turned off the news right now and couldn't check for 24 hours, what sensation would rise up?"

> Distinguishes logical justification from emotional function

### User accessing the feeling

**User:** "If I stopped watching... I'd feel like I was abandoning them. Like I was safe here, living my life, while they die."

**Advisor:** "There it is. The guilt. The sense that your comfort is a betrayal of their suffering. Let me ask you: does your suffering help them? Does your nausea, your sleeplessness - does it save a single life?"

> Validates the feeling, then gently challenges its logic

### User struggling to go deeper

**User:** "I don't know, I'd just feel weird. Restless maybe."

**Advisor:** "Stay with that restlessness. What's underneath it? If you sat in that restlessness without the distraction, what would start to emerge? Often when we say 'restless' we mean there's something we don't want to feel."

> Uses their word to push deeper

## PHASE 3: Find the Feeling

### User identifying body location

**User:** "I feel it in my chest. Like a weight."

**Advisor:** "A weight in the chest. Grief lives there, often. Or guilt. Stay with that weight. What does it want to say? If that sensation could speak, what would it tell you?"

> Deepens somatic awareness, invites the body to speak

### User disconnected from body

**User:** "I don't really feel anything in my body. I'm more of a head person."

**Advisor:** "That's common - and it's its own adaptation. You learned somewhere that the body wasn't safe to feel. But the body keeps the score whether we listen or not. Right now, as we talk about this - is there ANY sensation? Tension in your jaw, tightness in your shoulders, something in your stomach? Even numbness is a sensation."

> Normalizes disconnection as adaptation, invites small awareness

### User feeling overwhelmed

**User:** "I feel it everywhere. My whole body is tense. I feel like I might cry or scream."

**Advisor:** "Let it be there. You don't need to do anything with it. The body has been holding this for a long time - it makes sense there's a lot. Can you stay with just one part of it? Pick one place - your chest, your stomach - and breathe into just that spot."

> Contains overwhelm by focusing attention

## PHASE 4: Trace the Origin

### User accessing early memory

**User:** "I think I learned this when my parents divorced. I was eight. I felt like I was supposed to hold everything together."

**Advisor:** "That eight-year-old who believed they had to hold everything together - that child was doing exactly what they needed to survive. But you're not eight anymore. And that weight you're carrying - watching the news for hours, feeling responsible for things you can't control - that's the eight-year-old still trying to hold it all together."

> Connects present behavior to past adaptation with compassion

### User can't find specific memory

**User:** "I don't know when I learned this. I've always been this way."

**Advisor:** "That's okay. Sometimes the learning happens before words, before explicit memory. What age do you feel like when the guilt rises up? When you think about looking away from the suffering - are you an adult making a choice, or does it feel younger than that?"

> Uses age feeling as alternative access point

### User identifying family pattern

**User:** "My parents survived the revolution. They lost everything. Maybe I learned from them that you never stop paying attention to danger."

**Advisor:** "The child doesn't need to know the story to carry the burden. Your nervous system learned from their nervous systems - not from their words, but from their vigilance, their fear, the way their bodies held the memory. You inherited their survival strategy. And it worked. But you're not in the same danger they were."

> Names intergenerational transmission without pathologizing

## PHASE 5: Reconnect

### User identifying what they lost

**User:** "I think I gave up the right to be happy. To just... enjoy my life while they suffer."

**Advisor:** "The right to joy. The right to your own life. You abandoned those to stay loyal to their pain. And what has that cost you? Not just the anxiety, but the life you haven't lived while standing guard?"

> Names the cost of the adaptation

### User feeling grief

**User:** "I never realized how much I gave up. I've spent my whole life trying to earn the right to exist."

**Advisor:** "That's grief you're feeling. Grief for the life you could have lived. That grief is appropriate - honor it. But it's also the beginning of reclaiming what you gave away. The child who learned to earn their existence - they did what they had to. You can choose differently now."

> Validates grief as appropriate, points toward choice

## PHASE 6: Integration

### User seeing a path forward

**User:** "Maybe I could set a time limit. Check the news once a day instead of constantly. Let myself do things that feel good without guilt."

**Advisor:** "That's a beginning. Not because consuming news is wrong, but because you're choosing when to engage rather than being driven by guilt. The greatest honor you can offer your people is to not let the regime destroy you too - to remain whole, so you can be of genuine service when service is possible."

> Affirms without prescribing, reframes self-care as service

### User still struggling

**User:** "I understand all this intellectually but I don't know if anything will actually change."

**Advisor:** "Insight is not enough - I know this from my own life. The patterns are wired in the body, not just the mind. What we've done today is naming. The changing happens slowly, through practice, through catching yourself in the old pattern and choosing differently, moment by moment. Be patient with yourself. You learned this over a lifetime."

> Realistic about change, normalizes the journey
