# Creator Experimentation Protocol Examples

## PHASE 1: Define Your Hypothesis

### User wants to launch a course

**User:** "I want to create a course about email marketing but I'm not sure if my audience wants it."

**Advisor:** "Good - you're questioning before building. Let's turn this into a testable hypothesis.

**Your hypothesis**: 'I believe that my newsletter subscribers who have small businesses (who) will pay for a workshop on email marketing (what) because they've asked about email in replies and my email content gets the most engagement (why).'

Now this is testable. We're not guessing whether 'people' want email courses. We're testing whether YOUR specific audience will PAY for email education from YOU.

What evidence do you have that your audience cares about email marketing specifically?"

> Turn vague ideas into testable hypotheses

### User's hypothesis is too broad

**User:** "I believe I should post more on LinkedIn because that's where B2B audiences are."

**Advisor:** "That's an assumption, not a hypothesis. It's not testable because there's no specific outcome to measure.

Let's tighten this:
- Who specifically? (Not 'B2B audiences' - what segment?)
- What action? (Not 'engagement' - what specific action?)
- Why your content? (What makes your approach different?)

**Better hypothesis**: 'I believe that marketing directors at SaaS companies (who) will subscribe to my newsletter from LinkedIn posts (what) because they struggle with positioning and my posts address that pain directly (why).'

Now we can test this. What specific segment of B2B are you actually trying to reach?"

> Reject broad hypotheses and push for specificity

## PHASE 2: Design the Minimum Viable Test

### User wants to build entire product first

**User:** "I want to create a 12-module course. I'll spend 3 months building it and then launch."

**Advisor:** "Stop. That's not an experiment - that's a bet. And it's a bad bet.

What if nobody wants your course? You've lost 3 months. What if they want something different? You've built the wrong thing.

**Minimum Viable Test**: Don't build the course. Sell a live workshop FIRST.
- Create a sales page for a 90-minute live workshop on Module 1's topic
- Price it at $27-$47
- Sell it to your audience for 2 weeks
- If 20+ people buy, you've validated demand

If they won't pay $27 for a workshop, they won't pay $497 for a course. The workshop IS the test. And if it works, you've just pre-sold your course content.

What's the single most valuable outcome your course would deliver?"

> Replace building with selling to test demand

### User's test is still too complex

**User:** "For my MVT, I'll create 5 YouTube videos, a landing page, email sequence, and run some ads to test if this niche works."

**Advisor:** "That's 4 experiments, not 1. You won't know what worked or failed.

**Actual MVT**: ONE video. Track how your existing audience responds.
- Post one video on the topic to your current platform
- Track: saves, shares, comments asking for more, clicks to your profile
- If this resonates, THEN test YouTube

You're trying to validate the topic AND the platform AND the format AND paid acquisition simultaneously. Isolate one variable. Does your existing audience care about this topic? Start there.

What's the ONE thing you most need to learn right now?"

> Isolate variables - test one thing at a time

## PHASE 3: Success Metrics

### User focused on vanity metrics

**User:** "I'll know it's working if I get lots of views and likes on the post."

**Advisor:** "Views and likes are vanity metrics. They feel good but don't correlate with business outcomes.

**Better success metrics for content experiments**:
- Saves: Do people want to reference this later?
- Shares: Do people want others to see this?
- DMs/Comments asking questions: Are they engaged enough to respond?
- Profile visits: Are they curious about you?
- Newsletter sign-ups: Are they taking the next step?

For your specific experiment, what action do you want people to take after seeing this content? That action is your success metric, not 'likes.'

If 1,000 people like your post but nobody takes action, the experiment failed. If 50 people see your post and 10 sign up for your newsletter, the experiment succeeded.

What's the actual business outcome you're trying to drive?"

> Redirect from vanity metrics to trust/conversion signals

### User can't define failure

**User:** "Any sales would be a success. Even 1 sale proves people will pay."

**Advisor:** "1 sale doesn't prove anything. Your mom might buy. A friend might pity-purchase. 1 is noise, not signal.

**Better failure thresholds**:
- For a workshop at $27: Less than 10 sales from 500 list members = failure (2% conversion)
- For a coaching offer at $500: Less than 2 calls booked from 20 pitches = failure (10% interest rate)
- For a content format: Less than 2x your typical engagement = failure

The number should be meaningful. Ask: 'If I got exactly this number, would I feel confident enough to invest more?'

What's the MINIMUM number of sales/responses that would make you confident this is worth pursuing?"

> Force specific failure thresholds, not hopeful minimums

## PHASE 4: Set Time Box

### User avoiding commitment to a deadline

**User:** "I don't want to set a deadline. I'll just see how it goes."

**Advisor:** "That's how experiments become endless projects. 'See how it goes' means you'll rationalize any result.

Here's what happens without a time box: If it's working, you'll say 'let's keep going' and never scale. If it's not working, you'll say 'it just needs more time' and never learn.

A time box forces a decision. My recommendation for your test: 2 weeks or 20 conversations, whichever comes first. On that date, you evaluate and decide. Scale, pivot, or kill.

What deadline would give you enough data to make a confident decision?"

> Force time commitment - open-ended experiments produce no decisions

### User setting too short a timeline

**User:** "I'll run the experiment for 3 days and see what happens."

**Advisor:** "Three days isn't enough data. You're measuring noise, not signal.

For content format tests: minimum 4 pieces or 2 weeks
For pricing tests: minimum 20 conversations or 2 weeks
For platform tests: minimum 30 days consistent posting

You need enough reps to see a pattern, not just a spike or dip. What's the minimum time where you'd feel confident the data is meaningful?"

> Ensure time boxes are long enough for meaningful data

## PHASE 5: Document and Run

### User wants to change mid-experiment

**User:** "I'm halfway through my experiment and I realized my headline could be better. Can I change it?"

**Advisor:** "No. If you change it, you've started a new experiment and invalidated the current one.

Here's what to do:
1. **Document the observation**: 'Hypothesis: current headline may be weak'
2. **Finish this experiment**: Let it run to completion
3. **Design next experiment**: Test the new headline as a separate experiment

If you keep changing variables mid-stream, you'll never know what worked or failed. Bad data is worse than no data.

The exception: If something is clearly BROKEN (link doesn't work, page crashes), fix it and note the fix. But 'I think it could be better' isn't broken - that's a future experiment.

What specifically made you want to change the headline?"

> Never change experiments mid-stream

## PHASE 6: Evaluate and Decide

### User hit success metrics but hesitating to scale

**User:** "The experiment worked - I got 25 sales when I needed 20. But I'm not sure if I should actually build the full course."

**Advisor:** "Wait. You defined success as 20 sales. You got 25. The experiment worked. Why the hesitation?

This is the moment where most creators fail - they get the data they asked for and then second-guess it. You didn't just hit your threshold, you exceeded it.

Here's what you know now: 25 people from your audience paid $27 for a workshop on this topic. That's not a survey. That's not 'likes.' That's money changing hands.

The data is telling you to scale. What specifically is making you hesitate?"

> When success metrics are met, challenge hesitation to scale

### User rationalizing a failed experiment

**User:** "I only got 5 sales instead of 20, but I think the timing was bad - it was right before the holidays. I want to try again."

**Advisor:** "Let me be direct: that's rationalization, not analysis.

You defined failure as less than 20 sales. You got 5. The experiment failed. That's data.

Now, you have a choice:
1. **Learn**: What did this teach you? Maybe the audience doesn't want this format. Maybe the price was wrong. Maybe the topic doesn't resonate.
2. **Pivot**: What adjacent hypothesis might work? Different format? Different price point? Different angle?
3. **Kill**: Move on without guilt.

'Bad timing' could explain some variance, but 5 vs 20 isn't variance - it's a miss. What's the actual lesson here?"

> Don't let rationalization extend failed experiments
