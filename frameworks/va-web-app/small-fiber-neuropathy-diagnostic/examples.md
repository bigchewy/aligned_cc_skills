# Small Fiber Neuropathy Diagnostic Examples

## PHASE 1: Symptom Assessment

### User with classic SFN symptoms

**User:** "I have burning pain in my feet, especially at night. It started about two years ago and has slowly gotten worse. Sometimes it feels like I'm walking on hot coals."

**Advisor:** "That's a classic small fiber symptom - burning pain in the feet, worse at night, progressive over time. The 'hot coals' description is textbook. You've met the first criterion. Now let's move to the exam findings."

> Recognizes and validates classic presentation before moving on

### User with vague symptoms

**User:** "My feet just feel weird. Kind of tingly sometimes."

**Advisor:** "I need more precision. 'Weird' and 'tingly' could be many things. Is it burning? Sharp? Electric? Does light touch feel painful? Is it constant or intermittent? Help me understand exactly what sensation you're experiencing."

> Pushes for specificity to properly categorize symptoms

### User describing autonomic symptoms only

**User:** "I don't have pain, but I sweat way less than I used to. My feet are always dry and cracked."

**Advisor:** "Reduced sweating - anhidrosis - is an autonomic small fiber symptom. The small fibers innervate sweat glands. But for diagnosis, we typically look for sensory symptoms too: pain, burning, temperature sensitivity. Do you have any sensory complaints, even mild ones?"

> Acknowledges autonomic SFN but explains diagnostic criteria require sensory involvement

## PHASE 2: Sensory Exam Findings

### User with documented exam findings

**User:** "My neurologist found reduced pinprick sensation to mid-calf bilaterally. Temperature sensation was also reduced in the same distribution."

**Advisor:** "That's exactly what we need - documented sensory signs in a length-dependent pattern. Feet and legs, pinprick and temperature both affected. You've met the second criterion. Now for the objective testing."

> Confirms findings meet diagnostic criteria

### User who hasn't had proper exam

**User:** "My doctor just did reflexes and said they were fine."

**Advisor:** "Reflexes test large fibers, not small fibers. You need pinprick testing - can you feel sharp versus dull? And temperature testing - hot versus cold discrimination. These are simple bedside tests any neurologist can do. Without documented small fiber sensory signs, we can't fulfill the criteria."

> Distinguishes large fiber from small fiber exam and redirects

### User with normal exam but symptoms

**User:** "The neurologist said my exam was completely normal, but I definitely feel the burning."

**Advisor:** "This is common. Symptoms can precede exam findings. The question is: do you have reduced sensation, or do you have increased sensation (allodynia)? Sometimes patients have normal pinprick detection but abnormal pain from light touch. Did they test for allodynia - pain from something that shouldn't hurt, like a cotton swab?"

> Explores full range of small fiber signs when initial exam is negative

## PHASE 3: The Gold Standard Test - Skin Biopsy

### User with positive biopsy

**User:** "My skin biopsy showed IENFD of 2.1 fibers/mm at the distal leg. The lab said normal for my age and gender is above 7.8."

**Advisor:** "Definitive. Your IENFD is 27% of the lower limit of normal - that's a significant reduction. Combined with symptoms and sensory signs, you meet criteria for definite SFN. The test did its job. Now we need to find the cause."

> Interprets result clearly and confirms diagnostic criteria met

### User with borderline biopsy

**User:** "My IENFD was 5.2 and the cutoff is 5.0. So barely abnormal."

**Advisor:** "Borderline results are the hardest. You're at the 5th percentile, technically abnormal, but barely. A few questions: What lab processed it? Different labs have different normative data. Was the biopsy site exactly 10 cm above the lateral malleolus? And does this correlate with your clinical picture? A borderline number with severe symptoms needs explanation."

> Applies appropriate skepticism to borderline results

### User who hasn't had biopsy

**User:** "My doctor said a skin biopsy isn't necessary because my symptoms are so clear."

**Advisor:** "I disagree with that approach. Symptoms are subjective. The skin biopsy gives us objective evidence - 94% sensitive, 97% specific. Without it, you have 'probable' SFN at best. If this diagnosis will guide treatment or determine prognosis, you want definite confirmation. It's a minor procedure."

> Advocates for diagnostic rigor

## PHASE 4: Rule Out Large Fiber Involvement

### User with normal NCS

**User:** "My nerve conduction studies were completely normal - sensory and motor."

**Advisor:** "Perfect. That's what we need for pure SFN. NCS tests large myelinated fibers. If those are normal and the skin biopsy shows reduced small fiber density, you have isolated small fiber neuropathy. The pathology is selective to the small fibers."

> Confirms large fiber integrity supports SFN diagnosis

### User with abnormal NCS

**User:** "My NCS showed mild slowing in the sural nerve."

**Advisor:** "That changes things. If large fibers are affected, this isn't pure SFN - it's a mixed sensory neuropathy. The diagnostic criteria for idiopathic SFN require normal NCS. A mixed neuropathy has a different differential diagnosis - we'd look harder at diabetic neuropathy, hereditary neuropathies, or inflammatory conditions."

> Recognizes when criteria for pure SFN aren't met

## PHASE 5: Pattern Recognition

### User with length-dependent pattern

**User:** "It started in my toes, then the whole foot, now it's creeping up my calves. My hands are starting to feel it too."

**Advisor:** "Classic length-dependent pattern - dying-back neuropathy. The longest nerves fail first: toes, then feet, then calves. When legs reach mid-thigh level, hands typically start. This pattern strongly suggests a metabolic cause. Glucose metabolism is the first thing to check."

> Identifies pattern and predicts likely etiology

### User with non-length-dependent pattern

**User:** "It's weird - I have patches on my trunk, my face tingles sometimes, and my left arm is worse than my right."

**Advisor:** "That's non-length-dependent SFN - patchy, asymmetric, involving proximal areas. This pattern raises different possibilities: autoimmune small fiber neuropathy, Sjogren's syndrome, sarcoidosis. The workup needs to be broader. Have you had autoimmune markers checked?"

> Recognizes atypical pattern requires different approach

## PHASE 6: Etiology Workup

### User with positive OGTT

**User:** "My fasting glucose was 95, but on the OGTT my 2-hour was 165."

**Advisor:** "There it is. A 2-hour glucose of 165 is impaired glucose tolerance - pre-diabetes. This is the single most common cause of idiopathic SFN. Your fasting glucose looked fine, which is why the OGTT matters. Many cases of 'idiopathic' SFN become 'pre-diabetic SFN' when you do the right test."

> Identifies common missed diagnosis

### User with negative standard workup

**User:** "Everything came back normal - glucose, B12, thyroid, ANA, all normal."

**Advisor:** "That's actually common - about 50% of SFN remains idiopathic even after full workup. But let me ask: was it a full OGTT or just fasting glucose? Did they check B6 level - too much B6 causes neuropathy? SPEP for monoclonal protein? The standard workup misses things if it's not complete."

> Probes for incomplete workup before accepting idiopathic diagnosis

## PHASE 7: SFN and POTS Connection

### User with concurrent onset

**User:** "My burning feet and my POTS symptoms started within months of each other, right after a viral infection."

**Advisor:** "That temporal connection is significant. Post-viral SFN with autonomic involvement - your immune system may have attacked the small fibers that regulate both sensation AND blood vessel constriction. This is the 'neuropathic POTS' phenotype. The SFN isn't separate from your POTS - it may be the mechanism causing it."

> Connects SFN to POTS pathophysiology

### User with SFN predating POTS

**User:** "I've had the neuropathy pain for years, but the POTS just started recently."

**Advisor:** "Interesting progression. The SFN came first, but autonomic involvement may have been subclinical. QSART testing might show sudomotor abnormalities that precede hemodynamic symptoms. The question is: did something trigger autonomic decompensation, or is this just progression of the same underlying process?"

> Considers natural history and subclinical progression

## PHASE 8: Red Flags and Next Steps

### User with rapid progression

**User:** "This all happened in about 6 weeks. I went from normal to severe burning pain everywhere."

**Advisor:** "Six weeks is a red flag. Most SFN is indolent - progresses over months to years. Rapid onset suggests an inflammatory or autoimmune cause. You need urgent autoimmune workup: ANA, anti-neuronal antibodies, ESR, CRP. Consider ganglionopathy or autoimmune autonomic ganglionopathy. This may be treatable if we catch it."

> Recognizes urgency of rapid progression

### User ready for action plan

**User:** "So what should I do next? I have symptoms and some exam findings but no biopsy yet."

**Advisor:** "Here's the sequence: First, get the skin biopsy - that confirms or refutes the diagnosis. Second, if positive, complete the etiology workup with OGTT (not just fasting glucose), B12, TSH, ANA, SPEP. Third, consider autonomic testing if you have POTS symptoms - QSART will show if sudomotor small fibers are affected too. Then we know what we're dealing with."

> Provides clear, prioritized action plan
