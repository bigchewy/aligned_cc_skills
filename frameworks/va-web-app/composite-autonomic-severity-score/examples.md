# Composite Autonomic Severity Score (CASS) Examples

## PHASE 1: Gather Test Results

### User with comprehensive testing

**User:** "I had full autonomic testing at Cleveland Clinic. HRV to deep breathing was 8 bpm (normal >10), Valsalva ratio 1.4 (normal >1.5). Standing BP dropped from 125/80 to 100/70 at 3 minutes. QSART was reduced at the foot and distal leg sites."

**Advisor:** "Good data. Three domains covered. HRV and Valsalva for cardiovagal, orthostatic BP for adrenergic, QSART for sudomotor. Cleveland Clinic knows the methodology - I trust these results. Let's score each component. Starting with cardiovagal."

> Validates testing source and confirms all three domains are covered

### User with partial testing

**User:** "My cardiologist did a tilt table. HR went from 75 to 115 at 5 minutes. Blood pressure didn't change much. That's all they measured."

**Advisor:** "That's orthostatic assessment only - helpful but incomplete. We have adrenergic data (BP stable) and can infer HR response, but no cardiovagal or sudomotor testing. CASS requires all three components for proper scoring. Without HRV to deep breathing, Valsalva, and sweat testing, I can give you partial information. Should we work with what you have, or do you have access to more complete testing?"

> Notes testing limitations while offering to work with available data

### User with autonomic testing results document

**User:** "I have the autonomic lab report. Cardiovagal: mild abnormality. Adrenergic: normal. Sudomotor: moderate abnormality at distal sites."

**Advisor:** "That's the summary - useful. But I want the numbers behind those labels. Different labs use different cutoffs. 'Mild abnormality' might be borderline at one center and clearly abnormal at another. Can you share the actual values? HRV in bpm, Valsalva ratio, specific QSART volumes at each site?"

> Pushes for actual numbers rather than summary interpretations

## PHASE 2: Score the Cardiovagal Component

### Clear cardiovagal deficit

**User:** "HRV to deep breathing was 4 bpm. Normal range says >10. Valsalva ratio was 1.2, normal >1.5."

**Advisor:** "Cardiovagal function is clearly impaired. HRV of 4 bpm is less than half the normal threshold. Valsalva ratio also reduced. This means your vagus nerve isn't modulating heart rate properly - parasympathetic pathway deficit.

**Cardiovagal score: 2 (Moderate)**. Not absent, but clearly reduced. Age matters here - what's your age? Normal values decline with age, and I want to confirm we're using appropriate norms."

> Explains the physiological meaning and confirms age-appropriate norms

### Normal cardiovagal function

**User:** "HRV was 14 bpm, and Valsalva ratio was 1.8."

**Advisor:** "Normal cardiovagal function. HRV of 14 is above threshold, Valsalva ratio of 1.8 is robust. Your parasympathetic pathway is intact - the vagus nerve is modulating heart rate appropriately.

**Cardiovagal score: 0 (Normal)**. This is actually important information. If you have autonomic symptoms, they're not coming from parasympathetic failure."

> Notes that normal findings are diagnostically meaningful

## PHASE 3: Score the Adrenergic Component

### Orthostatic hypotension present

**User:** "Standing BP dropped from 130/85 lying down to 95/60 at 3 minutes. Valsalva showed delayed phase II recovery and absent phase IV overshoot."

**Advisor:** "Clear adrenergic deficit. That's a 35 mmHg systolic drop sustained at 3 minutes - meets orthostatic hypotension criteria. Valsalva findings confirm it: delayed phase II means slow vascular compensation, absent phase IV means no sympathetic overshoot.

**Adrenergic score: 2-3 (Moderate to Severe)**. Your sympathetic vasoconstrictor pathway isn't maintaining vascular tone. This is the mechanism behind your lightheadedness on standing."

> Connects test findings to symptoms

### Normal BP but excessive tachycardia

**User:** "BP stayed stable when I stood - 120/80 both positions. But my HR went from 70 to 110."

**Advisor:** "Adrenergically, you're compensating. BP maintenance with significant tachycardia means your sympathetic system is working overtime to maintain pressure. This is POTS pattern, not orthostatic hypotension.

**Adrenergic score: 0-1 (Normal to Mild)**. The tachycardia isn't an adrenergic failure - it's compensation. POTS criteria are separate from CASS severity grading. You can have significant POTS with normal CASS."

> Distinguishes POTS from adrenergic failure

## PHASE 4: Score the Sudomotor Component

### Length-dependent pattern

**User:** "QSART showed 0.2 microliters at the foot (normal >0.8), 0.5 at the distal leg (normal >0.7), and normal at the forearm and proximal leg."

**Advisor:** "Classic length-dependent pattern. Worst at the foot, better proximally, normal in the upper extremities. This is postganglionic small fiber dysfunction - the nerves controlling sweat glands are affected in a dying-back pattern, typical of peripheral neuropathy.

**Sudomotor score: 2 (Moderate)**. The pattern tells us this is peripheral, not central. QSART won't show a brain or spinal cord lesion - it's measuring postganglionic fibers."

> Explains localization implications of the pattern

### Diffuse sudomotor abnormality

**User:** "Sweat testing showed reduced responses everywhere - all four QSART sites abnormal, and TST showed 60% anhidrosis including the trunk."

**Advisor:** "Diffuse sudomotor failure. When TST shows trunk involvement, we're looking at a different pattern than length-dependent neuropathy. Trunk anhidrosis suggests either central involvement or severe, widespread peripheral failure.

**Sudomotor score: 3 (Severe)**. The TST pattern is important - does it spare the face and scalp? That helps distinguish central from peripheral causes."

> Uses TST pattern for localization

## PHASE 5: Calculate Total CASS and Identify Pattern

### Peripheral neuropathy pattern

**User:** "So I have cardiovagal 1, adrenergic 1, sudomotor 2?"

**Advisor:** "**Total CASS: 4/9** - Moderate autonomic dysfunction.

But the pattern matters more than the number. Sudomotor is your predominant deficit with length-dependent distribution. Cardiovagal and adrenergic are mildly involved but not the main story.

This is a **peripheral postganglionic pattern** - suggests small fiber neuropathy as the underlying mechanism. Classic for diabetic neuropathy, idiopathic small fiber neuropathy, or certain autoimmune conditions.

Have you had a skin biopsy for intraepidermal nerve fiber density? That would confirm small fiber involvement."

> Interprets pattern rather than just reporting numbers

### Central pattern

**User:** "Cardiovagal 0, adrenergic 3, sudomotor 2 with trunk involvement on TST."

**Advisor:** "**Total CASS: 5/9** - Moderate autonomic dysfunction.

The pattern here concerns me. Preserved cardiovagal with severe adrenergic deficit and truncal sudomotor involvement - this is a **central/preganglionic pattern**. The trunk anhidrosis is the key finding.

This pattern can be seen in multiple system atrophy or other central autonomic disorders. Do you have any parkinsonian features? Cerebellar signs? Urinary dysfunction early in the course? The clinical context matters for interpretation."

> Recognizes concerning pattern and asks relevant follow-up

## PHASE 6: Clinical Integration

### CASS explains symptoms

**User:** "This makes sense. My main problems are lightheadedness on standing and reduced sweating in my feet."

**Advisor:** "CASS explains your symptoms well. The adrenergic and sudomotor deficits account for lightheadedness on standing and distal anhidrosis.

**Treatment implications**: With moderate CASS, you have residual autonomic function to work with. Salt and fluid loading, compression, countermaneuvers - these harness remaining tone. If that's not enough, midodrine for vasoconstriction support makes physiological sense given your adrenergic deficit.

**Prognosis depends on cause**: Diabetic neuropathy tends to progress slowly with glycemic control. Post-viral may improve. Autoimmune may respond to treatment. What's the suspected etiology?"

> Connects findings to treatment approach

### CASS doesn't explain symptoms

**User:** "My CASS is normal but I still have severe POTS symptoms."

**Advisor:** "That's not a contradiction. POTS patients often have normal CASS. The tachycardia criteria - 30+ bpm increase on standing - are sufficient for POTS diagnosis. CASS grades autonomic failure, not POTS severity.

Normal CASS in POTS actually tells us something: you likely don't have neuropathic POTS with significant denervation. Deconditioning, hypovolemia, or hyperadrenergic mechanisms are more likely driving your symptoms.

This is good news for treatment. Without significant neuropathy, reconditioning protocols like the Levine Protocol can be highly effective. Have you tried structured exercise therapy?"

> Explains why normal CASS doesn't rule out POTS and guides treatment
