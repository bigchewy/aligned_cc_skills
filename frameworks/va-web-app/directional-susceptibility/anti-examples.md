# Directional Susceptibility Anti-Examples

### Treating symptoms without identifying the pattern

**User:** "My back hurts when I sit for long periods."

**Wrong:** "You should take more breaks and stretch your back. Here are some seated stretches you can do at your desk."

**Right:** "Pain with sitting suggests your spine may have developed flexion susceptibility - it moves into flexion more easily than other directions, and sitting reinforces this all day. Before we talk about solutions, let's identify your specific pattern. Tell me what positions make it worse and what makes it better."

> Dr. Sahrmann's DSM framework requires identifying the directional pattern FIRST. Treating symptoms without understanding the susceptibility is treating effects, not causes.

### Focusing on pain location instead of movement direction

**User:** "My left shoulder hurts. What exercises strengthen the left shoulder?"

**Wrong:** "Let's work on strengthening your left rotator cuff and deltoid. Here are some targeted exercises."

**Right:** "The left shoulder is where you feel it, but directional susceptibility tells us the pain location isn't necessarily the problem. Which direction makes the pain worse - internal rotation, external rotation, flexion, or extension? And what activities put your shoulder in that position repeatedly? The pattern predicts the injury."

> Directional susceptibility focuses on DIRECTION of movement stress, not the anatomical location of pain. The susceptible direction is the diagnostic key.

### Prescribing stretching for directional susceptibility

**User:** "My hips are tight and my lower back hurts. Should I stretch my hip flexors?"

**Wrong:** "Yes, tight hip flexors pull your pelvis forward and strain your back. Let's add some hip flexor stretches to your routine."

**Right:** "Hip flexor tightness might contribute to extension susceptibility in your spine, but stretching alone won't change your directional pattern. The question is: when you stand and walk, does your spine go into extension because your hips can't extend? That's motor control, not tissue length. We need to identify your directional susceptibility first, then determine whether hip mobility or movement retraining is the priority."

> Dr. Sahrmann consistently emphasizes that directional susceptibility is a motor control issue, not a tissue length problem. Stretching doesn't change movement patterns.

### Missing the "everywhere" nature of DSM

**User:** "I do yoga three times a week for flexibility. Why do I still have this problem?"

**Wrong:** "Maybe you need different yoga poses, or to hold them longer. Have you tried yin yoga?"

**Right:** "Here's the insight: yoga three times a week is maybe 4 hours. What about the other 110+ waking hours? Directional susceptibility pervades ALL your movement - how you sit, how you bend, how you walk, how you sleep. If you have flexion susceptibility and spend 60 hours a week flexed at a desk, 4 hours of yoga can't overcome that. The intervention has to address daily movement patterns, not just exercise sessions."

> Dr. Sahrmann's key point: DSM affects every movement, all day. Exercise sessions can't compensate for habitual movement patterns.

### Accepting patient's diagnosis of "tightness"

**User:** "I know I'm just really tight. I've always been inflexible."

**Wrong:** "Some people are naturally less flexible. Let's work on a comprehensive stretching program to gradually increase your range."

**Right:** "Let me challenge that assumption. 'Tightness' is a feeling, not a diagnosis. Often what feels tight is actually your nervous system's protective response to directional susceptibility - the muscles are guarding against further movement in a direction that's already stressed.

Can you actually NOT move in a certain direction, or does it just feel restricted? Let's test whether this is true tissue limitation or motor control that we can change."

> Dr. Sahrmann repeatedly challenges the assumption that perceived tightness equals tissue shortness. DSM is about where movement GOES, not tissue length.

### Treating different areas as separate problems

**User:** "I have knee pain AND back pain. They must be two different issues, right?"

**Wrong:** "Let's address them separately. For the knee, we'll do these exercises. For the back, these different exercises."

**Right:** "Probably not separate. Directional susceptibility creates predictable cascades. For example, if your hip has limited extension, your lumbar spine extends to compensate - that's your back pain. And if your hip has limited rotation, your knee rotates to compensate - that's your knee pain.

The common thread is likely hip dysfunction creating directional susceptibility both above and below. Let's assess your hip mobility and see if one pattern explains both symptoms."

> Dr. Sahrmann views the body as a kinetic chain where directional susceptibility in one area creates compensatory susceptibility in others.

### Recommending general exercise instead of pattern-specific intervention

**User:** "What exercises should I do to prevent injury?"

**Wrong:** "A balanced program of strengthening and stretching. Core work, hip mobility, shoulder stability. Here's a general program."

**Right:** "Generic exercise can't address directional susceptibility because the susceptibility is specific. First, what direction does YOUR body move too easily? A person with flexion susceptibility needs completely different intervention than someone with extension susceptibility.

Let me assess your movement patterns first. Then I can prescribe exercises that specifically counter YOUR directional susceptibility, not generic 'prevention' exercises."

> Dr. Sahrmann's approach requires pattern-specific intervention. General exercise may even reinforce existing susceptibility.

### Focusing on strength when the issue is motor control

**User:** "I have weak glutes. That's probably why my back hurts - I need to strengthen them."

**Wrong:** "You're right, glute weakness is a common cause of back pain. Let's do bridges, clamshells, and hip thrusts to strengthen your glutes."

**Right:** "Your glutes might test strong but not activate during functional movement. That's not weakness - it's directional susceptibility. Your spine has learned to move INSTEAD of your hips.

We could strengthen your glutes all day, but if your motor pattern still sends movement to your spine during bending, lifting, and walking, the strong glutes won't help. First, let's see if you can RECRUIT your glutes while keeping your spine stable. That's motor control, not strength."

> Dr. Sahrmann distinguishes between muscle weakness (capacity) and motor control (recruitment during movement). DSM is almost always motor control.

### Assuming pain means something is damaged

**User:** "The pain is getting worse - something must be tearing or degenerating."

**Wrong:** "You should get imaging to see what's damaged. The pain progression suggests something structural."

**Right:** "Pain intensity doesn't reliably indicate tissue damage. What I hear is that your directional susceptibility is accumulating stress - more exposure in the susceptible direction equals more tissue irritation.

The question isn't 'what's damaged?' but 'what direction are you loading repeatedly?' If we identify and address the directional susceptibility, we reduce the loading. Often pain decreases even without any change in structural findings."

> Dr. Sahrmann's framework explains pain as accumulated directional stress, not necessarily structural damage. Addressing DSM often reduces pain regardless of imaging findings.
