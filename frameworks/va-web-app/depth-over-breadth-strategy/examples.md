# Depth Over Breadth Strategy Examples

## PHASE 1: Assess Current Content Approach

### Creator realizing they're stuck in breadth mode

**User:** "I've been posting 3 times a week for 6 months. Each post covers a different topic - productivity tips, communication advice, leadership insights. I'm always creating but nothing seems to break through."

**Advisor:** "That's classic breadth mode. You're covering the surface of many topics, but not owning any of them. When someone thinks 'productivity tips,' do they think of YOU specifically? Or could they get the same content from 100 other creators?

Let's test: Pick your best post from the last month. If someone searched for that topic on Google, would YOUR post be the best result they could find?"

> Identify the symptom: high output, low ownership of topics

### Creator who fails the bookmark test

**User:** "Honestly, no. I wouldn't bookmark my own stuff. It's good, but it's not... reference material. It's more like quick thoughts."

**Advisor:** "That's an important realization. 'Good' isn't the bar anymore. There's too much 'good' content. The bar is: 'Would someone save this to come back to later?'

Quick thoughts have their place - they keep you visible. But they don't create authority. When did you last create something that could genuinely serve as a reference? Something someone would send to a colleague asking about that topic?"

> Use the bookmark test to make quality concrete

## PHASE 2: Diagnose the Problem

### Creator identifying time as the barrier

**User:** "It's definitely time. I feel like I need to post consistently to stay visible, so I can't spend two weeks on one piece."

**Advisor:** "Here's the math nobody does: You post 3 times per week. That's 150+ pieces per year. How many of those will anyone remember in 6 months? Maybe 2-3?

What if instead you posted once per week but made each piece comprehensive? 52 reference-quality pieces per year versus 150 forgettable ones. Which builds more authority?

The 'stay visible' fear keeps creators trapped in breadth mode. What if visibility came from being THE source on a topic, not from being constantly in someone's feed?"

> Challenge the frequency assumption directly

### Creator identifying knowledge as the barrier

**User:** "I think I don't know enough to go really deep. I'm worried I'll run out of things to say."

**Advisor:** "That's actually good news - it means research is your opportunity. You don't need to know everything before you start. You need to be willing to learn more than anyone else.

The research IS the content. 'I analyzed 200 email subject lines and here's what I found' is more valuable than 'Here are my thoughts on email subject lines.' You're not the expert - you're the researcher who does the work others won't.

What topic could you research more thoroughly than anyone else has?"

> Reframe knowledge gap as research opportunity

## PHASE 3: Topic Selection for Depth

### Validating a topic against the three criteria

**User:** "What about 'how to negotiate a raise'? A lot of people search for that."

**Advisor:** "Let's test it.

**Searchable?** Yes - high search volume. People actively look for this.

**Completable?** Maybe not. 'How to negotiate a raise' is actually pretty broad. It includes: timing, research, scripts, body language, handling objections, following up, industry differences... Can you cover ALL of that exhaustively?

**Evergreen?** Mostly yes, though specific numbers and market conditions change.

I'd narrow it further. What about 'How to research your market rate before negotiating a raise' or 'Word-for-word scripts for every raise negotiation scenario'? Both are narrower and more completable."

> Push for narrower scope to enable true depth

### Rejecting a topic that's too broad

**User:** "I want to write about content marketing."

**Advisor:** "That's a category, not a topic. 'Content marketing' includes strategy, execution, platforms, formats, distribution, measurement, team structure, tools... You can't be comprehensive about all of content marketing.

What's the specific problem within content marketing? Is it 'How to distribute content beyond just posting it'? Is it 'How to measure content marketing ROI'? Is it 'How to create a content calendar that actually works'?

Pick one slice. Go a mile deep on that slice. What specific content marketing problem do you understand better than most people?"

> Refuse to accept broad topics - always push for specificity

## PHASE 4: Research Phase Design

### Identifying research that creates unfair advantage

**User:** "I could analyze subject lines from successful newsletters. I subscribe to like 50 newsletters already."

**Advisor:** "Good starting point. But '50 newsletters' is surface-level. What would make this undeniable?

What if you analyzed subject lines from the top 100 newsletters over 6 months? That's ~2,600 subject lines. You could categorize them, measure open rates (via surveys or publicly available data), identify patterns nobody else has identified.

Now you're not sharing opinions - you're sharing data nobody else has. 'I analyzed 2,600 subject lines from 100 top newsletters. Here's what actually works.' That's a piece nobody can replicate easily."

> Push from 'adequate research' to 'unfair advantage research'

### Adding primary research to secondary research

**User:** "I can find a lot of resources online about this. I'll synthesize the best advice."

**Advisor:** "Synthesis is necessary but not sufficient. Everyone can read the same resources and synthesize them. What's YOUR unique contribution?

Consider: What if you interviewed 10 practitioners who've actually done this? Or surveyed 100 people about their experience? Or documented your own case studies in detail?

Primary research - data you gather yourself - is the moat. Secondary research - synthesizing what exists - is the foundation. You need both, but primary research is what makes your piece the definitive source."

> Distinguish between synthesis (table stakes) and primary research (differentiation)

## PHASE 5: Structure for Depth

### Building structure for both scanners and deep readers

**User:** "I'd have sections on: why subject lines matter, what makes a good subject line, examples of good subject lines, and common mistakes."

**Advisor:** "That's a start, but it doesn't pass the comprehensiveness test. What about:

- Different subject line strategies by newsletter type (personal, B2B, ecommerce)
- How subject lines interact with preview text
- Testing methodology - how to A/B test subject lines
- The psychology of why certain patterns work
- Industry-specific considerations
- Subject line length optimization
- Personalization techniques
- Subject lines for specific goals (opens vs. clicks vs. conversions)

A scanner should be able to jump to 'testing methodology' and get exactly what they need. A deep reader should finish understanding everything about subject lines. Does your structure allow both?"

> Expand structure to truly comprehensive coverage

### Addressing edge cases and exceptions

**User:** "I think my structure covers the main points."

**Advisor:** "Main points isn't enough. What about edge cases?

- What if you have a tiny list vs. a large list?
- What if you're in a regulated industry?
- What about subject lines for transactional emails vs. promotional?
- What about international audiences?
- What about accessibility considerations?

Comprehensive means someone can apply your advice to their specific situation, even if their situation is unusual. What edge cases might your readers encounter that your current structure doesn't address?"

> Push for edge case coverage that makes the resource complete

## PHASE 6: Create the Plan

### Setting a specific commitment

**User:** "I think I can get this done in about a month. I'll start by gathering all the newsletters this week."

**Advisor:** "Good timeline. Let's make the first step specific.

This week's task: Gather subject lines from your top 50 newsletters for the past 3 months. Create a spreadsheet tracking: date, subject line, newsletter name, your own open behavior (did you open or not?).

That's your research foundation. By next week, you'll have 600+ data points to analyze.

What day this week will you block time to start gathering? And how many hours?"

> Lock in specific first action with day and time

### Final commitment and motivation

**User:** "I'll block Thursday afternoon - 3 hours to start building the spreadsheet."

**Advisor:** "Good. Thursday afternoon, 3 hours, subject line spreadsheet.

Remember why this matters: Anyone can write '10 Subject Line Tips.' Almost nobody will analyze 600+ real subject lines from successful newsletters. That's the depth that creates authority.

One comprehensive resource beats 10 forgettable posts. When you publish this, you want people to think: 'This is THE guide to newsletter subject lines.' That's the bar."

> Reinforce the 'why' behind the effort
