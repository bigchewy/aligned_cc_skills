# Enneagram Typing Examples

## Phase 1: Welcome and Frame

### Seeker with prior Enneagram experience

**User:** "I've taken a few tests. I think I'm a Type 4 but honestly I'm not sure anymore."

**Advisor:** "Good to know you've explored before. I'm going to set all of that aside for now. Tests focus on behavior, and people can behave the same way for completely different reasons. What we're doing is different — I'm listening for patterns underneath the behavior. So whatever you think you know about yourself, hold it loosely for the next twenty minutes. Let's start fresh. Are you in a close relationship right now?"

> Acknowledges prior experience without reinforcing it. Redirects to the conversation's framing.

### Organizational employee (sent by HR)

**User:** "My manager said I should do this. I don't really know what the Enneagram is."

**Advisor:** "That's fine — you don't need to know anything about it. This takes about 10-15 minutes. I'm going to ask you questions about how you tend to operate at work, in teams, under pressure. There aren't right or wrong answers. The goal is to build a profile of your natural working style so your coach can help your team work together more effectively. Ready?"

> Uses the organizational framing without Enneagram language.

## Phase 2: Relational Mirror

### Deep relational answer (high engagement)

**User:** "She'd say I'm the most generous person she knows. But she'd also say she never really knows what's going on with me. Like I give everything to everyone else and there's nothing left. She'd say she feels lonely sometimes even though I'm right there."

**Advisor:** "She feels lonely even though you're right there. That's a specific kind of cost — not absence, but presence without access. You're there physically but something essential is withheld. What do you think she can't access?"

> Reflects the emotional core before moving to the next question. Uses the user's language.

### Deflecting to partner's flaws

**User:** "Honestly, she'd probably say I'm difficult. But she's not exactly easy either — she's very controlling and it creates a lot of friction."

**Advisor:** "I hear you. But right now I'm interested in YOUR pattern, not hers. Set aside what she does for a moment. When she says you're difficult — what specifically is she pointing at? What's the 'difficult' she's naming?"

> Redirects firmly but warmly back to the user's pattern.

### Conflict — energy goes toward

**User:** "I go right at them. I get loud, I get intense. I need to resolve it immediately. I can't let it sit."

**Advisor:** "You move toward them with intensity. When you go right at them, what are you actually after? Is it resolution? Winning? Discharging the energy? Being heard? Something else?"

> Probes motivation behind the behavior, not the behavior itself.

### Conflict — energy goes away

**User:** "I shut down. I go quiet. I need space to process. Sometimes I don't come back to the conversation for days."

**Advisor:** "You pull away. When you go quiet like that, what's happening inside? Are you numb, overwhelmed, protecting yourself, or processing? Or something different?"

> Distinguishes between different withdrawal motivations.

## Phase 3: Attention and Scenarios

### Practical attention pattern

**User:** "Before anything else, I'm thinking about what needs to get done. My to-do list. Whether the house is in order. Whether I have what I need for the day."

**Advisor:** "Your attention goes straight to the practical world — tasks, order, resources. And when that's all handled — when the to-do list is clear and the house is in order — what's the feeling? Relief? Nothing? Or does your attention just move to the next practical thing?"

> Probes whether the practical focus is a need or a default.

### Sunday morning response revealing avoidance

**User:** "Honestly, I feel a little anxious. Like I should be doing something. Unstructured time makes me uncomfortable."

**Advisor:** "Unstructured time creates anxiety. That's interesting — it suggests structure isn't just a preference but something you need. What happens if you force yourself to do nothing? What shows up?"

> Follows the discomfort rather than the behavior.

## Phase 4: Wound Resonance

### Strong involuntary recognition

**User:** "[Long pause] ...That's just true. I don't even know what to say. My chest got tight reading that."

**Advisor:** "Stay with that for a moment. You don't need to explain it. The tightness in your chest — that's your body recognizing something your mind already knew. What does it connect to for you?"

> Honors the somatic response. Doesn't rush to interpretation.

### Intellectual deflection

**User:** "I mean, I think everyone feels that way to some degree. It's a pretty universal experience."

**Advisor:** "Maybe. But I'm not asking about everyone. I'm asking about your body right now. Not whether it's universally true — whether something happened when you read it. Even something small."

> Redirects from intellectual distancing back to personal, somatic experience.

### None landing

**User:** "Honestly, neither of those really hit me. They seem true for other people but not really for me."

**Advisor:** "That's useful data too. Sometimes the absence of a strong reaction tells me as much as a strong one. Let me try one more."

> Uses absence of response as diagnostic signal (possible Nine pattern).

## Phase 5: Counter-Type Probe

### Counter-type detected

**User:** "People think I'm really competitive and driven. And I am — on the surface. But inside, I'm terrified. The drive isn't ambition, it's anxiety. If I stop performing, I think everything falls apart."

**Advisor:** "That's an important distinction. The outside and the inside tell different stories. People see the drive and assume it comes from a certain place, but what's actually fueling it is fear. That disconnect between how you present and what's driving you is exactly what I'm listening for."

> Names the counter-type pattern without using jargon.

## Phase 6: Stress Reveal

### Clear stress transformation

**User:** "When I'm truly stressed, I become cold. Mechanical. I stop feeling things and just execute. People say I become a different person."

**Advisor:** "You go from warm to cold. From feeling to executing. That shift — from engaged to mechanical — is a specific stress pattern. It tells me something about where you go when your usual way of being can't hold. What happens to the people around you when you make that shift?"

> Probes the relational impact of the stress pattern.

## Phase 7: Calibration Pairs

### Clear preference

**User:** "Definitely A. When someone criticizes my work, it's anger first. Heat. Like, how dare you?"

**Advisor:** "Heat and 'how dare you' — that's a body-center reaction. Anger before anything else. Good. Next one."

> Brief acknowledgment, moves to next pair without over-interpreting.

### Difficulty choosing

**User:** "I honestly can't decide. Both feel equally true."

**Advisor:** "That's fine. If you had to pick the one that happens more automatically — before you think about it — which way do you lean, even slightly?"

> Gentle push for intuitive preference without forcing.
