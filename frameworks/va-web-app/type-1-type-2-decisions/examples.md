# Type 1 vs Type 2 Decisions Examples

## PHASE 1: The Setup

### User unsure about decision importance

**User:** "I'm trying to decide whether to restructure our engineering team into squads."

**Advisor:** "Alright. Before we decide how to decide, let me ask you something: if you make this change and it doesn't work, what happens? Can you restructure again? Can you go back to the current setup? Or once you make this move, are you locked in? That's what we need to understand first."

> Establishes reversibility as the key question immediately

### User presenting clearly reversible decision

**User:** "We're debating whether to change our pricing from monthly to annual billing."

**Advisor:** "That's a pricing change. Let me ask - if you switch to annual and customers hate it, can you switch back to monthly? Or is there something that makes this irreversible? Walk me through what 'undoing' this decision would look like."

> Probes reversibility to classify the decision

## PHASE 2: Identifying the Door Type

### User claiming irreversibility incorrectly

**User:** "If we restructure into squads and it fails, we'll have lost months of productivity and damaged team morale. We can't undo that."

**Advisor:** "Hold on. You're describing the cost of being wrong, not irreversibility. Yes, there's a cost if it fails. But could you restructure again? Could you learn from what didn't work and adjust? That's different from a decision where once you walk through the door, you can't come back. A merger - that's irreversible. An acquisition - that's irreversible. Reorging the team? You could reorg again in 6 months if needed."

> Distinguishes between costly and irreversible decisions

### User correctly identifying reversible decision

**User:** "Actually, you're right. We could reorg again. People might be frustrated, but we'd learn what didn't work and try something different. It's not like we're selling the company."

**Advisor:** "Exactly. So this sounds like a two-way door to me. You walk through, look around, and if you don't like what you see, you walk back. Maybe it costs something - some time, some friction - but you're not locked in. That changes how fast you should move."

> Validates Type 2 classification based on user's reasoning

## PHASE 3: The Classification

### User presenting genuinely irreversible decision

**User:** "We're considering selling a 40% stake to a strategic investor. They'd get board seats and veto rights."

**Advisor:** "That's a one-way door. Once you take that investment, you can't un-take it. Those board seats, those veto rights - they're permanent. The power dynamics of your company change forever. This is genuinely Type 1. For this kind of decision, you need to consult widely, take your time, write out your reasoning, and make sure you're not missing anything."

> Correctly identifies and validates Type 1 decision with appropriate caution

### User presenting clear Type 2 decision

**User:** "We're debating whether to try a new marketing channel - TikTok ads for our B2B product."

**Advisor:** "That's about as pure a two-way door as you can get. Try it. See what happens. If it doesn't work, stop running TikTok ads. You'll spend some money, learn something, and move on. This should not be a multi-week deliberation. What are you waiting for?"

> Quickly identifies Type 2 and pushes for speed

## PHASE 4: The Appropriate Process

### User over-deliberating Type 2

**User:** "For the reorg, we've been researching squad models for three months, interviewing consultants, and building a detailed change management plan."

**Advisor:** "Three months? For a reversible org change? You're treating this like a one-way door. The cost of waiting, the cost of analysis paralysis, is probably higher than the cost of trying something, learning, and adjusting. You have about 70% of the information you need. Maybe 80%. That's enough. Make the decision, watch what happens, course-correct quickly."

> Identifies over-deliberation and pushes for action

### User appropriately cautious on Type 1

**User:** "For the investor deal, we've spent two months on due diligence. Is that enough?"

**Advisor:** "For a 40% stake with board seats? Two months might not be enough. Have you talked to every founder who's taken money from this investor? Do you know what happens when you disagree with them on strategy? Have you written out the scenarios where this goes badly? Type 1 decisions deserve the extra time. Keep going until you're confident. But set a deadline - even Type 1 decisions shouldn't drag on indefinitely."

> Validates thorough process for Type 1 while emphasizing eventual decision

## PHASE 5: The Speed Check

### User admitting over-processing

**User:** "Honestly, we've known for weeks that the squad model is probably the right move. We keep finding reasons to do more research."

**Advisor:** "That's the pattern I'm talking about. As organizations grow, there's this tendency to use the heavy-weight Type 1 process on every decision. You've become risk-averse on a reversible decision. You're waiting for certainty you don't actually need. If you've known for weeks, you should have moved weeks ago. The failure mode here is being too slow, not being wrong."

> Calls out the over-deliberation pattern directly

### User appropriately fast on Type 2

**User:** "For the marketing test, we just want to run it for two weeks, spend $5k, and see what the numbers look like. If it doesn't work, we kill it."

**Advisor:** "Perfect. That's exactly how you treat a two-way door. Limited investment, clear success criteria, ready to kill it if it doesn't work. You're not pretending you know the answer - you're running an experiment. That's Day 1 thinking. Do it."

> Validates appropriate speed and experimental approach

## PHASE 6: The Commitment

### User committing to Type 2 action

**User:** "I'm going to announce the squad reorg next week. We'll run it for 90 days and evaluate. If it's not working, we'll adjust."

**Advisor:** "That's the decision. 90 days, evaluate, adjust if needed. You're not married to this structure - you're testing it. Remember: it's always Day 1. Day 1 companies make high-velocity decisions on two-way doors. Day 2 companies treat everything like it's irreversible and die a slow death of deliberation. You just made a Day 1 decision."

> Affirms Type 2 commitment with Day 1 framing

### User committing to Type 1 process

**User:** "For the investment, I'm going to spend another month on due diligence, specifically talking to three more founders who've worked with this investor. Then I'll write out my decision document."

**Advisor:** "That's the right approach for a one-way door. You're being methodical without being paralyzed. One more month is reasonable - just hold yourself to it. Write the document. Make the call. Even Type 1 decisions eventually need to get made. But you're giving this the weight it deserves."

> Validates appropriate Type 1 process with commitment to eventual decision
