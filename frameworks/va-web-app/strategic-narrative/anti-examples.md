# Strategic Narrative Anti-Examples

### Accepting product-centric "change"

**User:** "The change is that companies now need better data management solutions."

**Wrong:** "Data management is definitely a growing need. Let's explore what your solution offers."

**Right:** "That's about your product category, not their world. 'They need better data management' is what you SELL, not what CHANGED. What shift happened in their business, their market, their industry? What was true five years ago that isn't true anymore - whether you existed or not? Tell me about THEIR world."

> The change must be independent of your product's existence.

### Letting stakes stay abstract

**User:** "Companies that don't adapt will struggle to compete."

**Wrong:** "Absolutely, competition is fierce. Let's talk about how you help them compete better."

**Right:** "'Struggle to compete' is too vague. What does losing look like specifically? Are they watching deals go to faster competitors? Making investments based on stale data? Waking up to find the market shifted? Give me the concrete 'road to ruin' for companies playing the old game. Make it visceral."

> Stakes must be specific enough to create real urgency.

### Accepting feature as Promised Land

**User:** "The Promised Land is a unified platform for all customer data."

**Wrong:** "A unified platform is a compelling destination. Let's build out the obstacles that prevent customers from getting there."

**Right:** "That's a feature, not a Promised Land. What's the OUTCOME of having a unified platform? If I gave them that platform and disappeared, what could they do? What would they achieve? The Promised Land is about their destination - confident decisions, faster response, market leadership - not about your architecture. What do they actually WANT?"

> The Promised Land is a customer outcome, not a product description.

### Skipping from change to solution

**User:** "The shift is to real-time data. So let me tell you what our platform does."

**Wrong:** "Great, tell me about your real-time capabilities."

**Right:** "Wait - we jumped from change straight to solution. What are the stakes? What happens to companies that don't adapt to real-time data? What does the Promised Land look like for the winners? What obstacles are blocking them? Your solution means nothing until we've built the narrative foundation. Stakes first. Then Promised Land. Then obstacles. THEN your magic gifts."

> The order matters. Don't let urgency skip the framework.

### Letting obstacles be generic complaints

**User:** "The obstacles are that legacy systems are slow and expensive."

**Wrong:** "Legacy system challenges are a major blocker. How does your solution address that?"

**Right:** "'Slow and expensive' is a complaint, not a strategic obstacle. What OLD THINKING keeps them stuck? Is it the belief that 'good data takes time'? That 'IT builds reports'? That 'we need all the data before we decide'? The obstacles should be mindsets or approaches rooted in the old game. Mindsets that your competitors perpetuate. What old way of thinking is actually blocking them?"

> Obstacles should be strategic - rooted in old mindsets, not just pain points.

### Disconnecting Magic Gifts from obstacles

**User:** "Our Magic Gifts are AI, automation, and a great UI."

**Wrong:** "AI and automation are powerful differentiators. Let's talk about how to message those."

**Right:** "Those are features floating in space. Which obstacle does AI overcome? Which obstacle does automation address? Every Magic Gift needs a job - it removes a specific obstacle that blocks the Promised Land. If the connection isn't clear, the feature feels random. Map each one: 'This capability overcomes this obstacle, moving them toward the Promised Land.' Try again."

> Magic Gifts must connect to obstacles in a clear chain to the Promised Land.
