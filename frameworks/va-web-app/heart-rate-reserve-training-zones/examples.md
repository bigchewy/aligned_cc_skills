# Heart Rate Reserve Training Zones Examples

## PHASE 1: Understanding the Why

### User already monitors HR

**User:** "I have an Apple Watch. I use it for workouts but I'm not sure I'm using HR data correctly."

**Advisor:** "Good - you've got the hardware. The watch gives you real-time feedback, which is exactly what we need. Most people just glance at HR without knowing what the numbers mean for their specific physiology. We're going to change that by calculating zones that actually fit your cardiovascular capacity, not generic percentages."

> Validates existing equipment while setting up the value of personalized zones

### User has no monitoring

**User:** "I don't track anything. I just exercise by feel."

**Advisor:** "Exercise by feel works when your body signals are reliable. With autonomic dysfunction, they're often not. What feels 'moderate' might actually be pushing you into overexertion, or what feels 'hard' might be well within your capacity. Getting some form of HR monitoring isn't optional for this - it's the difference between guessing and training. Even a basic chest strap for $30-40 will do."

> Direct about necessity of monitoring without being judgmental

### User has chest strap

**User:** "I have a Polar chest strap from when I used to run."

**Advisor:** "Perfect. Chest straps are the gold standard for accuracy - much better than wrist-based monitors, especially during exercise when wrist contact can be inconsistent. Dust it off and pair it with your phone or watch. That's all you need."

> Acknowledges quality of equipment, provides practical next step

## PHASE 2: Collecting Baseline Data

### User knows their numbers

**User:** "I'm 42 and my morning resting HR averages around 72."

**Advisor:** "Good data. Age 42, resting HR 72. That gives us everything we need. Your estimated max is 178, and your heart rate reserve is 106. Let me calculate your zones."

> Efficient progression when data is available

### User has elevated resting HR

**User:** "I'm 35, but my resting HR is around 95. Is that going to mess up the zones?"

**Advisor:** "It changes them, but it doesn't mess them up - this is exactly why we use HRR instead of simple percentage-of-max. Your elevated resting HR compresses your usable range. With a max around 185 and resting at 95, your HRR is only 90 instead of the 120 it might be with a resting HR of 65. That means your Zone 2 is a narrower band, which actually makes it more important to stay in it precisely. As you recondition, we expect that resting HR to drop, which will expand your zones."

> Explains how elevated resting HR affects zones without dismissing it as a problem

### User doesn't know resting HR

**User:** "I have no idea what my resting HR is. I've never measured it."

**Advisor:** "Then that's assignment one. Here's the protocol: keep your phone or watch by your bed. When you wake up - before sitting up, before checking your phone, before coffee - lie still for two minutes and then check your pulse. Do this for at least 3 days, ideally 5, and average the results. The morning resting HR before the day's stress hits is your true baseline. Come back with that number and we'll calculate your zones."

> Provides specific measurement protocol rather than generic advice

## PHASE 3: Calculating Zones

### Standard calculation

**User:** "Okay, I'm 38 with a resting HR of 68."

**Advisor:** "Let's calculate:

Your estimated max HR: 220 - 38 = 182
Your resting HR: 68
Your heart rate reserve: 182 - 68 = 114

Your zones:
- Zone 1 (Recovery): Below 136 - easy movement, recovery
- Zone 2 (Base): 136-148 - where most training happens
- Zone 3 (Threshold): 148-159 - only when progressing well
- Zone 4 (VO2max): 159-171 - much later in protocol
- Zone 5 (Neuromuscular): Above 171 - fully recovered only

For now, 136-148 is your sweet spot. If you're staying in that range and can hold a conversation, you're in the right place."

> Provides complete calculated zones with practical guidance

### User questions the formula

**User:** "Is 220-age accurate? I've heard that formula isn't reliable."

**Advisor:** "You're right to question it. 220-age is a population average with significant individual variation - could be off by 10-15 bpm either direction. If you've ever had a max HR test or know from previous training that your true max is different, we should use that. For our purposes, though, HRR accommodates some of that error because we're working from your resting HR too. And since you're staying in Zone 2, not pushing to max, a few bpm of error in max HR estimation doesn't change the protocol much."

> Acknowledges limitation honestly while explaining why it's acceptable for this use case

## PHASE 4: Zone Assignment for Current Status

### User in early recovery

**User:** "I'm pretty early in this. Orthostatic symptoms are still significant."

**Advisor:** "Then Zone 1-2 only. Zone 2 is your ceiling, not your target. If you drift above it, ease off. The goal isn't to train hard - it's to consistently load your cardiovascular system in a range that stimulates adaptation without triggering autonomic backlash. For you, that probably means the lower half of Zone 2 most days."

> Clear ceiling establishment for early recovery

### User progressing well

**User:** "I've been doing the protocol for about 6 weeks. Orthostatic tolerance is better and I'm handling the workouts fine."

**Advisor:** "Good progress. At 6 weeks with stable improvement, we can start introducing brief Zone 3 intervals - maybe 2-3 minutes within a Zone 2 workout, once or twice a week. Not sustained Zone 3 work, just touching it and coming back down. Watch how you recover. If the next day feels fine and your resting HR stays stable, you can keep it. If you notice any regression, drop back to Zone 2 only."

> Cautious progression with specific parameters and monitoring criteria

### Former athlete wanting more intensity

**User:** "I used to train at high intensities all the time. Staying in Zone 2 feels painfully easy."

**Advisor:** "I get it. Your athletic memory says this isn't 'real' training. But here's the thing: your cardiovascular system needs rebuilding, not just stimulating. The slow Zone 2 work is what drives cardiac remodeling - increasing stroke volume, improving efficiency. High-intensity work burns glycogen and triggers stress hormones, but it doesn't rebuild the pump the same way. Think of Zone 2 as foundation work, not easy work. The intensity comes later."

> Validates athletic identity while explaining physiological rationale

## PHASE 5: Practical Application

### User asks about cardiac drift

**User:** "My HR keeps creeping up during workouts even when I don't feel like I'm working harder."

**Advisor:** "That's cardiac drift - normal physiology. As you exercise, plasma volume decreases through sweating, core temperature rises, and the heart has to beat faster to maintain output. Some drift is expected, especially in longer sessions. What's not normal: significant drift in the first 10-15 minutes, or drift that pushes you two zones up. If you're starting in Zone 2 and ending in Zone 4, either the sessions are too long or your recovery between sessions isn't adequate."

> Normalizes expected drift while flagging problematic patterns

### User setting up alerts

**User:** "How should I set up my watch alerts?"

**Advisor:** "Set a single alert at the top of your allowed zone - probably the top of Zone 2 for now. When it buzzes, ease off. Don't set multiple alerts - you'll spend more time looking at your wrist than training. The goal is a ceiling alarm, not constant feedback. And remember: 5-10 bpm variation is noise. You're watching trends, not chasing exact numbers."

> Practical setup guidance that avoids over-monitoring

## PHASE 6: Progression Criteria

### User ready to progress zones

**User:** "I've been consistently in Zone 2 for a month. Workouts feel easier, morning HR is down 8 bpm. Can we add some Zone 3?"

**Advisor:** "That's textbook readiness. Morning HR down 8 bpm is significant cardiovascular adaptation. Consistent Zone 2 completion without excessive fatigue, workouts feeling easier at same HR - all green lights. Time to recalculate your zones with the new resting HR, then add Zone 3 intervals: 2-3 minutes within your Zone 2 sessions, twice a week max. Monitor your recovery. If morning HR stays stable and you're not regressing, we continue. If anything goes backward, we pause the Zone 3 work."

> Validates readiness, recalculates zones, provides specific progression parameters

### User showing warning signs

**User:** "The last few sessions have felt harder than they used to at the same heart rate."

**Advisor:** "That's a warning sign. Same HR feeling harder means your body's working harder to produce the same output - either fatigue accumulation, inadequate recovery, or early overtraining. First step: check your morning resting HR. If it's elevated, take 2-3 easy days, Zone 1 only or complete rest. If it's normal, we look at other factors - sleep, hydration, stress. Don't push through this. When the same workout feels harder, your body is telling you something."

> Takes warning sign seriously, provides diagnostic steps
