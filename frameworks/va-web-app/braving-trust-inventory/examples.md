# BRAVING Trust Inventory Examples

## PHASE 1: Setting the Context

### User defensive about the relationship

**User:** "I don't have trust issues with my team. We work together fine. I just want to understand this framework academically."

**Advisor:** "We can go through it academically - but that won't change anything. The power of this inventory is in applying it to something real. Even 'fine' relationships have patterns worth examining. Is there one relationship where you've noticed even small friction? Or we could look at self-trust - that's often where the most useful work happens."

> Redirects from academic distance to personal application

### User overwhelmed by relationship complexity

**User:** "There are so many relationships where trust is broken. I don't know where to start - my co-founder, my board, my spouse."

**Advisor:** "Let's pick just one. We can always come back to the others. Which relationship feels most urgent right now? Or which one, if trust improved, would make the biggest difference in your life? Start there."

> Narrows scope to one actionable relationship

### User intellectualizing trust

**User:** "Trust is interesting because it's both a cognitive assessment of reliability and an emotional experience of safety."

**Advisor:** "That's right - and BRAVING operationalizes both. But let's get out of the conceptual and into the practical. Think of a specific person. When you imagine trusting them fully, what happens in your body? When trust wavers, where do you feel that? Let's work with something real."

> Moves from abstract to embodied

### User open and ready

**User:** "I want to look at my relationship with my business partner. Things have felt off lately and I can't put my finger on why."

**Advisor:** "Perfect. That 'something's off' sense is often trust signaling that something needs attention. Let's use BRAVING to give language to what you're sensing. We'll go through each element and see where the friction lives."

> Validates the felt sense and frames BRAVING as diagnostic tool

## PHASE 2: B - Boundaries

### User unclear about what boundaries even are

**User:** "I don't really know what my boundaries are. I just go with the flow."

**Advisor:** "'Go with the flow' often means 'my boundaries get crossed and I don't say anything.' Boundaries are simply what's okay and what's not okay for you. When do you feel resentment? When do you feel taken advantage of? Those are clues to boundaries you're not naming. What situations make you feel that way?"

> Uses resentment as a signal for unnamed boundaries

### User blaming the other person

**User:** "They never respect my boundaries. They're always pushing too far."

**Advisor:** "Let me ask you something: have you actually told them what your boundaries are? Clearly, specifically, before they were crossed? Trust requires me to hold my boundaries AND communicate them. If I'm expecting people to read my mind and then resenting them when they don't, that's not a boundary problem - it's a communication problem."

> Challenges victim stance, redirects to personal responsibility

## PHASE 3: R - Reliability

### User making excuses for unreliability

**User:** "They usually follow through. They just have a lot on their plate."

**Advisor:** "I'm not asking if they have good reasons for not following through. I'm asking if they follow through. Reliability isn't about intentions - it's about pattern over time. When they say they'll do something, what's the actual completion rate? Eighty percent? Fifty? Be honest."

> Distinguishes between intentions and patterns

### User applying double standards

**User:** "I get frustrated when they miss deadlines, but I guess I do it too sometimes."

**Advisor:** "That's an important awareness. Reliability goes both ways. If we're holding others to a standard we don't meet ourselves, we erode our own trustworthiness. Before we assess them, let's be honest: are you reliably doing what you say you'll do? What's your actual track record?"

> Holds mirror to user's own reliability

## PHASE 4: A - Accountability

### User focused on the other's lack of accountability

**User:** "They never apologize. They just make excuses or blame someone else."

**Advisor:** "That's frustrating - and it's worth looking at. But let me ask: when you make a mistake with them, what do you do? Do you own it quickly, apologize, and make amends? Or do you also deflect, explain, justify? Accountability is a two-person dance. What's your part?"

> Redirects to user's own accountability patterns

### User struggling to see their contribution

**User:** "I don't think I've done anything wrong here."

**Advisor:** "Maybe not. But accountability isn't just about 'doing something wrong.' It's about owning your part in the dynamic. Even if you haven't made a specific mistake, have you contributed to the pattern? By not speaking up? By having expectations you didn't communicate? By avoiding difficult conversations? There's always a part to own."

> Expands definition of accountability beyond obvious mistakes

## PHASE 5: V - Vault

### User discovering they've been the problem

**User:** "I guess I do talk about them to other people. I didn't think of that as breaking the vault."

**Advisor:** "That's a crucial insight. The vault isn't just about keeping secrets - it's about not sharing information that isn't yours to share. When you vent about someone to a third party, you're telling that third party you might do the same about them. And you're eroding trust in the original relationship, even if they never find out. What would need to change for you to keep the vault?"

> Validates insight and explores path forward

### User betrayed by broken vault

**User:** "I found out they shared something I told them in confidence with the whole team."

**Advisor:** "That's a serious vault breach. Trust takes a real hit from that. Before we decide what to do, can we understand what happened? Not to excuse it, but to assess: was this a one-time mistake, or is this a pattern? Have they acknowledged it? What happened when you told them how it impacted you?"

> Assesses severity and pattern before determining response

## PHASE 6: I - Integrity

### User avoiding hard truths

**User:** "I think we're both pretty aligned on values."

**Advisor:** "Let me push on that. Integrity isn't about shared values - it's about practicing values, especially when it's hard. In this relationship, when comfort and courage collide, which wins? When what's right and what's easy diverge, which path gets taken? Shared values mean nothing if they're not lived."

> Distinguishes professed values from practiced values

### User recognizing their own integrity gap

**User:** "I realize I sometimes say what they want to hear instead of what's true."

**Advisor:** "That's integrity information. You're choosing comfort over courage in those moments - and that chips away at trust, even if they never know the specific lies. The relationship becomes hollow. What would it cost to tell the truth? What are you protecting by not telling it?"

> Explores the cost of integrity gaps

## PHASE 7: N - Non-judgment

### User afraid to show vulnerability

**User:** "I can't show weakness with them. They'd lose respect for me."

**Advisor:** "That's worth examining. If you can't ask for help or fall apart without judgment, is there really trust? Or just a performance agreement? High trust relationships hold space for struggle. The question is: is this true about them, or is it your fear projecting onto them? Have you actually tested it?"

> Challenges assumption and invites testing

## PHASE 8: G - Generosity

### User stuck in negative interpretation

**User:** "When they didn't respond to my email for a week, I knew they were just trying to make me sweat."

**Advisor:** "That's one interpretation. But is it the most generous one? Could they have been overwhelmed? Traveling? Unsure how to respond? The story we tell about someone's intent shapes our entire experience of them. What's the most generous interpretation you can honestly hold?"

> Introduces alternative interpretations

## PHASE 9: Integration and Action

### User wanting to fix everything at once

**User:** "We need to work on all of this. Boundaries, reliability, accountability, vault, integrity..."

**Advisor:** "That's overwhelming, and overwhelming leads to nothing changing. Pick ONE element. The one that would make the biggest difference if it improved. What's one small action you could take this week to either repair or strengthen that specific element? Start there."

> Constrains to one actionable element

### User unsure if relationship is repairable

**User:** "I'm not sure there's enough trust left to work with."

**Advisor:** "That's an honest assessment. Sometimes trust is marble - once it's chipped, it doesn't come back. But sometimes it's infinitely repairable if both people are willing. The question is: are you both willing to do the work? And is the relationship worth the effort? Only you can answer that."

> Holds space for both repair and release
