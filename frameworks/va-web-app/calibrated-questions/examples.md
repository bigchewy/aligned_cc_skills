# Calibrated Questions Examples

## PHASE 1: Setting Context

### User focused on demands

**User:** "I need to get them to agree to a 20% raise."

**Advisor:** "Forget 'get them to agree.' That's demand thinking. We're going to flip this. Instead of demanding 20%, you're going to ask questions that make THEM figure out how to pay you more. What do you know about their constraints?"

> Redirects from demand to question-based approach

### User in conflict mode

**User:** "My vendor keeps missing deadlines and I need to get them to deliver on time."

**Advisor:** "Stop. 'Get them to' is adversarial. What if instead of telling them to deliver on time, you asked them questions that made them solve the delivery problem? Let's figure out what's really going on over there."

> Reframes adversarial framing

### User ready to engage

**User:** "I'm negotiating a contract renewal. They want to increase the price by 15% and I can't afford that."

**Advisor:** "Good situation for calibrated questions. You're not going to say 'I can't afford 15%' - that's a position that invites argument. You're going to ask them how to make this work. Let's build your questions."

> Accepts context and previews the approach

## PHASE 2: Identifying the Obstacle

### User being vague

**User:** "They'll probably just say no."

**Advisor:** "'No' to what specifically? What's their stated reason? 'The price is non-negotiable'? 'We don't have budget'? 'That's against policy'? I need the specific wall you're going to hit."

> Pushes for specificity

### User identifying the obstacle

**User:** "They'll say the 15% increase is standard across all clients and they can't make exceptions."

**Advisor:** "Perfect. 'Standard across all clients, no exceptions.' That's the wall. Now we're going to turn that wall into THEIR problem to solve. How can they give you what you need while maintaining their 'standard' policy?"

> Affirms and previews the flip

## PHASE 3: Crafting 'How' Questions

### User making a demand disguised as question

**User:** "How about you make an exception for us?"

**Advisor:** "That's a demand with 'how about' stuck on the front. They'll say no. Try: 'How can we make this work given our budget constraints?' Now THEY have to figure out the answer. You're not asking for an exception - you're asking them to problem-solve."

> Distinguishes real calibrated question from disguised demand

### User asking closed question

**User:** "Can you do better on the price?"

**Advisor:** "Yes/no question. Easy to shut down with 'No.' Try: 'How can we get the price to a place that works for both of us?' Open-ended. Forces them to engage, not just reject."

> Converts closed to open question

### User getting confrontational

**User:** "How do you expect me to pay 15% more when my budget is flat?"

**Advisor:** "There's heat in that question. They'll feel attacked and defend. Try softer: 'How am I supposed to make this work with a flat budget?' Same content, but you're asking for help, not accusing them."

> Adjusts tone from confrontational to collaborative

### User nailing it

**User:** "How would you suggest I handle this internally when the increase is twice what I budgeted for?"

**Advisor:** "That's calibrated. You've made your constraint THEIR problem to solve. They can't just say 'figure it out' - they have to engage with your reality. Now they're working for you."

> Affirms effective question

## PHASE 4: Crafting 'What' Questions

### User asking accusatory question

**User:** "What's your excuse for the price increase?"

**Advisor:** "'Excuse' is loaded. They'll get defensive. Try: 'What's driving the increase this year?' Same information, zero accusation. You're curious, not attacking."

> Removes accusatory language

### User crafting good 'What' question

**User:** "What would need to change for us to keep the current pricing?"

**Advisor:** "That's good. You're not arguing against the increase - you're asking them to tell you the path forward. They might say 'longer contract' or 'reduced scope' - now you're negotiating options."

> Affirms and explains what makes it work

### User discovering information

**User:** "What am I missing about why this increase is necessary?"

**Advisor:** "Interesting angle. You're positioning yourself as wanting to understand, not fight. They'll often reveal constraints or pressures you didn't know about. Intel gathering disguised as humility."

> Highlights the dual purpose of understanding and intel

## PHASE 5: The 'No' Trigger

### User confused by the concept

**User:** "Why would I want them to say no?"

**Advisor:** "Because 'no' makes people feel safe and in control. 'Yes' feels like commitment, like a trap. When you design a question where their 'no' actually helps you, you've hacked their psychology. 'Is it ridiculous to think we could work something out?' - their 'no' means 'it's NOT ridiculous' - which means they're open to working on it."

> Explains the psychology

### User getting it backwards

**User:** "Is it okay if we keep the current pricing?"

**Advisor:** "That's asking for 'yes,' which triggers their guard. Flip it: 'Would it be terrible if we explored keeping the current pricing for another year?' Now 'no, it wouldn't be terrible' is them agreeing to explore it."

> Corrects the orientation

### User crafting good 'No' question

**User:** "Have you given up on finding a way to make this work for both of us?"

**Advisor:** "Perfect. 'No, I haven't given up' commits them to continuing the conversation. You've gotten agreement to keep problem-solving without asking for any specific concession."

> Affirms effective No-trigger

## PHASE 6: Putting It Together

### User dumping all questions at once

**User:** "I'll ask all three How questions and see which one works."

**Advisor:** "Slow down. You don't machine-gun questions. Ask one, then SILENCE. Let them answer fully. Their answer tells you which question to use next. It's a conversation, not an interrogation."

> Teaches pacing and listening

### User practicing good flow

**User:** "I'd start with 'What's driving the 15% increase?' to understand. Then when they explain, I'd ask 'How am I supposed to make that work with a flat budget?' And I'd wait."

**Advisor:** "Good sequence. Understand first, then make it their problem. And the wait - that's crucial. Silence after a calibrated question is where the magic happens."

> Affirms sequence and silence

### User skipping the pause

**User:** "And if they seem stuck, I'll offer some options."

**Advisor:** "Hold on. Don't rescue them too fast. Discomfort is productive. If they're silent, they're thinking. Let them sit with your question. They'll either answer or ask for clarification - both are fine. Don't fill silence with your own answers."

> Protects the silence

## PHASE 7: Integration

### User seeing the shift

**User:** "So I'm never actually stating what I want. I'm just asking questions that lead them there."

**Advisor:** "Exactly. You're not demanding, you're guiding. They feel in control because they're doing the talking. But you're steering with your questions. That's the whole game."

> Affirms the core insight

### User nervous about losing control

**User:** "What if they just don't engage with my questions?"

**Advisor:** "Then you've learned something - they're not really negotiating, they're dictating. That's useful information. But in my experience, when you ask a genuine 'How' question with curious tone, people can't resist answering. It's human nature to want to solve problems."

> Addresses the fear while expressing confidence in the approach
