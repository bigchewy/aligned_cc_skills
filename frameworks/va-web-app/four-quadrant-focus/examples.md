# Four-Quadrant Focus Examples

## PHASE 1: The Problem

### User with clear sprawl

**User:** "We have a B2B SaaS product, an enterprise version, a self-serve consumer product, and we're also doing consulting to make ends meet. Plus we have a mobile app and a desktop app."

**Advisor:** "That's a mess. Let me count: two customer types, three business models, two platforms. No wonder you can't tell a coherent story. Nobody outside your company knows who you are or what you do. That's what happens when you add and add without subtracting."

> Names the sprawl directly and connects it to lack of clarity

### User who thinks they're focused

**User:** "We're really just focused on one thing - helping small businesses."

**Advisor:** "That's an audience, not a product focus. Walk me through what you actually sell. All the products, all the services, all the things you're maintaining. 'Helping small businesses' might describe your market, but I want to see the sprawl you're actually managing."

> Distinguishes market focus from product/service sprawl

## PHASE 2: Finding Your Two Dimensions

### User with clear dimensions

**User:** "I think our two dimensions are pretty clear: we have Small Business vs. Enterprise on one axis, and Simple vs. Complex product on the other."

**Advisor:** "Good. Those are real distinctions that actually matter for how you build and sell. Small business and enterprise have different sales cycles, different pricing, different support needs. Simple and complex have different development costs, different buyer sophistication. Now you've got four quadrants - let's see what fills them."

> Validates dimensions that have real operational meaning

### User struggling to find dimensions

**User:** "I'm not sure what our two dimensions are. Maybe industry vertical? Or price point?"

**Advisor:** "Let me ask it differently. When you win a customer, what makes them different from customers you lose? Is it their size? Their sophistication? Whether they want self-serve or high-touch? The dimensions should be the two questions that most determine who buys and how you serve them. What patterns have you seen?"

> Helps surface dimensions from actual customer patterns

## PHASE 3: Drawing the Grid

### User mapping clearly

**User:** "Okay, so in the Small Business + Simple quadrant, we have our self-serve product. In Small Business + Complex, we have our premium tier. Enterprise + Simple, we have... actually nothing. Enterprise + Complex, we have our enterprise product and consulting."

**Advisor:** "Now I can see it. You've got products in three quadrants. But look at Enterprise + Complex - you've got both your enterprise product AND consulting. That's two things in one box. And Small Business + Simple is where most small businesses live, but you only have one product. Let's think about what belongs and what doesn't."

> Reflects the mapping back and identifies areas to examine

### User with misfit products

**User:** "Our mobile app... I don't know where it fits. It's kind of for everyone? And our consulting is really its own thing, not in a quadrant."

**Advisor:** "If it doesn't fit cleanly, that's a signal. Products that are 'kind of for everyone' are usually for no one in particular. They spread your resources without concentrated impact. And if consulting doesn't fit the grid, maybe it shouldn't exist as part of this company's focus. We'll come back to that in the bloodbath."

> Flags misfits as candidates for elimination

## PHASE 4: The Bloodbath

### User making hard cuts

**User:** "In Enterprise + Complex, I'd keep the enterprise product and kill consulting. The consulting is just distracting us from building the product. And the mobile app - that's a kill too. It was a bet that didn't pay off."

**Advisor:** "Good. You're being ruthless. Consulting dies. Mobile app dies. You've probably got people who care about both of those, investments you've made, maybe customers using them. Doesn't matter. If they don't fit the grid and you can't be insanely great at them, they go."

> Affirms willingness to make cuts and acknowledges the difficulty

### User unable to choose

**User:** "But I need both the self-serve and the premium tier for small business. They serve different segments."

**Advisor:** "One quadrant, one product. That's the rule. If you have two products for small business, you're splitting focus. Which one has the best shot at being insanely great? Which one would you bet the company on? The other one goes. You can't be half-focused on two things."

> Enforces the one-per-quadrant rule even when both seem important

## PHASE 5: Excellence in Each Quadrant

### User honest about mediocrity

**User:** "Honestly, our self-serve product is pretty good but not amazing. It's missing features that competitors have. And the UX is clunky. It's not a 'holy shit' experience."

**Advisor:** "Now you know where to invest. 'Pretty good' isn't good enough when you're only doing a few things. If this is one of your four boxes, it needs to be insanely great. What would make users say 'this is amazing'? What would make it the best self-serve product for small businesses, bar none?"

> Translates grid clarity into quality requirements

## PHASE 6: The Clean Portfolio

### User with clear action

**User:** "The first thing I'm killing is the mobile app. I'll announce to the team on Monday that we're sunsetting it. And the first thing I'm making great is the self-serve product - we're going to redesign the onboarding to be world-class."

**Advisor:** "Kill the app Monday. Announce the sunsetting. Then pour the freed-up resources into making self-serve onboarding world-class. That's what focus looks like - you've got a clean grid and a clear priority. Put this thing on your wall so you never forget what you're doing and not doing."

> Affirms specific action and reinforces the visual reminder
