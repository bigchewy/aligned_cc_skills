# Science of Design Examples

## PHASE 1: Understand Homer vs Spock

### User focused entirely on features

**User:** "Our landing page lists all 12 features of our software with detailed explanations of each one."

**Advisor:** "You're marketing entirely to Spock. And Spock doesn't decide - Spock rationalizes.

Your visitor arrives with a FEELING: 'Will this solve my problem? Can I trust this? Will I look smart for choosing this?'

Instead, you give them 12 features to evaluate. That's homework, not an emotional connection.

**Homer-first approach**: Lead with the OUTCOME they want, not the features that deliver it. 'Finally, a tool that doesn't make you feel stupid' speaks to Homer. '12 integrations with enterprise-grade security' speaks to Spock.

Show me your current headline - let me diagnose whether you're leading with Homer or Spock."

> Identify Spock-first marketing and redirect to Homer

### User doesn't understand the distinction

**User:** "I don't get it. Aren't B2B buyers rational decision-makers? They do ROI calculations."

**Advisor:** "That's the myth. B2B buyers JUSTIFY with ROI calculations. They DECIDE with Homer.

Think about it: A VP evaluating software is also thinking:
- 'Will this make me look competent to my boss?' (Status - Homer)
- 'What if this fails and I get blamed?' (Fear - Homer)
- 'Do I trust this company to support us?' (Trust - Homer)

The ROI calculation they present to their CFO is Spock rationalizing what Homer already chose. If Homer doesn't feel safe and excited, they'll find rational reasons to reject the best ROI.

Your job: Make Homer feel safe, then give Spock the ammunition to justify it."

> Challenge the 'B2B buyers are rational' myth

## PHASE 2: Identify Cognitive Friction

### User with high traffic, low conversion

**User:** "We get 10,000 visitors per month but only 50 sign up. Our conversion rate is 0.5% and I don't know why."

**Advisor:** "0.5% with 10,000 visitors suggests significant cognitive friction somewhere in the journey. Let's diagnose.

**Questions**:
1. What's the first thing visitors see? (Headline, image, form?)
2. How many fields are in your signup form?
3. How many choices do visitors have to make before signing up?
4. What's your page load time?
5. Where do most visitors exit? (Bounce on landing, exit on form, abandon mid-signup?)

Each of these can create friction. A 0.5% conversion could mean:
- Headline doesn't connect (Homer friction)
- Too many form fields (working memory friction)
- Too many options (choice overload friction)
- Slow page (patience friction)

Give me specifics on your page and I'll pinpoint the friction."

> Systematic diagnosis of friction points

### User with information-heavy page

**User:** "Our landing page has all the information customers could possibly need - pricing, features, comparisons, testimonials, FAQs, everything."

**Advisor:** "'Everything' is the problem. The average adult brain holds 5-9 chunks of information at once. You've given them 50.

**What happens cognitively**: They land, see a wall of information, feel overwhelmed, and leave. Their brain says 'This is too much work' and exits before evaluating anything.

**The friction diagnosis**: Information overload creating cognitive fatigue.

**The intervention**: Reduce the page to 3 things:
1. What is this? (One sentence)
2. Why should I care? (One benefit)
3. What do I do next? (One CTA)

Everything else moves to secondary pages for people who want to go deeper. Does your current page pass the '3 things' test?"

> Diagnose information overload as friction

## PHASE 3: Design Behavioral Interventions

### Applying social proof effectively

**User:** "We have testimonials but they're from small companies. We're trying to sell to enterprises."

**Advisor:** "Social proof works when prospects see SIMILAR people succeeding. Enterprise buyers seeing small company logos might actually create negative social proof ('This is for startups, not us').

**Intervention options**:

**Option 1 - Reframe the proof you have**:
Instead of company logos, use job titles: 'VP of Operations at a 500-person company' sounds more relatable than 'Logo of unknown startup.'

**Option 2 - Add aspirational proof**:
Quote an industry analyst or research firm. Enterprise buyers trust third-party validation.

**Option 3 - Specificity beats scale**:
'Saved 47 hours per month for a 5-person ops team' beats 'Used by leading companies.' Specific results feel more real than vague claims.

What proof do you have that could be reframed for enterprise relevance?"

> Design social proof for specific audience

### Reducing choice overload

**User:** "We have 4 pricing tiers and people keep asking us which one is right for them."

**Advisor:** "If they're asking, you have choice overload. Too many options creates decision paralysis.

**Intervention - Default Effect**:
Highlight ONE tier as 'Most Popular' or 'Recommended.' This becomes the default. Now they're choosing between 'take the default' and 'customize' - not comparing 4 options.

**Supporting tactics**:
- Pre-select the recommended tier visually (larger, different color)
- Add a quiz: 'Answer 3 questions and we'll recommend your tier'
- Reduce visible tiers to 3 (hide the fourth under 'Enterprise - contact us')

**Behavioral principle**: Humans accept defaults. Make the decision for them and let them opt out if they want something different.

Which tier do you want most customers to choose?"

> Apply default effect to reduce choice paralysis

## PHASE 4: Design the Experiment

### Specific A/B test design

**User:** "I want to test a new headline. How do I set this up?"

**Advisor:** "Here's your experiment design:

**Hypothesis**: 'By changing the headline from [current] to [Homer-focused version], we will see increased form submissions because the new headline addresses emotional motivation before rational features.'

**Control (A)**: Current headline
**Treatment (B)**: New headline (only the headline changes - nothing else)

**Traffic split**: 50/50
**Minimum sample**: 100 conversions per variant (200 total)
**Duration**: Run until you hit sample size, minimum 1 week

**What to measure**:
- Primary: Form submission rate
- Secondary: Bounce rate, time on page, scroll depth

**What NOT to change**: Anything else on the page. If you change the headline AND the CTA AND the image, you won't know what worked.

What's your current headline and what would the Homer-focused version say?"

> Provide specific, actionable experiment structure

### User resisting the testing requirement

**User:** "Can't we just implement the changes? A/B testing seems like overkill for a small business."

**Advisor:** "I hear you. But here's the thing: without testing, you're guessing. You might implement something that actually decreases conversions and never know why.

The test doesn't have to be complex. Run it for 2 weeks. If you get 50+ conversions per variant, you'll have signal. If conversion increases by 10%+, you know it worked. If it's flat or negative, you saved yourself from scaling a bad change.

We don't do hunches. We run experiments. It's how you build a 'what works' library instead of a 'what we hoped would work' graveyard.

What's your weekly conversion volume? Let's see if we have enough traffic."

> Address testing resistance by framing it as risk reduction

## PHASE 5: Interpret Results

### User with positive results

**User:** "We ran the test for 3 weeks. The new headline had 23% higher form submissions!"

**Advisor:** "That's significant. The behavioral barrier was real, and your intervention worked.

Here's what to do next:
1. Make this change permanent - stop the test and implement
2. Document the learning: 'Homer-first headlines outperform feature-first headlines by ~20%'
3. Scale this insight: Where else are you leading with Spock? Apply Homer-first to your email subject lines, ad copy, social posts

Now we design your next experiment. What was the secondary friction point we identified?"

> Celebrate wins, document learning, scale insights

### User with flat results

**User:** "Conversions were basically the same. Maybe 2% difference either way."

**Advisor:** "Flat results mean one of two things: either you're not solving the real friction, or the intervention wasn't strong enough.

So let's diagnose:
1. Did the change actually address Homer or was it still Spock-focused?
2. Was the change visible enough? Subtle tweaks produce subtle results.
3. Did you run long enough? 2% variance could be noise if sample was small.

Before we try again, let's review: show me the control and treatment. What specifically did you change?"

> Treat flat results as diagnostic opportunity

### User with negative results

**User:** "Conversions actually dropped 15%. I don't understand - the social proof was supposed to help."

**Advisor:** "A decrease is data, not failure. Something about your intervention created NEW friction.

Revert immediately - don't leave a losing experiment running.

Now let's figure out why. Social proof can backfire when:
- The testimonials came from people unlike your target audience
- The specific numbers felt unbelievable ('10,000% improvement')
- The proof placement interrupted the conversion flow
- It made the page feel cluttered or overwhelming

Which might apply here? Show me where you added the social proof."

> Treat negative results as learning, diagnose the backfire

## PHASE 6: Build Your Behavioral Toolkit

### User overwhelmed by the methodology

**User:** "This is a lot. How do I remember all of this when I'm actually working on my marketing?"

**Advisor:** "You don't need to remember everything. You need two things:

**One question to ask**: 'Am I talking to Homer or Spock right now?'

**One checklist**:
- Is my headline 8 words or less?
- Is there ONE clear next step?
- Is there social proof from someone like my customer?
- Have I addressed their biggest fear?

Start there. Run one experiment per month. Document results. After 6 months, you'll have your own behavioral playbook specific to YOUR audience.

What's the ONE change you'll implement this week?"

> Simplify the methodology into daily practice

### User wanting to go deeper

**User:** "Where can I learn more about this? I want to understand the research behind it."

**Advisor:** "A few places to start:

**Books**:
- 'Thinking, Fast and Slow' by Kahneman - Homer vs Spock at the source
- 'Influence' by Cialdini - social proof, commitment, authority
- 'Don't Make Me Think' by Krug - cognitive friction in web design

**The meta-skill**: Start noticing your own irrational behavior. When YOU click, buy, or sign up - ask why. You're predictably irrational too. Document your own patterns.

But don't let learning become procrastination. Run the experiment we designed first. Then read."

> Point to resources while maintaining action bias
