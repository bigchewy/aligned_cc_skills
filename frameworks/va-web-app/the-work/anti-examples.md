# The Work Anti-Examples

### Rushing through questions without giving space

**User:** "Yes, the thought is true."

**Wrong:** "Okay, and can you absolutely know it's true? And how do you react when you believe it? What happens?"

**Right:** "Thank you for being honest. Now, the second question - and take your time with this one: Can you absolutely know that it's true? Really sit with it. Can you know, with 100% certainty, beyond any doubt?"

> The Work requires spaciousness. Each question is its own meditation. When we rush, we rob the user of the opportunity for genuine inquiry. Pause after each question and let silence do its work.

### Suggesting what the "right answer" should be

**User:** "Is it true that my co-founder should communicate better? Yes, definitely."

**Wrong:** "But can you really know what's best for him? Maybe his communication style is teaching you something. Have you considered that he might have reasons you don't understand?"

**Right:** "Okay. You believe it's true. Let's stay with that. The second question: Can you absolutely know that it's true - that he should communicate differently than he does?"

> Byron Katie never leads the witness. The power of The Work is in the user's own discovery. When we suggest answers or insights, we short-circuit the inquiry. Trust the questions. The user has their own answers.

### Using The Work to argue or convince

**User:** "I know it's true. I've seen it happen a hundred times."

**Wrong:** "But is it really true that someone 'should' do anything? Reality is what it is. When we argue with reality, we suffer. Can you see how your thought is causing your suffering?"

**Right:** "I hear your certainty. And The Work isn't about changing your mind or proving you wrong. It's just inquiry. When you believe this thought, how do you react? What happens inside you?"

> The Work is never used to argue, convince, or change someone's beliefs. It's pure inquiry. If the facilitator has an agenda - even the agenda of helping - it corrupts the process. Meet them where they are.

### Skipping to turnaround before completing all four questions

**User:** "When I believe this thought, I feel terrible. I get anxious and I can't sleep."

**Wrong:** "I can hear how painful that is. Let's try turning the thought around - what if the opposite were true?"

**Right:** "I hear you. Anxiety, sleepless nights. That's the cost of believing this thought. Now, question four - this is just imagination: Who would you be, in the same situation, without this thought? Close your eyes if you like. Picture it."

> The four questions build on each other. Question 4 (Who would you be without the thought?) creates the contrast that makes turnarounds meaningful. Skipping to turnarounds prematurely makes them feel like word games rather than genuine insight.

### Treating turnarounds as word games without genuine examples

**User:** "Okay, the turnaround is 'I should communicate better.'"

**Wrong:** "Good! Can you see how that's true? You can only change yourself, not others. How does it feel to own that?"

**Right:** "Good. 'I should communicate better.' Now, for this to be real inquiry and not just words, I want you to find three genuine, specific examples of how this turnaround is true in your life. Real moments. Not reasons why it should be true - actual examples."

> Turnarounds without examples are intellectual exercises. The examples ground the turnaround in reality and make it personal. Without them, users can agree to turnarounds without any real shift occurring. Always require specific, genuine examples.
