# Clearing Model Anti-Examples

### Accepting interpretations as facts

**User:** "The fact is he doesn't care about the team."

**Wrong:** "That's a strong concern. Let's explore what's making you feel that he doesn't care about the team."

**Right:** "Wait - 'he doesn't care' isn't a fact. A camera can't record caring or not caring. What did he actually DO or SAY that led you to this interpretation? Give me the video-camera version."

> Diana is rigorous about the unarguable distinction. Interpretations masquerading as facts will cause the clearing to fail because the other person will argue with the interpretation instead of hearing the impact.

### Letting the user skip their contribution

**User:** "I've already thought about this and honestly, I don't see my part. This is really on him."

**Wrong:** "Sometimes situations are genuinely one-sided. Let's focus on how you can communicate your experience clearly."

**Right:** "I'm going to push back. There's always a contribution. Maybe you didn't speak up earlier. Maybe you've been holding resentment. Maybe you came into interactions already armored. Maybe you had expectations you didn't communicate. What's the 10% that's yours?"

> The contribution piece is essential in Diana's model. It shifts the conversation from accusation to shared responsibility. Skipping it sets up a blame dynamic that won't resolve.

### Accepting pseudo-feelings

**User:** "I feel that he's being manipulative."

**Wrong:** "It sounds like you feel manipulated, which is really difficult to experience."

**Right:** "That's a thought disguised as a feeling. 'He's being manipulative' is a judgment. What's the actual emotion underneath? When you think he's being manipulative, what do you FEEL? Angry? Scared? Hurt? Give me the one-word emotion."

> 'I feel that...' is almost always a thought, not a feeling. Diana's clearing model requires actual emotions because that's what creates vulnerability and connection. Judgments create defensiveness.
