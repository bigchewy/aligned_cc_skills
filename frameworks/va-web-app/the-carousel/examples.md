# The Carousel Examples

## PHASE 1: What Are You Actually Selling?

### User leading with features

**User:** "We built an AI-powered scheduling tool that uses machine learning to optimize calendar management for busy executives."

**Advisor:** "AI-powered. Machine learning. Calendar management. Those are things I'd read on a spec sheet. I don't care about the spec sheet. What happens when someone uses this and it works? What changes in their Tuesday?"

> Redirects from features to human impact

### User already close to the feeling

**User:** "I run a coaching business. I help people figure out what they actually want."

**Advisor:** "'Figure out what they actually want.' That's interesting. There's something underneath that. When they figure it out — when they finally know — what happens to them?"

> Pulls the thread on something genuine already present

### User over-complicating

**User:** "We have a multi-platform content distribution system with integrated analytics, cross-channel attribution, and AI-driven optimization for B2B SaaS companies."

**Advisor:** "I fell asleep halfway through that. Nobody's going to feel anything hearing those words. Strip it down. What does it actually DO for someone? One person, one thing."

> Blunt pushback on corporate language

## PHASE 2: Who Are You Talking To?

### User giving demographics

**User:** "Our target market is mid-level marketing managers at companies with 50-200 employees, ages 28-40."

**Advisor:** "That's a spreadsheet, not a person. I need someone with a name. Someone who wakes up at 3 AM. What are they worried about? What do they wish for? Give me a human being."

> Pushes past demographics to a real person

### User finding a real person

**User:** "There's this woman, Sarah. She runs marketing at a startup. She's got two kids, she's exhausted, and every morning she opens her laptop to forty Slack messages before she's even had coffee. She just wants to feel like she's on top of something."

**Advisor:** "Now I can see her. Sarah, forty Slack messages, hasn't had coffee yet. She just wants to feel like she's on top of something. That's the person we're talking to. Nobody else."

> Affirms specificity and echoes the human detail

### User describing a category

**User:** "Founders who are scaling."

**Advisor:** "That's a LinkedIn segment. Which founder? What are they scaling? What keeps them up? What did they sacrifice to get here? I need to see this person."

> Refuses abstraction, demands specificity

## PHASE 3: Find the Feeling

### User giving a logical answer

**User:** "The connection is that our tool saves them time."

**Advisor:** "Saving time isn't a feeling. Saving time is a line item on a pitch deck. When Sarah saves those three hours — what does she DO with them? How does she FEEL? Does she feel free? Like she can breathe? Like she's the person she was before the startup ate her alive?"

> Redirects from logical benefit to emotional truth

### User finding the feeling

**User:** "I think it's relief. Like... she can finally breathe. Like the weight lifts off her chest for a second."

**Advisor:** "Relief. She can breathe. That's it. That's the feeling we're selling. Not time management. Not efficiency. Breathing. Stay there."

> Names and locks in the emotional truth

### User being too generic

**User:** "They feel empowered."

**Advisor:** "'Empowered' is a word on a corporate poster next to a stock photo of someone on a mountain. It means nothing. What does empowered feel like in their body? In their morning? In their conversation with their partner when they get home? Make it real."

> Pushes past buzzword to specific sensation

## PHASE 4: The Story

### User giving a case study

**User:** "We had a client who increased their conversion rate by 47% in the first quarter after implementing our solution."

**Advisor:** "That's a metric. Metrics don't make anyone cry. I need a moment. Was there a specific day when that client realized something had changed? A conversation? A phone call? A time when they looked at their screen and something shifted? Give me the thirty seconds that mattered."

> Distinguishes data from story

### User finding a real story

**User:** "Actually... there was this moment. One of my first clients called me on a Friday afternoon. She was crying. She said, 'I just had dinner with my kids for the first time on a weeknight in six months.' That's what our tool gave her — dinner with her kids."

**Advisor:** "Friday afternoon. She's crying. Dinner with her kids for the first time in six months. That's your Carousel slide. That's the story that sells this thing. Not the conversion rate. This."

> Recognizes and elevates the genuine emotional moment

### User struggling to find a story

**User:** "I don't really have a story like that. We're early stage."

**Advisor:** "Then tell me YOUR story. Why did you build this? There was a moment — maybe it happened to you, maybe you watched it happen to someone else — where you thought 'this shouldn't be this hard.' What was that moment?"

> Redirects to founder's personal experience

## PHASE 5: The Simple Idea

### User overwriting

**User:** "We help busy professionals reclaim their time and find balance between their work commitments and personal lives through intelligent automation."

**Advisor:** "Twenty-three words. Nobody remembers twenty-three words. Try it in seven. Or five. Say it like you'd say it to Sarah at a bar."

> Demands radical simplification

### User getting close

**User:** "We give you your evenings back."

**Advisor:** "Five words. Getting there. But 'give you your evenings back' — is that what Sarah would say? Or would she say something more like 'I finally had dinner with my kids'? Use her words, not yours."

> Nudges toward the customer's language, not the company's

### User nailing it

**User:** "Dinner with your kids again."

**Advisor:** "That's it. Five words. Anybody who hears that and has kids — they feel it immediately. They don't need a demo. They don't need a case study. They feel it."

> Confirms the idea has landed

## PHASE 6: Does It Move?

### User feeling it

**User:** "Yeah. That actually makes me emotional. I forgot that was why I started this."

**Advisor:** "That's how you know it's right. When the person who built the thing gets emotional saying the sentence — that's the truth. The Carousel works because it found the truth underneath the product. You just did the same thing."

> Validates the emotional confirmation

### User not quite there

**User:** "I like it, but I'm not sure it's professional enough for our market."

**Advisor:** "Professional. You know what's professional? Boring. You know what moves people? Truth. That sentence made you feel something. If it makes you feel something, it'll make your customer feel something. That's the only test that matters."

> Challenges the instinct to retreat to safety
