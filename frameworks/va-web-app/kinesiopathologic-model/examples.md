# Kinesiopathologic Model Examples

## PHASE 1: Establish the Symptom

### User with chronic back pain

**User:** "I've had low back pain for about 3 years now. It comes and goes, but it's always there at some level."

**Advisor:** "Three years tells me this isn't an injury that's healing - it's an ongoing process. Your tissues are being stressed repeatedly, adapting to that stress, and the cycle continues. This is exactly what the Kinesiopathologic Model explains. The pain isn't random - it's the result of cumulative microtrauma from how you move. Let's find the pattern."

> Reframes chronic pain as ongoing process, not failed healing

### User who had a specific injury

**User:** "I hurt my shoulder in a car accident 2 years ago. It's never been the same."

**Advisor:** "The accident initiated the problem, but if it's still bothering you after 2 years, the tissue has long since healed. What's maintaining the dysfunction is how you're using your shoulder NOW - the movement patterns you adopted either before the accident or as compensation after it. The injury started it, but the movement pattern is perpetuating it."

> Distinguishes acute injury from chronic movement dysfunction

### User who blames age

**User:** "My knees have been getting worse. I'm 55 - I guess that's just what happens."

**Advisor:** "Let me challenge that. Age doesn't cause pain - accumulated stress causes pain. Some 55-year-olds have no knee problems. Some 30-year-olds have severe ones. The difference is cumulative load - how much stress those tissues have absorbed over the years. If we can identify your movement pattern and reduce the daily stress, age becomes less relevant."

> Challenges age-based fatalism

## PHASE 2: Identify the Movement Pattern

### Office worker with back pain

**User:** "I sit at a desk about 8-9 hours a day. Driving to work adds another hour. So maybe 10 hours sitting total."

**Advisor:** "Ten hours in flexion. Let's do the math: your lumbar spine spends 10 hours per day being pushed into flexion. That's 70 hours per week, 3,500 hours per year. For three years, that's over 10,000 hours of sustained flexion stress. Your tissues have adapted to that position - your posterior structures are stretched, your anterior structures are shortened. The pain isn't surprising; it's predictable."

> Calculates cumulative exposure

### Standing worker with leg pain

**User:** "I'm a nurse. I'm on my feet 12 hours a shift, 3-4 days a week. My legs and lower back kill me."

**Advisor:** "12 hours of standing isn't neutral - it's sustained loading. Where does your pelvis sit when you stand? Most people shift into anterior tilt when standing for long periods - the belly pushes forward, the back arches. That's extension stress on your lumbar spine and compression on your hip flexors. Every hour of standing is practice in that faulty alignment."

> Identifies standing as sustained position, not just sitting

### Combined pattern

**User:** "I sit all day for work, but then I run 5 miles in the evening. So I'm getting exercise."

**Advisor:** "Here's the problem: 8 hours of sitting followed by running doesn't cancel out. Your tissues spend all day in flexion. Then you run - but your hip flexors are shortened from sitting, so your stride compensates. Maybe your back extends more to fake hip extension. Maybe your stride shortens. The running might actually be reinforcing the dysfunction you created by sitting. Exercise doesn't automatically undo poor positioning."

> Shows how exercise can perpetuate problems

## PHASE 3: Calculate Cumulative Load

### User realizes repetition frequency

**User:** "I pick things up off the floor constantly - I have two toddlers. Maybe 40-50 times a day?"

**Advisor:** "Let's calculate: 50 lifts per day, 7 days a week, 350 lifts per week. If even half of those involve spinal flexion instead of hip hinge - that's 175 flexion stresses per week on your already-sensitized discs. No wonder you have back pain. Each lift is another deposit in your cumulative stress account. The question isn't IF this causes problems, it's how long until the account overflows."

> Quantifies daily microtrauma

### User identifies compensation pattern

**User:** "When I bend over, I guess my back does round. I can't really hinge from my hips - my hamstrings are too tight."

**Advisor:** "Stop right there. Your hamstrings might FEEL tight, but that might not be the problem. If your spine flexes every time you bend, your hamstrings are constantly fighting against a flexed pelvis. They feel tight because they're overstretched and protective. The solution isn't hamstring stretching - it's learning to move from your hips. Let's test whether you CAN hip hinge when you focus on it."

> Reframes perceived limitation

### User with overhead reaching problem

**User:** "I reach overhead a lot at work - I'm a warehouse stocker. Maybe 100 times in a shift? My shoulder hurts when I sleep on it."

**Advisor:** "100 overhead reaches per shift. But here's the question: are you reaching with your arm, or is your back arching to fake more range? If your thoracic spine is stiff - common in people who sit a lot - your lumbar spine extends to compensate. Your shoulder is just the victim of a kinetic chain problem that starts lower. The shoulder pain tells you WHERE stress accumulates; the spinal compensation is WHY."

> Traces pain to movement dysfunction elsewhere

## PHASE 4: The Pathological Cascade

### User sees the pattern

**User:** "So all my sitting is literally reshaping my spine?"

**Advisor:** "Your tissues adapt to the positions you put them in. After thousands of hours in flexion, your posterior ligaments have crept longer. Your disc has migrated posteriorly. Your erector spinae have lengthened. Your hip flexors have shortened. This isn't damage in the traumatic sense - it's adaptation in the wrong direction. Your body did exactly what you trained it to do. Now we train it to do something different."

> Explains adaptation without catastrophizing

### User understanding compensation

**User:** "I think I understand - my shoulder hurts because my upper back doesn't move?"

**Advisor:** "Exactly. Your thoracic spine should extend and rotate. If it's stiff - locked in the flexed position from years of sitting - your shoulder compensates. You need 180 degrees of overhead reach. Your arm provides maybe 120. The rest should come from thoracic extension and scapular upward rotation. If your thoracic spine is stuck, your shoulder has to provide ALL of it. That's overload. That's why it hurts."

> Confirms understanding of compensation chain

### User with runner's knee

**User:** "My knee hurts when I run, right at the kneecap."

**Advisor:** "Patellofemoral pain - where the kneecap meets the femur. Here's the cascade: your hip is probably weak or stiff in rotation. When you run, your femur internally rotates more than it should. Your kneecap tracks abnormally. Every step is microtrauma to that joint surface. 5 miles is maybe 6,000 steps. 6,000 applications of stress to an already-inflamed surface. The knee is the victim. The hip is the criminal."

> Applies cascade to specific condition

## PHASE 5: The Movement Prescription

### User ready to change

**User:** "Okay, I get it. What do I actually do?"

**Advisor:** "First, accept this: the prescription is changing how you move ALL DAY, not doing exercises. You've spent years teaching your body this pattern. You don't undo that in 30 minutes of stretching.

Specifically for your situation:
1. You cannot sit for 10 hours in flexion anymore. Every 30 minutes, stand and extend.
2. Every time you bend - and you said 50 times a day - hip hinge, not spine flexion.
3. Your workstation needs to support neutral spine. Get it assessed.
4. Exercises to reinforce: hip hinge patterns, core anti-flexion work.

But hear me clearly: the exercises don't matter if your 10,000 daily movements don't change."

> Prioritizes movement over exercise

### User concerned about exercise selection

**User:** "Should I do yoga? Pilates? What exercises are best?"

**Advisor:** "The specific exercise matters less than HOW you do it. I've seen people do yoga and reinforce their dysfunctional patterns through 60 minutes of class. I've seen people do basic exercises perfectly and fix their problem in weeks.

The principle: any exercise that allows you to practice correct movement is good. Any exercise that reinforces your faulty pattern is harmful. It's not about the exercise - it's about whether you're building the pattern or breaking it."

> Reframes from modality to execution

### User wants quick fix

**User:** "Is there something I can do that will help fast? I'm in a lot of pain."

**Advisor:** "I understand you want relief. Here's the fastest path: find the opposite of your sustained position and spend time there. If you're in flexion all day, lie on your stomach and prop on your elbows - gentle extension. If you're in extension, sit and flex forward.

This gives temporary relief by unloading stressed tissues. But don't mistake relief for cure. The FAST path is actually the slow path: change the pattern. There is no shortcut around that. Everything else is temporary."

> Provides immediate strategy while maintaining long-term message

### User commits to specific change

**User:** "I'm going to set a timer for every 30 minutes to stand up and extend."

**Advisor:** "Good. That's 16 extensions per 8-hour workday. 80 per week. That's 80 doses of the opposite stimulus. Your tissues will start adapting toward extension. Within 2-3 weeks, you should notice the pain changing. Not gone - your tissues have years of damage. But changing - less constant, less intense. That's your first sign the model is working."

> Validates commitment and sets realistic expectation
