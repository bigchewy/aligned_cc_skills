# Leap of Faith Assumptions Examples

## PHASE 1: The Setup

### User with implicit assumptions

**User:** "We're building a marketplace connecting home chefs with busy professionals who want healthy meals."

**Advisor:** "Let me tell you about Dropbox. Drew Houston didn't build the product first - he made a video to test whether people wanted it. The waitlist went from 5,000 to 75,000 overnight. He tested his leap of faith before building. Your marketplace has a stack of assumptions in it that you might not even see yet. Let's surface them before you build anything."

> Sets up the framework with the Dropbox example

### User who already knows the key assumption

**User:** "I think the biggest assumption is whether busy professionals will trust food from stranger's kitchens."

**Advisor:** "You've already identified a major leap of faith. Trust in stranger-cooked food. That's specific and testable. Before we dig into that one, let's make sure we've surfaced all the assumptions. Your business depends on more than trust - it depends on supply, demand, pricing, logistics. Let's list everything before we prioritize."

> Acknowledges their insight but ensures comprehensive assumption surfacing

## PHASE 2: Surfacing Assumptions

### User listing surface assumptions

**User:** "We assume people want healthy meals and are too busy to cook."

**Advisor:** "Those are too easy. Everyone would agree with those. What are the assumptions that might NOT be true? 'Busy professionals would order from stranger's kitchens instead of restaurants.' 'Home chefs would want to cook for strangers for the price we can offer.' 'Health-conscious people would trust food without nutritional labels.' Give me the assumptions that make you nervous."

> Pushes past obvious assumptions to ones that could actually be wrong

### User getting to real assumptions

**User:** "Okay: that home chefs can reliably produce food at restaurant-like consistency. That professionals will pay $15+ for a home-cooked meal when they could DoorDash for similar price. That we can solve the last-mile delivery problem without destroying the economics. That the health department won't shut us down immediately."

**Advisor:** "Now we're getting somewhere. Consistency, pricing against substitutes, unit economics on delivery, regulatory risk. Each of those could kill your business if you're wrong. And right now, you don't know which ones are actually valid. Let's categorize these."

> Validates specific, falsifiable assumptions

## PHASE 3: Value and Growth Hypotheses

### User conflating value and growth

**User:** "If we make great food, customers will tell their friends and we'll grow virally."

**Advisor:** "You're conflating two different hypotheses. The value hypothesis is: will customers actually use and love this product? The growth hypothesis is: how will new customers discover it? Great food doesn't automatically create viral growth - that's a separate assumption. Which of your assumptions are about whether customers will value the product, and which are about how you'll find new customers?"

> Separates value from growth assumptions

### User categorizing correctly

**User:** "Value: professionals will pay $15+ for home-cooked meals and trust the food quality. Growth: we'll get initial customers through food blogger partnerships and grow through referrals from satisfied customers."

**Advisor:** "Good. Now you can see they're separate bets. You could nail the value hypothesis - customers love the food - but fail on growth because referrals don't actually happen at scale. Or vice versa. Both have to work. Which is riskier? Which one, if wrong, would invalidate everything?"

> Validates categorization and sets up prioritization

## PHASE 4: The Riskiest Assumption

### User picking the easiest to test

**User:** "The riskiest is whether the food will be good. We should test that first since it's the foundation."

**Advisor:** "Is food quality really the riskiest, or just the easiest to imagine testing? You can make the food good - that's execution. But will people trust strangers' kitchens? Will they pay $15 when Uber Eats is the same price for restaurant food? Those feel riskier to me because they're about customer behavior you can't control. If I gave you $100 to bet on which assumption would turn out false, where would you bet?"

> Challenges easy assumption choice with the betting test

### User identifying true risk

**User:** "Honestly, the riskiest is whether people will trust home kitchen food. We could make great food and still fail if the trust barrier is too high. That's the real leap of faith."

**Advisor:** "That's it. The trust assumption is your biggest leap of faith. Great food doesn't matter if customers never try it due to trust. Everything else depends on that being validated. Now - how do we test it without building a full marketplace?"

> Validates the risky assumption identification and transitions to testing

## PHASE 5: The Experiment Design

### User proposing to just build the product

**User:** "We should build an MVP marketplace and see if people sign up."

**Advisor:** "That's months of work and still doesn't directly test trust. What's your Dropbox video? Drew didn't build file syncing to test demand - he made a video. Can you test whether people would trust home kitchen food without building a marketplace? What if you ran a pop-up dinner? Posted on Nextdoor offering home-cooked meals? Created a landing page that asked 'Would you pay $15 for a healthy meal cooked in a neighbor's kitchen' and measured actual sign-ups with a payment intent?"

> Pushes for lightweight experiment over building

### User designing minimal experiment

**User:** "We could do a Nextdoor experiment: offer 10 home-cooked meals at $15 to neighbors, handle payment through Venmo, see who actually buys and eats the food. Zero technology, maximum learning about trust."

**Advisor:** "That's it. No marketplace needed. You'll learn if real people will actually pay for and eat food from a stranger's kitchen. If 10 people from Nextdoor won't do it, why would thousands on your platform? And you can run this experiment in a week. That's the leap of faith assumption tested with minimum effort."

> Validates minimum viable experiment design

## PHASE 6: The Commitment

### User committing to experiment

**User:** "I'll post on Nextdoor this weekend offering 10 meals. I'll price at $15, accept Venmo, and deliver myself. If 7 out of 10 sell and people actually eat the food and respond positively, the trust hypothesis is validated."

**Advisor:** "Now you have a test. Specific hypothesis: trust isn't a barrier at $15 for neighbor-cooked food. Experiment: 10 meals on Nextdoor. Success metric: 7+ sales and positive feedback. Timeline: this weekend. If you validate, you have evidence to move forward. If you don't, you've saved months of building the wrong thing. Every day you spend building is a day operating on faith instead of evidence."

> Confirms complete experiment design with all elements

### User finding invalidating result

**User:** "We tried the Nextdoor experiment. Only 2 people bought, and both asked a ton of questions about food safety first."

**Advisor:** "That's invaluable learning. Your leap of faith assumption - that trust wouldn't be a barrier - was invalidated. Two sales out of however many saw the post, and even those two were skeptical. The trust barrier is real. Now you know before building a marketplace. The question is: can you solve the trust problem, or should you pivot to a different model where trust is less central?"

> Handles invalidating result as valuable learning
