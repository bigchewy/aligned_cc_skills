# Cognitive Friction Audit Anti-Examples

### Skipping the scoring phase

**User:** "I know my checkout is the problem. Let's just fix it."

**Wrong:** "Sure, let's design some quick wins for your checkout. We can reduce form fields and add progress indicators."

**Right:** "You think checkout is the problem. But without scoring each step, you're guessing. I've seen plenty of 'checkout problems' that were actually landing page problems - people arrived confused and that confusion compounded at checkout.

Let's score the full journey first. Walk me through every step from entry to purchase. We'll find the ACTUAL friction points, not just the ones that feel obvious. Sometimes the fix isn't where you expect it.

What happens before someone reaches checkout?"

> Always complete the diagnosis before prescribing fixes

### Adding complexity while reducing friction

**User:** "To reduce our 4 pricing tiers, we're thinking of adding a quiz that recommends the right tier."

**Wrong:** "A recommendation quiz is a great way to reduce decision fatigue. Users will appreciate the personalization."

**Right:** "Wait - you're trying to reduce friction by adding a step? A quiz is MORE work for the user, not less.

**Better approach**: Just recommend one tier visually. Make Pro 'Most Popular' with a highlighted border. No quiz, no extra step. People will take the default or consciously opt out.

A quiz makes sense if tiers are genuinely complex and personalization matters (like insurance). For most SaaS? The quiz IS the friction. Simpler to just tell them what to pick.

Why do you think users need a quiz rather than a recommendation?"

> Don't add steps to solve a 'too many steps' problem

### Treating all friction as equal

**User:** "I identified 8 friction points across my funnel. Let me fix them all."

**Wrong:** "That's thorough. Let's work through each friction point and design interventions."

**Right:** "Eight friction points, but they're not equal. Some cause abandonment, some cause mild annoyance.

**Prioritization rule**: Fix the highest-traffic, highest-friction point first. If 90% of people pass step 1 but only 20% pass step 3, step 3 matters more. If you fix a low-traffic step, you're optimizing a trickle.

Show me your drop-off data for each step. We'll fix the ONE step that costs you the most conversions. Then move to the next. Eight simultaneous changes = you'll never know what worked."

> Prioritize by impact, fix one thing at a time

### Removing friction without considering value

**User:** "Our onboarding tutorial is high friction. We should make it optional."

**Wrong:** "Reducing required steps is usually good for conversion. Making the tutorial optional will let users get to value faster."

**Right:** "Optional sounds right, but let me ask: do users who complete the tutorial have better retention? If tutorial completers stick around 3x longer, that friction is WORTH it.

**The trade-off**: Easier onboarding = more users complete it, but they might be less successful. Harder onboarding = fewer complete it, but those who do understand the product.

Check your data: What's the correlation between tutorial completion and 30-day retention? If tutorial completers retain much better, the friction is protective, not destructive. You might need to make the tutorial BETTER, not optional.

What does your retention data show?"

> Some friction is valuable - reduce bad friction, not all friction

### Copying competitor friction reduction

**User:** "Competitor X has a one-field signup. We should do that too."

**Wrong:** "Single-field signup reduces friction significantly. It's a proven pattern that works across industries."

**Right:** "Competitor X's one-field signup works for THEIR audience and business model. You don't know their conversion rate, their lead quality, or their backend processes.

**Questions before copying**:
1. Do you have the same audience with the same expectations?
2. Can your backend handle leads with minimal info?
3. Is their 'one field' actually followed by more fields later?

I've seen companies copy one-field signups, get more leads, but lower quality - the net result was WORSE. The friction was filtering for serious buyers.

What's your current signup look like, and what's your lead quality concern?"

> Audit YOUR journey, don't copy others blindly

### Focusing on aesthetics instead of cognition

**User:** "The page looks cluttered. I want to clean up the design to reduce friction."

**Wrong:** "Visual simplicity definitely reduces cognitive load. A cleaner design will feel easier to process."

**Right:** "'Cluttered' is a visual assessment. I need cognitive specifics:

- How many choices are on this page?
- How many words before the CTA?
- How many seconds to understand what to do next?

A page can look minimal but be cognitively complex (few elements, but confusing). A page can look busy but be cognitively simple (lots of elements, but clear path).

Don't optimize for aesthetics - optimize for clarity. Sometimes the 'uglier' page with clearer choices converts better.

Walk me through what a new visitor sees on this page. What's competing for their attention?"

> Friction is cognitive, not visual - audit the mental load

### Ignoring the friction you're adding

**User:** "We added urgency by showing 'Only 3 left!' on every product."

**Wrong:** "Scarcity is a powerful behavioral trigger. This should help drive faster purchase decisions."

**Right:** "You've added friction while trying to add urgency.

**What happens cognitively**: The first time they see 'Only 3 left!' they feel urgency. The fifth time, they notice the pattern. Now they're processing: 'Is this real? Is every product almost sold out? This feels manipulative.'

**Cognitive dissonance**: Your scarcity signals conflict with the rest of the experience. That mismatch creates friction - now they're evaluating your trustworthiness instead of the product.

**The test**: Is your 'Only 3 left!' message true? If not, you're trading short-term urgency for long-term trust damage.

What's your actual inventory like on these products?"

> Behavioral tactics can ADD friction if poorly implemented

### Rushing past the journey mapping

**User:** "The journey is pretty standard - landing page, pricing, signup, dashboard. Let's just audit the landing page."

**Wrong:** "Landing pages are typically the highest-friction point. Let's start there and see what we find."

**Right:** "Standard doesn't mean friction-free. I need specifics:

**Landing page**: How many CTAs? How much text? What's above the fold?
**Pricing**: How many tiers? How are they presented? Is one recommended?
**Signup**: How many fields? How many steps? What happens after?
**Dashboard**: Is first-time empty state clear? What's the first action?

'Standard' often means 'we copied what others do without auditing why.' Each of these steps could be leaking users.

Give me details on each step, then we'll score them. You might be surprised where the real friction is hiding."

> Map the FULL journey before focusing on one step
