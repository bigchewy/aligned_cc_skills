# Opposite Action Examples

## PHASE 1: Identify the Emotion

### User naming vague emotion

**User:** "I just feel bad about everything."

**Advisor:** "Bad is a cover word. Underneath 'bad' there's usually something more specific - fear, anger, sadness, shame, guilt. Each of these has different action urges.

Is it more like fear - anxious, worried, scared? Or more like sadness - heavy, withdrawn, hopeless? Or anger - frustrated, resentful, irritated? Or shame - wanting to hide, feeling exposed?

Which one fits best?"

> Helps differentiate the specific emotion

### User conflating thoughts and emotions

**User:** "I feel like I'm going to get fired."

**Advisor:** "That's a thought, not a feeling. 'I'm going to get fired' is a prediction about the future. The emotion is what that prediction creates in you.

When you think 'I'm going to get fired,' what do you feel? Fear? Anxiety? Dread? Shame?"

> Clarifies the distinction

### User is open

**User:** "I'm feeling really anxious about this presentation tomorrow. On a scale, maybe 70 out of 100."

**Advisor:** "Anxiety at 70. That's significant - definitely affecting your thinking and behavior.

Now let's get clear on what anxiety is urging you to do."

> Validates and moves forward

## PHASE 2: Identify the Action Urge

### User not seeing the urge

**User:** "I don't have an urge to do anything. I just feel anxious."

**Advisor:** "Every emotion comes with an urge, even if it's subtle. Anxiety typically urges us to avoid, escape, or obsessively prepare. Anger urges attack. Sadness urges withdrawal.

What does your anxiety want you to do about the presentation? Cancel it? Over-prepare until 3am? Rehearse until you're exhausted? Avoid thinking about it altogether?"

> Names common anxiety urges

### User seeing multiple urges

**User:** "I want to call in sick. But I also want to stay up all night preparing. They're opposite."

**Advisor:** "Both are anxiety-driven. Calling in sick is escape. Staying up all night is excessive preparation. Both are the emotion running the show.

Which urge is stronger right now? Which one are you most likely to act on?"

> Shows both can be problematic

### User is open

**User:** "The anxiety is urging me to avoid it. Cancel the meeting, say I'm sick, anything to not have to stand up there."

**Advisor:** "So the action urge is avoidance - escape the situation. That's what fear typically drives.

Now let's check whether that urge fits the facts."

> Confirms and moves to fact-checking

## PHASE 3: Check the Facts

### User catastrophizing

**User:** "The facts are that I'm going to mess up and everyone will see I'm incompetent."

**Advisor:** "Those aren't facts - those are predictions. Facts are things that have already happened or are objectively true right now.

What are the actual facts? Have you prepared? Have you given presentations before? How did those go? What's the realistic probability of total failure versus just being nervous?"

> Distinguishes predictions from facts

### User minimizing real risk

**User:** "I mean, I guess it's not that big a deal. I'm probably overreacting."

**Advisor:** "Maybe. But let's not dismiss your emotion without checking. Sometimes anxiety is actually telling you something important.

What's the real situation? Is there a genuine threat here, or is your anxiety responding to a perceived threat that's not real?"

> Balances - doesn't just invalidate the emotion

### User is open

**User:** "Okay, facts: I've prepared for this. I've given similar presentations before and they went okay - not perfect, but okay. The worst realistic outcome is probably that I'm nervous and stumble a bit, not career-ending disaster."

**Advisor:** "Good fact-checking. You're prepared. You have a track record of 'okay.' The catastrophic outcome you're imagining isn't the realistic outcome.

So does your anxiety at 70 fit the actual facts of this situation?"

> Confirms realistic assessment

## PHASE 4: Evaluate the Action Urge

### User still defending avoidance

**User:** "But avoiding would relieve the anxiety. That's helpful, right?"

**Advisor:** "In the short term, yes. Avoidance always relieves anxiety immediately. That's why we do it.

But what happens long term? The next presentation will be scarier because you avoided this one. The relief is borrowed - you pay it back with interest later.

Would avoiding move you toward your goals and values, or away from them?"

> Shows the long-term cost of avoidance

### User seeing the pattern

**User:** "Acting on the urge would keep me stuck. I always avoid and then I never get more comfortable."

**Advisor:** "You've identified the pattern. Avoidance maintains anxiety over time. Each escape teaches your brain 'this is dangerous' rather than 'I can handle this.'

Since the emotion doesn't fit the facts and acting on it would make things worse, this is a case for Opposite Action."

> Confirms the case for opposite action

### User is open

**User:** "The urge to avoid doesn't fit - I know I can handle this. And avoiding would just make the pattern worse."

**Advisor:** "Right. The emotion is firing, but it doesn't match reality. And the urge would take you away from your values.

That's when we use Opposite Action. Let's identify what that would be."

> Transitions to opposite action

## PHASE 5: Identify the Opposite Action

### User unsure what opposite means

**User:** "What's the opposite of avoiding? Going anyway?"

**Advisor:** "Going anyway is part of it, but opposite action is more than just doing the thing. The opposite of avoidance is approach. Not just showing up, but approaching fully - engaging, participating, putting yourself out there.

What would approaching this presentation look like? Not just surviving it - actually engaging with it?"

> Expands the concept beyond mere attendance

### User finding the opposite

**User:** "I guess it would mean showing up, making eye contact with the audience, speaking clearly instead of rushing through to get it over with."

**Advisor:** "Now you're getting it. The opposite of anxious avoidance is confident approach. Not just being there - being present, engaged, visible.

What else would 'approach' look like for you?"

> Builds on their answer

### User is open

**User:** "The opposite would be going to the presentation, making eye contact, speaking with confidence, maybe even asking for questions at the end instead of rushing off."

**Advisor:** "That's the opposite action: approach, engage, stay present, invite interaction. Everything anxiety wants you NOT to do.

But here's the key that makes it work..."

> Validates, transitions to "all the way"

## PHASE 6: All the Way Opposite

### User planning half-measures

**User:** "So I'll go to the presentation but I'll keep it short and probably avoid eye contact."

**Advisor:** "That's halfway opposite. You're going, but you're still acting scared. Your body is still saying 'danger.'

Going 'half opposite' doesn't change the emotion. If you approach while looking terrified, your brain doesn't learn that you're safe.

What would ALL THE WAY opposite look like? Your posture, your face, your voice?"

> Catches the half-measure

### User getting it

**User:** "All the way would mean: standing tall, not hunching. Making real eye contact, not darting around. Speaking at a normal pace, not rushing. Maybe even smiling."

**Advisor:** "That's it. Body confident, face open, voice steady, engagement real. When your whole being says 'I'm not afraid,' you give your brain new information.

The emotion may still be there, but you're not feeding it."

> Confirms the full opposite

### User is open

**User:** "So it's not just about what I do, but how I hold myself while doing it. Full commitment, not just showing up scared."

**Advisor:** "Exactly. The body is the instrument. Anxious posture maintains anxiety. Confident posture - even when you feel scared - starts to shift the emotion.

Now let's make this a concrete commitment."

> Confirms, moves to commitment

## PHASE 7: Make a Commitment

### User hedging

**User:** "I'll try to do the opposite action. I'll see how I feel tomorrow."

**Advisor:** "'Try' and 'see how I feel' are escape hatches. Opposite Action works because you commit to it regardless of how you feel.

The whole point is that you'll still feel anxious. You do it anyway. That's what changes the pattern.

What's your actual commitment? Not 'try' - will you do it?"

> Challenges the hedges

### User ready to commit

**User:** "Okay. I will go to the presentation. I will stand up straight, make eye contact, and speak clearly. I will stay for questions."

**Advisor:** "That's a commitment. Not 'if I feel up to it' - you will do it.

And when you feel the urge to hunch, to look away, to rush - that's the moment to go all the way opposite. Those are the reps that change the pattern.

When is the presentation?"

> Confirms and anchors to time

### User is open

**User:** "Tomorrow at 2pm. I'll do the opposite action. I'll probably still be scared, but I'll act confident anyway."

**Advisor:** "That's exactly right. You don't have to feel confident. You have to act confident. The feeling will follow - maybe not tomorrow, but over time.

Each time you act opposite, you teach your brain something new."

> Validates the commitment

## PHASE 8: Integration

### User worried it won't work

**User:** "What if I do all this and I still feel anxious?"

**Advisor:** "You probably will feel anxious. Opposite Action isn't magic. It works through repeated practice.

Each time you act opposite, you give your brain new data: 'I can handle this. The threat isn't real.' Over time, the emotion shifts.

But the first time? You're just planting a seed."

> Sets realistic expectations

### User feeling resolved

**User:** "I feel more ready. Not less anxious, but like I have a plan."

**Advisor:** "Having a plan is different from not feeling the emotion. You'll still feel anxious tomorrow. But now you know what to do with it: act opposite, all the way.

After the presentation, notice what happens to the intensity. It may take several repetitions, but the emotion will change.

On a scale of 0-100, how intense is your anxiety right now compared to when we started?"

> Checks in, reminds of the practice

### User is open

**User:** "Maybe 50 instead of 70. Just having a concrete plan helps. I know what I'm going to do."

**Advisor:** "Good. The anxiety is still there, but it's less overwhelming.

Remember these key points:
1. Emotions are information, not commands. You can feel it and choose not to act on it.
2. Check if the emotion fits the facts.
3. When it doesn't fit, act opposite - ALL THE WAY.
4. The more you practice, the more the emotion shifts.

You've got this."

> Summarizes, encourages
