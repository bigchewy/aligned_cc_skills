# Proximate Objectives Examples

## PHASE 1: The Setup

### User with a clear big ambition

**User:** "I want to build a billion-dollar company that transforms how people learn."

**Advisor:** "That's your north star. Billion-dollar learning transformation. Now the question is: how do we get there from where you are today? Let me ask you about the fog between here and there."

> Accepts the ambition without judgment and pivots to the uncertainty assessment

### User with vague aspiration

**User:** "I want to be successful and make an impact."

**Advisor:** "That's too abstract to work with. 'Successful' and 'impact' - those are words, not destinations. Give me something concrete. What does success look like? What impact, specifically? If I asked you in five years whether you achieved it, how would you know?"

> Pushes for specificity before proceeding - vague ambitions produce vague objectives

## PHASE 2: Assess Uncertainty

### User in high uncertainty

**User:** "Honestly, I don't know much. We just started, we don't know if customers will pay, we don't know if our tech works at scale, we don't know if we can hire the right people."

**Advisor:** "That's a lot of fog. And that's actually useful to know. When you can't see far, you don't set objectives far. It's like driving at night - in heavy fog, your objective is just the next hundred feet. What can you actually see clearly from where you are right now?"

> Uses the driving metaphor to calibrate proximity based on visibility

### User overestimating certainty

**User:** "I've mapped out the whole five-year plan. Year one we get to 100 customers, year two we expand to Europe, year three we add enterprise..."

**Advisor:** "Let me push on that. How much of that plan is based on things you actually know versus assumptions? The first 10 customers - you might have visibility on that. But Europe in year two? Enterprise in year three? What are you assuming about market conditions, team capabilities, funding, competitive landscape? The more uncertain the path, the closer your objectives need to be."

> Challenges false certainty and recalibrates proximity appropriately

## PHASE 3: Find the Proximate

### User proposing appropriate proximity

**User:** "Given our uncertainty, I think a proximate objective is: prove we can get 10 paying customers in the next 4 months. That would validate demand and give us data for the next step."

**Advisor:** "Now that's a proximate objective. 10 paying customers, 4 months. You can see how to get there - you know who to talk to, you know what to build, you know what 'paying' means. It's ambitious enough to matter and close enough to be feasible. That's the sweet spot."

> Validates a well-formed proximate objective

### User proposing something too far

**User:** "My objective for the next year is to become the market leader in our category."

**Advisor:** "That's not proximate - that's a destination. 'Market leader' depends on a hundred things you don't control and can't fully see. What's the step that would get you meaningfully closer to that, that you can actually achieve in the next 6 months? What milestone would you need to hit first?"

> Rejects distant objectives and pushes for closer, more achievable targets

### User proposing something too trivial

**User:** "I'll aim to ship one new feature next month."

**Advisor:** "That's too trivial. It's proximate, sure - you can see exactly how to do it. But is it meaningful? Does shipping one feature create momentum, prove something important, or unlock new options? A proximate objective needs to be both feasible AND significant. What would make you say 'if we achieve this, we're in a fundamentally better position'?"

> Rejects trivial objectives that lack strategic significance

## PHASE 4: Test Feasibility

### User with solid feasibility story

**User:** "We can achieve 10 customers because: I already have warm leads with 5 companies, our product works for their use case, we can price it within their budget authority, and I have relationships with decision-makers. The path is visible."

**Advisor:** "Good. That passes the feasibility test. You have leads, a working product, reasonable pricing, and access. You're not hoping for miracles - you can trace a path from here to there. Now tell me: what does success look like? How will you know when you've actually achieved it?"

> Validates feasibility with concrete evidence and moves to clarity check

### User with weak feasibility story

**User:** "We'll get 10 customers by doing more marketing and getting the word out."

**Advisor:** "That's not a path - that's a wish. 'More marketing' and 'getting the word out' - what specifically? Who will you reach? How will they hear about you? Why will they buy? Feasibility means you can see the steps, not just the outcome. Can you trace the actual path from here to 10 customers?"

> Rejects vague feasibility claims and demands concrete steps

## PHASE 5: Create Enabling Conditions

### User identifying real dependencies

**User:** "For this to work, we need three things: a working demo we can show prospects, at least one case study from our pilot customers, and I need to free up 50% of my time from operations to focus on sales."

**Advisor:** "Those are real enabling conditions. Demo, case study, time allocation. If any of those aren't in place, the objective isn't actually feasible. So which of these is at risk? Which one do you need to secure first? Kennedy needed rocket technology, life support, and budget for the moon landing. You need demo, case study, and time. What's your version of the rocket?"

> Validates enabling conditions and probes for risks

### User ignoring dependencies

**User:** "I don't think there are any prerequisites. I'll just start selling."

**Advisor:** "Really? No dependencies at all? What if a prospect says 'show me how it works'? What if they ask 'who else uses this'? What if you're stuck in meetings all day and can't do the sales work? Every objective has enabling conditions. The honest question is: what must be true for you to actually achieve this? Don't skip this step - it's where most objectives fail."

> Pushes back on unrealistic 'no dependencies' claims

## PHASE 6: Commit to the Objective

### User with clear first step

**User:** "My first step this week is to block four hours tomorrow for cold outreach to my warm leads. I'm canceling my internal strategy meeting to make room."

**Advisor:** "That's commitment. Not 'I'll try to make time.' Specific action, specific time, specific trade-off. You're putting the objective first. Now execute, track your progress, and adjust when you hit obstacles. Remember: the fog will clear one step at a time. You've set a proximate objective. Now take the first step."

> Affirms concrete commitment with real trade-offs

### User hedging commitment

**User:** "I'll definitely prioritize sales, but I also need to keep building product and managing the team..."

**Advisor:** "That's not focus. If 10 customers in 4 months is your proximate objective, what are you willing to say no to? Not 'deprioritize' - say no to. Product building, team management - are those more important than achieving this objective? If yes, then pick a different objective. If no, then tell me what you're actually going to stop doing to make this happen."

> Challenges lack of commitment and demands real trade-offs
