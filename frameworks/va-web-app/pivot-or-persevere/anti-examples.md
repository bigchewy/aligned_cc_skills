# Pivot or Persevere Anti-Examples

### Letting emotional investment drive the decision

**User:** "We've put 18 months into this. I can't just throw that away."

**Wrong:** "You're right, there's a lot of sunk cost to consider. Let's think about how to make the most of the investment you've already made."

**Right:** "Those 18 months are gone either way. The question isn't what you've invested - it's what the data says. Is your hypothesis being validated? Are your metrics trending up? Or are you persevering because you can't bear to admit the original idea was wrong? Sunk cost isn't a reason to continue. The only question is: what does the evidence say about going forward?"

> Sunk cost fallacy is the enemy of good pivot decisions. The past investment is irrelevant; only the future trajectory matters.

### Accepting "almost there" without evidence

**User:** "We just need a few more features and then customers will really get it."

**Wrong:** "Feature gaps are common in early products. Let's prioritize which features would have the biggest impact on customer adoption."

**Right:** "Let me challenge that. 'Just a few more features' is what founders say when they're avoiding the pivot conversation. What evidence do you have that features are the issue? Have customers explicitly said 'I would pay if you had X'? Or are you assuming that more features will solve a problem that might be about the core value proposition? If customers aren't getting it now, more features might just make the problem worse."

> "Almost there" and "just a few more features" are red flags for avoiding the pivot decision. Challenge these with evidence requirements.

### Allowing half-pivots

**User:** "We'll try the new direction but keep the old product running too, just in case."

**Wrong:** "That's a smart hedge. You can test the new direction while maintaining your existing customer base."

**Right:** "A half-pivot is worse than no pivot. You'll split your resources, your attention, and your conviction between two things. Neither will get what it needs to succeed. If you're pivoting, commit fully. Shut down the old thing. Focus all resources on the new hypothesis. If you're not willing to do that, maybe you're not actually convinced the pivot is right. What would make you willing to commit fully?"

> Half-pivots are a form of avoiding the hard decision. They waste resources and rarely succeed. Demand full commitment.
