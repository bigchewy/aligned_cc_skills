# Pivot or Persevere Examples

## PHASE 1: The Setup

### User with ambiguous results

**User:** "We've been working on our product for 8 months. We have some users but growth is slow. I don't know if we should keep going or try something different."

**Advisor:** "That's exactly the situation where this framework matters. Some users but slow growth - you're in the ambiguous middle where most startups get stuck. There's no bigger destroyer of creative potential than the misguided decision to persevere on a failing strategy. Let's figure out what the data is actually telling you."

> Sets up the framework with the key quote about perseverance being destructive

### User clearly in trouble

**User:** "Our product isn't getting any traction. We've tried everything but nobody's buying."

**Advisor:** "Before we jump to conclusions, let's be rigorous about this. What was your original hypothesis? What did you expect to happen? And what actually happened? I need to understand whether you're facing invalidated hypotheses or execution problems before we talk about pivoting."

> Slows down to gather data even when user is ready to give up

## PHASE 2: The Hypothesis Review

### User rationalizing results

**User:** "Our hypothesis was that small businesses would pay for better invoicing. We got 50 signups but only 3 paying customers. But I think the pricing was wrong and the onboarding was confusing and we launched at a bad time."

**Advisor:** "Let me push back on that. You're making excuses for why the data doesn't show what you expected. 50 signups, 3 paying - that's a 6% conversion. Is that validating or invalidating your hypothesis? If small businesses desperately needed better invoicing, would only 3 out of 50 pay? Be honest with yourself - are you explaining away disconfirming evidence?"

> Calls out rationalization of poor results

### User being honest about data

**User:** "We hypothesized that agencies would pay $100/month for project profitability tracking. We got 25 trial signups, 15 became paying customers, and 12 are still active after 3 months. But growth has stalled - we can't seem to get more than 20-30 new signups per month no matter what we try."

**Advisor:** "That's an honest data picture. 60% trial-to-paid conversion is strong - that suggests the value hypothesis is working. But you're stuck on growth. That's a different problem than 'nobody wants this.' Let's look at whether you're making sufficient progress on the right thing."

> Acknowledges honest data presentation and focuses on the real issue

## PHASE 3: The Sufficient Progress Question

### User making slow progress

**User:** "We're growing about 5% month over month. It's positive, but it's not the hockey stick growth investors want."

**Advisor:** "Here's the real question: if you continue at 5% monthly growth for another 6 months, another year - where do you end up? Does that trajectory lead somewhere real, or are you optimizing a path to nowhere? 5% compounded is meaningful over time, but is the market big enough? Are unit economics working? Slow growth that leads to a real business is different from slow growth that leads to eventual death."

> Applies the trajectory question to evaluate slow growth

### User on a dead trajectory

**User:** "At current rates, we'd have maybe 200 customers in a year, each paying $50/month. That's $120K ARR. With our burn rate, we'd run out of money before hitting profitability."

**Advisor:** "So you're not making sufficient progress. The trajectory doesn't lead to survival. That's the honest assessment. Now the question is: is this an execution problem where more effort on the current path could change the trajectory, or is it a strategy problem where the path itself is wrong? What do you believe?"

> Confirms insufficient progress and poses the key strategic question

## PHASE 4: The Pivot Options

### User unclear on pivot types

**User:** "I know we need to change something, but I'm not sure what. A whole new product?"

**Advisor:** "A pivot isn't 'start over.' There are many types of pivots. Let me give you the taxonomy: Zoom-in - one feature becomes the whole product. Zoom-out - your product becomes one feature of something bigger. Customer segment - same product, different customer. Customer need - same customer, different problem. Channel - same product, different distribution. Which of these feels relevant? What piece of what you've learned could you keep?"

> Introduces pivot taxonomy to give concrete options

### User identifying specific pivot

**User:** "Actually, one of our features - the cash flow forecasting - is what customers love most. Everything else they barely use. Maybe we zoom in on that?"

**Advisor:** "That's a zoom-in pivot. You take the one thing that's working and make it the whole product. What evidence do you have that cash flow forecasting specifically is what customers value? If you stripped away everything else and just sold forecasting, would your existing customers stay? Would new customers pay more for something focused?"

> Explores the specific pivot option with clarifying questions

## PHASE 5: The Decision Criteria

### User leaning toward persevere

**User:** "I feel like we just need more time. We're so close. A few more months and we'll break through."

**Advisor:** "Let me be direct. 'We're so close' and 'just a few more months' are things I hear from founders who pivot 18 months too late. Here's the criteria: are your metrics trending in the right direction? Is the core hypothesis being validated? Or are you adding features to avoid facing the truth that your hypothesis was wrong? If your metrics are flat despite genuine effort, that's not 'almost there' - that's invalidation."

> Challenges emotional perseverance with specific criteria

### User with clear pivot signal

**User:** "Our hypothesis was that small agencies would pay for full project management. The data shows they love our forecasting feature but ignore everything else. The core hypothesis was wrong."

**Advisor:** "Then you have your answer. The original hypothesis - that they'd pay for full project management - was invalidated. But you learned something valuable: forecasting is what they want. That's a pivot signal, not a failure. A pivot is a change in strategy without a change in vision. You're still trying to help agencies succeed financially - you're just taking a different road to get there."

> Validates pivot based on clear evidence

## PHASE 6: The Commitment

### User committing to pivot

**User:** "I'm going to pivot. We'll zoom in on cash flow forecasting and strip away everything else."

**Advisor:** "If you're going to pivot, commit to it. A half-pivot is worse than no pivot. Don't keep the old features 'just in case.' Don't split resources between the old strategy and the new. Pivots require courage because they require admitting your previous hypothesis was wrong. That's not failure - that's learning. What's the specific next action?"

> Demands full commitment, warns against half-measures

### User committing to persevere

**User:** "Looking at the data, our core value hypothesis is being validated. The problem is growth, not product-market fit. I'm going to persevere, but with a specific focus on the growth channel."

**Advisor:** "That's a reasoned persevere decision. You're not just hoping - you've identified that value is validated and growth is the problem. Set a timeline. Define what sufficient progress looks like on growth. If you don't hit it by that date, you're back in this conversation. Perseverance without accountability is just stubborn denial."

> Validates persevere decision with accountability requirements
