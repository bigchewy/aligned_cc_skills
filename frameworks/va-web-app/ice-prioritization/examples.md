# ICE Prioritization Examples

## PHASE 1: Gather Experiment Candidates

### User provides clear list

**User:** "We're considering: 1) Adding a referral program, 2) Testing new onboarding email sequence, 3) Running Facebook ads to a new audience, 4) Adding a 'share' button after purchase."

**Advisor:** "Good list - four distinct experiments to compare. Let's score each one systematically. We'll start with the referral program. Before we score, give me one sentence on what you're testing: what's the specific change and what outcome are you measuring?"

> Gets specifics before scoring

### User has only one idea

**User:** "I really just want to focus on improving our landing page conversion."

**Advisor:** "ICE works best when comparing multiple options - it helps you avoid the trap of working on whatever's in front of you. What else could move the needle? Think about: other acquisition channels, activation improvements, retention experiments. Give me two more ideas, even rough ones. We'll score all three and see which deserves your focus."

> Pushes for comparison set

### User ideas are vague

**User:** "We want to 1) Improve onboarding, 2) Get more traffic, 3) Increase retention."

**Advisor:** "These are goals, not experiments. 'Improve onboarding' could mean a hundred different things. Let's get specific: What ONE change to onboarding could you test? 'Add a progress bar' is an experiment. 'Send a welcome email at hour 3 instead of hour 24' is an experiment. Make each of your three ideas into a specific, testable change."

> Insists on specificity before scoring

## PHASE 2: Score Impact

### User gives realistic Impact score

**User:** "For the referral program, I'd say Impact is 7. It could bring in a lot of users but won't double our growth."

**Advisor:** "7 is reasonable for a referral program. To calibrate: what percentage of your users do you think would actually refer someone? Industry average is 2-5%. If you get 5% of users referring 1 person each, that's a 5% lift. Is that a 7-level impact for your business? If your base is small, 5% more users might be huge. If you're already at scale, maybe it's more like a 5."

> Helps calibrate with real numbers

### User inflates Impact score

**User:** "Our new landing page will be a 10 for sure. It's going to completely change our conversion rate."

**Advisor:** "A 10 means doubling your metric. Your current conversion rate is what - maybe 3-5%? You're expecting to hit 6-10%? That's possible but rare for a single page change. What's your evidence? If it's 'I really like the new design,' that's optimism, not data. I'd suggest 7-8 unless you have test data or strong competitive benchmarks."

> Challenges inflated scores

### User deflates Impact score

**User:** "I guess the email sequence test is maybe a 3? It's just emails."

**Advisor:** "Don't undersell it. 'Just emails' are often high-leverage because they affect every new user. If your sequence reaches 100% of signups and you improve activation by even 10%, that compounds. What percentage of signups does this affect? If it's everyone, and activation is a key metric, this might be more like a 5-6. Impact is about leverage, not glamour."

> Pushes back on false modesty

## PHASE 3: Score Confidence

### User gives high Confidence with evidence

**User:** "Confidence is 9. We've seen referral programs work at three similar B2B SaaS companies, and we already have users asking for one."

**Advisor:** "Users asking for it is a strong signal - that's real demand data. And three comparable examples is solid precedent. 9 is justified here. The only reason I wouldn't give it a 10 is execution risk - referral programs need good mechanics to work. But your hypothesis that users will refer is well-supported."

> Validates high scores backed by evidence

### User gives high Confidence without evidence

**User:** "I'm really confident in this one - I'd say 9. Our CEO saw a competitor do it and it seems like it worked for them."

**Advisor:** "'CEO saw competitor do it' isn't evidence - you don't know their numbers, their audience, or whether it actually worked. That's optimism, not data. Without any direct evidence from your users or tests, I'd put this at 4-5. What would it take to get real data? Could you test a simplified version first?"

> Challenges high scores without evidence

### User gives low Confidence honestly

**User:** "Confidence is probably a 3. It's a complete guess - we've never tried anything like this."

**Advisor:** "Honest assessment. A 3 means you're basically testing a hypothesis from scratch. That's fine if the experiment is easy - you'll learn fast. If the experiment requires significant investment, low Confidence should make you pause. Is there a way to test a smaller version first to increase Confidence before going big?"

> Acknowledges honesty, suggests risk mitigation

## PHASE 4: Score Ease

### User gives realistic Ease score

**User:** "Ease is a 6. The email sequence will take about a week to write, set up, and test the flows."

**Advisor:** "A week is reasonable for a 6. Quick question: could you test a simpler version faster? Maybe just change the first email and measure impact before rewriting the whole sequence? If you can get meaningful data in 2-3 days instead of a week, that's an 8-9 for Ease. Sometimes the MVP of an experiment is much smaller than we initially plan."

> Suggests simplification

### User inflates Ease score

**User:** "Building a referral program should be easy - I'd say 8. Just add a link and some tracking."

**Advisor:** "Let's reality-check that. Referral programs need: unique links per user, tracking attribution, reward mechanics, fraud prevention, and a way to notify users of successful referrals. That's not 'just a link.' Is this a week of engineering work? More? If it's more than 3 days, you're looking at a 5-6, not an 8. Be honest about dependencies."

> Challenges underestimated effort

### User gives low Ease honestly

**User:** "Ease is a 2. This would require a complete rebuild of our checkout flow with multiple teams involved."

**Advisor:** "A 2 is a major investment. Unless Impact and Confidence are both 8+, a 2 Ease usually kills an experiment's priority. ICE score with low Ease gets crushed by easier experiments. Is there a smaller version that tests the same hypothesis? What's the core assumption you're trying to validate? Can you validate it without rebuilding checkout?"

> Pushes for simplification when Ease is low

## PHASE 5: Calculate and Compare

### High ICE score

**User:** "So the email sequence is 6 × 7 × 8 = 336."

**Advisor:** "336 is solid - that's a 'run this week' experiment. It has reasonable Impact, good Confidence, and high Ease. This should be near the top of your list. Now let's score your other experiments and see how they compare. What's your Impact score for the referral program?"

> Confirms priority, moves to comparison

### Moderate ICE score

**User:** "The landing page redesign is 8 × 5 × 4 = 160."

**Advisor:** "160 is borderline. High Impact, but Confidence is uncertain and it's not easy to test. Here's the thing: you're betting a lot of effort on a hypothesis you're only 50% sure about. Could you test a smaller change first - just the headline, or just the CTA button - to build Confidence before the full redesign? That smaller test might score 6 × 4 × 9 = 216, which is actually higher priority."

> Suggests experiment redesign for higher ICE

### Low ICE score revealing priority issues

**User:** "The referral program is 7 × 3 × 2 = 42."

**Advisor:** "42 is too low to prioritize. You have high-ish Impact, but low Confidence and very low Ease. This is a big bet on an uncertain hypothesis. I'd either: 1) Find a way to increase Confidence first with cheaper tests, or 2) Radically simplify - could you manually test referrals with 10 customers before building anything? Get real data before major investment."

> Uses low score to redirect effort

## PHASE 6: Prioritize and Commit

### Clear prioritization

**User:** "So our ranked list is: 1) Email sequence (336), 2) Share button (280), 3) Landing page (160), 4) Referral program (42)."

**Advisor:** "Perfect. Now the discipline: you work this list from the top. Don't skip the email sequence because the landing page seems more exciting. Don't deprioritize the share button because it feels too simple. When can you start the email sequence test?"

> Enforces discipline on ranked list

### User wants to skip priorities

**User:** "I know the referral program scored lowest, but it's the one I really want to work on."

**Advisor:** "That's exactly why we use ICE - it protects you from your own biases. You want to work on it because you're excited about it, not because it's the best use of your time. The numbers say: low Confidence on a hard build. Run your higher-scoring experiments first. If referral is really important, find a way to test the hypothesis more easily to increase your Confidence score."

> Resists emotional prioritization

### User commits to timeline

**User:** "I can start the email sequence test Monday. Should have results by end of week."

**Advisor:** "Good. Commit to it: Monday you write, Tuesday you launch, Friday you have initial data. Document what you learn - both for this experiment and to calibrate your future ICE scores. What's the one thing that could derail this timeline? Let's solve that now before it becomes an excuse."

> Locks in commitment, anticipates blockers
