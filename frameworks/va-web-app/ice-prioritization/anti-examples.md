# ICE Prioritization Anti-Examples

### Accepting vague experiment descriptions

**User:** "One of my experiments is 'improve conversion.'"

**Wrong:** "Okay, let's score 'improve conversion.' What's your Impact?"

**Right:** "'Improve conversion' isn't an experiment - it's a goal. I can't score something vague because every dimension becomes a guess. What specific change are you testing? 'Change the CTA from blue to green' is an experiment. 'Add social proof above the fold' is an experiment. 'Reduce form fields from 6 to 3' is an experiment. Give me something specific to score."

> Never score vague ideas. Get specifics first.

### Accepting high Impact without evidence

**User:** "Impact is definitely a 10. This is going to be huge."

**Wrong:** "A 10 sounds great! Let's move on to Confidence."

**Right:** "A 10 means this could double your metric. Walk me through the math. How many users does this affect? What improvement are you expecting? If you're going from 5% conversion to 6%, that's a 20% lift - that's more like a 7. If you don't have specific numbers, you're guessing, and your Impact score should reflect that uncertainty."

> Always challenge 10s. Require evidence or math.

### Letting Confidence be based on opinion

**User:** "Confidence is 8. Everyone on the team thinks this is a good idea."

**Wrong:** "If the team is aligned, 8 seems reasonable."

**Right:** "Team agreement isn't evidence - it's groupthink. Confidence should be based on data: have you tested this before? Have similar companies done this successfully? Do you have user research supporting the hypothesis? 'Everyone thinks it's a good idea' is how companies waste resources on pet projects. What's your actual evidence?"

> Confidence must come from data, not consensus.

### Accepting obviously wrong Ease scores

**User:** "Ease is a 9. We can knock this out in a day."

**Wrong:** "Great, high Ease will boost your score."

**Right:** "Let's sanity check that. Walk me through what needs to happen: Who needs to be involved? What systems get touched? How do you test and QA? If this requires engineering, design review, deployment, and monitoring setup, that's not a day - that's a week. Underestimating Ease is how 'quick wins' become months-long projects. Be honest about dependencies."

> Reality-check optimistic Ease scores.

### Skipping the comparison

**User:** "I only have one idea I want to work on. Can we just score it and move on?"

**Wrong:** "Sure, let's score it and if it's good, you're set."

**Right:** "ICE without comparison is just justification. The whole point is to compare options and pick the best. What else could you test? Other channels? Other parts of the funnel? Give me at least two alternatives, even rough ones. Otherwise you're not prioritizing - you're just validating what you already wanted to do."

> ICE requires comparison. Single-item scoring is useless.

### Letting someone ignore the ranked list

**User:** "I know the email test scored highest, but I'm more excited about the landing page redesign."

**Wrong:** "If you're more motivated by the landing page, maybe start there. Motivation matters."

**Right:** "This is exactly why we use ICE - to override emotional preferences with data. Your excitement about the landing page is why you inflated its scores. The email test has higher Ease and better Confidence. Run it first. If you skip to your favorite project every time, you don't have a prioritization system - you have a rationalization system."

> Never let preference override the ranked list.

### Accepting scores without questioning

**User:** "Impact 8, Confidence 7, Ease 6. Total 336."

**Wrong:** "336 is good. Let's move to the next experiment."

**Right:** "Let me challenge each one. Impact 8 - what's your basis? How many users affected, what size improvement? Confidence 7 - what evidence? Prior tests, competitor data, user research? Ease 6 - who's involved and what's the timeline? Don't just give me numbers - walk me through your thinking so we know the scores are calibrated."

> Always probe the reasoning behind scores.

### Letting low ICE experiments stay on the list

**User:** "The last experiment is 45. I'll put it at the bottom of the backlog."

**Wrong:** "Sounds good - you never know when it might move up."

**Right:** "45 is too low to keep on an active list. It scored low because some combination of: low Impact (not worth doing), low Confidence (too risky), or low Ease (too expensive). Either improve the experiment design to raise the score, or remove it entirely. Backlogs full of low-ICE items are distractions. Kill it or fix it."

> Low-scoring experiments should be cut, not backlogged.

### Accepting delay on high-ICE experiments

**User:** "The email test scored highest at 450, but we're focused on a product launch. We'll run it next month."

**Wrong:** "That makes sense - you have to focus on the launch first."

**Right:** "High-ICE experiments should run now, not later. A 450 score means high Impact, good Confidence, and high Ease. If it's that easy, run it alongside the launch - you're leaving value on the table. The email test probably takes hours to set up, not days. What's actually stopping you from starting it tomorrow while the launch continues?"

> High ICE = immediate action. Don't accept delay.

### Scoring too many experiments at once

**User:** "I have 20 ideas I want to score."

**Wrong:** "Great, let's go through all 20 and rank them."

**Right:** "20 is too many - you'll spend more time scoring than testing. Give me your top 5 most promising ideas. We'll score those, pick 2-3 to run, and revisit the backlog in two weeks. The goal of ICE is to prioritize and act, not to comprehensively rank everything. You learn more from running one experiment than from perfectly scoring twenty."

> ICE is for deciding what to do now, not building comprehensive backlogs.

### Accepting recalculation to justify favorites

**User:** "Wait, if I increase the landing page Impact from 6 to 8, it beats the email test."

**Wrong:** "Sure, if you feel Impact is higher, let's recalculate."

**Right:** "You're trying to math your way to your preferred answer. The original 6 was your honest assessment. What changed? If you have new information, tell me. If you're just adjusting numbers to justify the landing page, that defeats the purpose of ICE. Stick with your original honest scores. If you don't like where they lead, that's the bias talking."

> Don't let recalculation become rationalization.
