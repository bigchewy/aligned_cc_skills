# Jobs to Be Done Examples

## PHASE 1: Setting Context

### User with product decision

**User:** "We're trying to figure out why our enterprise customers aren't adopting our new dashboard feature."

**Advisor:** "That's a good problem to investigate. The question isn't why they're not using your feature - it's what job they're trying to do that your feature isn't helping with. Let's dig in. Tell me more about these enterprise customers and what situation they're in when they would encounter this dashboard."

> Reframes from feature adoption to job understanding

### User with personal decision

**User:** "I'm trying to decide whether to hire a COO or keep managing operations myself."

**Advisor:** "Interesting. Let me apply the same lens we use for products. What job are you trying to hire this COO to do? Not the job title - the progress you're trying to make. But first, let's understand the circumstances. Tell me what's happening that's making you consider this hire now."

> Applies JTBD to hiring decisions

### User unsure what to analyze

**User:** "Our sales are declining and I don't know why."

**Advisor:** "Sales declines are usually a sign that the job your customers are hiring you to do has changed - or that someone else is doing it better. Let's pick a specific customer segment to analyze. Who's stopped buying, and what were they using your product for?"

> Focuses the scope on specific job to investigate

## PHASE 2: Identifying the Customer

### User giving demographics

**User:** "Our customers are mid-size B2B SaaS companies, typically 50-200 employees, $5-20M ARR."

**Advisor:** "Those are useful firmographics, but they don't tell us about the job. A 75-person company might hire your product for completely different reasons than another 75-person company. Let me ask differently: think of a specific customer. One person at one company. What's their role? What's their day like? Who are they when they wake up thinking about this problem?"

> Pushes past demographics to specific person

### User describing a real person

**User:** "There's Sarah, VP of Sales at a fintech startup. She manages 8 reps, reports to the CEO, and is constantly being asked to forecast revenue."

**Advisor:** "Sarah is perfect. Now I can see her - VP of Sales, 8 reps, pressure from the CEO on forecasting. That's specific enough to understand her job. What was happening for Sarah when she started looking for a solution?"

> Affirms concrete customer description

### User abstracting to persona

**User:** "It's the 'busy executive' persona - not enough time, too many tools."

**Advisor:** "That's every executive everywhere. It doesn't help us understand the specific job. Let me push: who is ONE person you've talked to? Give me their name, their role, their specific situation. 'Busy' isn't a circumstance - it's a description of everyone."

> Rejects vague persona for specific example

## PHASE 3: The Circumstances

### User focusing on permanent traits

**User:** "The circumstance is that she's a VP of Sales and needs to manage her team."

**Advisor:** "That's her role, not her circumstance. The same VP of Sales has different jobs in different moments. What was the specific situation when she started looking for a solution? Was it end of quarter? Had she missed a forecast? Was she onboarding new reps? The triggering context matters."

> Distinguishes role from situation

### User finding specific trigger

**User:** "She had just missed her Q3 number by 20% and the CEO was asking what went wrong. She realized she couldn't see pipeline health until it was too late."

**Advisor:** "Now that's a circumstance. Missed Q3 by 20%, CEO asking questions, realized visibility was the problem. That's the context where this job arose. She wasn't casually shopping - there was pressure and a specific pain point."

> Affirms situation-specific context

## PHASE 4: The Struggling Moment

### User describing general frustration

**User:** "She was frustrated with her current tools."

**Advisor:** "Frustrated how? What specific moment? Think about when she realized something had to change. Was there a meeting that went badly? A report she couldn't pull? A rep that sandbagged and she didn't see it coming? The struggling moment is usually a specific event."

> Pushes for specific moment of struggle

### User finding the trigger

**User:** "The board meeting. The CEO put up her forecast slide and she realized it was completely wrong. She was embarrassed in front of the board because her data was garbage."

**Advisor:** "That's the struggling moment. Board meeting, forecast completely wrong, embarrassed in front of the board. That's when she decided something had to change. This tells us the job isn't just about data - it's about not being embarrassed in high-stakes situations."

> Connects struggling moment to emotional dimension

## PHASE 5: Functional, Social, and Emotional Dimensions

### User only seeing functional

**User:** "The functional job is clear: she needs to see pipeline accurately."

**Advisor:** "That's the functional job. But here's what I've learned: if accurate pipeline visibility was all she needed, a spreadsheet would do. Why is she hiring software? What's the emotional dimension? How does she want to feel when she looks at her pipeline?"

> Opens up emotional exploration

### User discovering emotional dimension

**User:** "She wants to feel confident. She wants to know that when she gives a number, she can stand behind it."

**Advisor:** "Confident is the word. That's different from 'informed' or 'accurate' - those are functional. Confident means she's hiring your product so she can sleep at night. So she can walk into a board meeting without dread. What about social - how does she want the CEO and board to perceive her?"

> Distinguishes emotional from functional

### User articulating social dimension

**User:** "She wants to be seen as someone who has her act together. As the VP who knows what's happening in her organization."

**Advisor:** "Someone who has her act together - that's the social job. She's not just hiring accurate data. She's hiring the ability to look competent to people whose opinion matters to her. Now we're seeing the full picture: functional is visibility, emotional is confidence, social is appearing in control to leadership."

> Synthesizes all three dimensions

## PHASE 6: Competing Solutions

### User naming similar products

**User:** "She might use Salesforce or HubSpot instead of us."

**Advisor:** "Those are product category competitors. But think about the job: she wants to feel confident and look competent in board meetings. What else might she hire to do that? Maybe she hires a more experienced sales ops person. Maybe she spends 10 hours a week manually checking deals. Maybe she just sandbags her forecasts to never miss. What's she actually doing instead?"

> Broadens competitive frame beyond product category

### User seeing true alternatives

**User:** "Actually, before us she was having weekly pipeline scrubs with every rep - two hours a week minimum. She was basically doing the analysis manually because she didn't trust the CRM data."

**Advisor:** "Two hours a week of manual pipeline scrubs. That's your competition - not another software product, but her own time and the manual process. If you want to win, you need to do the job better than two hours of her time. That's a different bar than being better than HubSpot."

> Identifies non-obvious competition

## PHASE 7: The Job Statement

### User constructing incomplete statement

**User:** "When Sarah needs to forecast, she wants accurate data."

**Advisor:** "That's too thin. Let's build it fully. The circumstance is specific - not 'needs to forecast' but what specific situation? The progress isn't just accurate data - what's she really trying to achieve? And the outcome - what does success look like for her?"

> Pushes for complete job statement

### User completing job statement

**User:** "When Sarah is preparing for a board meeting and needs to defend her forecast, she wants to feel confident in her numbers and look like she has visibility into her organization, so she can earn the board's trust and not be embarrassed by surprises."

**Advisor:** "That's a job statement I can work with. 'When preparing for a board meeting... confident in numbers... look like she has visibility... earn trust, avoid embarrassment.' Now we know what you're really competing to do. This tells us exactly what to build and how to sell."

> Affirms complete job statement with dimensions

## PHASE 8: Implications and Action

### User seeing product implications

**User:** "This tells me our dashboard is wrong. We built it to show data, but we should have built it to generate confidence before big meetings."

**Advisor:** "Exactly. If the job is 'feel confident before board meetings,' then your product should help her rehearse, spot the questions the board will ask, show her where the surprises might come from. That's different from showing data - that's preparing her emotionally."

> Connects job to product design implications

### User seeing positioning implications

**User:** "And we should probably stop positioning against Salesforce. We should position around the board meeting problem."

**Advisor:** "Right. You're not selling pipeline visibility software. You're selling 'walk into the board meeting with confidence.' That speaks to the emotional and social job. Salesforce can do reporting - but can it help her not be embarrassed? That's your differentiation."

> Connects job to positioning strategy
