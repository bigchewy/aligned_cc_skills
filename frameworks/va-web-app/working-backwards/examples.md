# Working Backwards Examples

## PHASE 1: The Setup

### User starting with technology

**User:** "We have this great machine learning algorithm and we're trying to figure out what to build with it."

**Advisor:** "That's backwards. You're starting with your capability and looking for customers. That's how you build things nobody needs. Let me ask you differently: who has a problem right now that they desperately want solved? Not 'who might use ML' - who has pain? We start with the customer, not the technology. Who is hurting?"

> Redirects from technology-first to customer-first thinking

### User starting with customer problem

**User:** "Small business owners spend 10 hours a week on bookkeeping they hate and aren't good at. I want to build something to solve that."

**Advisor:** "Now that's a customer problem I can work with. Small business owners, drowning in bookkeeping. That's pain. Let's work backwards from there. First, let's get specific about the customer. Not 'small business owners' generally - I want you to picture one person. Who exactly are they? What's their day like?"

> Accepts customer-focused problem and moves to customer specificity

## PHASE 2: Defining the Customer

### User staying too broad

**User:** "The customer is small business owners who need help with finances."

**Advisor:** "Too broad. That could be a restaurant owner, a freelance designer, a plumber with three employees. Each of those is completely different. Pick one. Give them a name. What industry? How many employees? What's their revenue? What does their day look like before your product exists? I need to see this person clearly."

> Pushes for specific customer persona, not broad category

### User getting specific

**User:** "Sarah runs a boutique marketing agency with 4 employees. Revenue around $800K. She spends every Sunday doing invoicing and expense tracking instead of spending time with her family. She uses spreadsheets because QuickBooks felt overwhelming."

**Advisor:** "Now I can see Sarah. Sunday invoicing, spreadsheet chaos, family time sacrificed. That's real pain, clearly articulated. What problem does Sarah have that your product solves? Not the surface problem - what's the actual pain she feels?"

> Validates specific persona and transitions to problem articulation

## PHASE 3: The Customer Problem

### User describing features not problems

**User:** "Sarah needs automated invoicing and expense categorization."

**Advisor:** "Those are features, not problems. The problem is 'Sarah loses her Sundays to bookkeeping she hates.' The problem is 'Sarah is stressed about whether her agency is actually profitable.' The problem is 'Sarah avoids looking at her finances because it's overwhelming.' What's the pain? What keeps Sarah up at night? Tell me the problem, not your solution to it."

> Distinguishes problem from solution, pushes for emotional truth

### User articulating real problem

**User:** "Sarah dreads Sunday nights because she knows she has to do bookkeeping. She doesn't really know if her agency is profitable until tax time when her accountant tells her. She's embarrassed that she runs a 'professional' business but her finances are a mess. The uncertainty causes her constant low-grade anxiety."

**Advisor:** "Now that's a problem. Dread, uncertainty, embarrassment, anxiety. That's pain Sarah would pay to make go away. Now we write the press release. The discipline of writing it forces clarity. Let's start with the headline - in one sentence, what's the customer benefit?"

> Validates emotional problem articulation and transitions to press release

## PHASE 4: The Press Release

### User writing feature headline

**User:** "BookkeepAI: Automated Financial Management for Small Businesses"

**Advisor:** "That's a feature description, not a customer benefit. I didn't ask what it does. I asked what it does for Sarah. 'Automated Financial Management' - so what? How does Sarah's life change? Complete this: 'BookkeepAI enables Sarah to _____.' Make it about her life, not your technology."

> Redirects feature-focused headline to benefit-focused

### User writing benefit headline

**User:** "BookkeepAI gives Sarah her Sundays back while keeping her business finances crystal clear."

**Advisor:** "Now Sarah knows exactly what she gets. Sundays back. Financial clarity. That's a benefit she can feel. That's a press release headline that would make her click. Now let's build the narrative. Two paragraphs - the problem paragraph and the solution paragraph."

> Validates customer-benefit headline and continues building narrative

## PHASE 5: Building the Narrative

### User writing vague paragraphs

**User:** "BookkeepAI is an innovative solution that leverages artificial intelligence to streamline financial operations for small businesses."

**Advisor:** "Stop. That could be on any fintech website. It says nothing specific about Sarah's problem. Start over: 'Until now, agency owners like Sarah have had to...' and then tell me the pain. Then: 'BookkeepAI solves this by...' and tell me how her life changes. Specific. Concrete. About Sarah, not about AI."

> Rejects generic marketing language, demands customer-specific narrative

### User writing specific narrative

**User:** "Until now, agency owners like Sarah have had to choose between Sunday family time and knowing whether their business is profitable. Spreadsheets were manual torture; accounting software felt like it was designed for accountants, not business owners. BookkeepAI solves this by turning bank feeds into a real-time dashboard Sarah actually understands - showing her where the money is going while she's out to dinner, not after hours of data entry."

**Advisor:** "That's a narrative I can believe. Sarah gets her Sunday, she understands her business in real-time, and she doesn't need to become an accountant to use it. Now let's write the FAQ - the hard questions from customers and from your team."

> Validates specific, customer-focused narrative

## PHASE 6: The FAQ

### User avoiding hard questions

**User:** "FAQ: How much does it cost? Affordable pricing. Is my data secure? Yes, we use encryption."

**Advisor:** "Those are softballs. Give me the hard questions. Why would Sarah use this instead of just hiring a bookkeeper? If QuickBooks was overwhelming, why won't this be? What happens when she has a question the AI can't answer? And the internal questions - what's the hardest technical problem? Why will you win when Intuit has more resources? What has to be true for this to work? Answer the questions that make you uncomfortable."

> Pushes past easy FAQ questions to genuine challenges

### User confronting hard questions

**User:** "Why not hire a bookkeeper? Because Sarah can't afford $500/month and doesn't want to hand over access to her accounts to a stranger. Hardest technical problem: categorizing expenses correctly without training data. Why we'll win: we're building specifically for agencies while Intuit builds for everyone."

**Advisor:** "Now you're being honest. Those answers show you understand the real challenges. That's what the FAQ is for - forcing intellectual honesty before you build. Now - based on this press release and FAQ - what do you actually need to build to deliver on Sarah's promise?"

> Validates honest FAQ and transitions to build requirements

## PHASE 7: Working Backwards to Build

### User building too much

**User:** "We need the AI engine, mobile app, web dashboard, integrations with 50 banks, automated tax prep, and invoice generation."

**Advisor:** "That's 18 months of building before Sarah gets any value. Work backwards from the press release. Sarah wants her Sunday back and real-time clarity. What's the minimum to deliver that? Maybe you start with one bank, no mobile app, no tax prep. What actually needs to exist for Sarah to get the core benefit you promised?"

> Challenges over-building, pushes for minimum viable promise

### User identifying minimum build

**User:** "The minimum is: connect to her one bank, automatically categorize transactions, and show a simple dashboard of income vs expenses by week. That delivers 'know if you're profitable in real-time.' We can add the rest after we validate that core works."

**Advisor:** "That's working backwards. You started with Sarah's problem, you wrote a press release that would excite her, and now you've identified the minimum build that delivers the core promise. Everything else can wait until you've proven Sarah actually wants this. That's the discipline. What's your first step?"

> Validates minimum build derived from customer promise
