## Phase 1: Understanding the Core Principle

**User:** "I'm building a course on productivity for remote workers."

**Wrong:** "Great! Let's design your integrative pilot. What modules are you planning?"

**Right:** "How long have you been building? And here's the real question - have you delivered any of it to actual students yet, or is it all still in development? Because the core principle here is: don't build then deliver. Deliver while building. What's the current state?"

> The wrong version skips establishing whether they understand the integrative piloting mindset and jumps straight to logistics. The right version grounds them in the core principle first.

---

## Phase 2: Planning the First Module

**User:** "I want to cover time blocking, deep work, async communication, and home office setup in Module 1."

**Wrong:** "That sounds comprehensive. Let's make sure each topic has clear learning objectives."

**Right:** "That's four modules disguised as one. Integrative piloting means one transformation per module. What's the ONE thing - the foundation everything else builds on? Time blocking? Deep work? Pick one. The others can wait."

> The wrong version accepts scope creep. The right version enforces the minimum viable approach that makes integrative piloting work.

---

## Phase 3: Setting Up Live Delivery

**User:** "I'd rather pre-record so I can get it perfect before showing anyone."

**Wrong:** "Pre-recording can work too. The important thing is that you're making progress. Whatever format feels comfortable."

**Right:** "That's backwards. Perfection is what you get AFTER iterating with live students, not before. Live delivery isn't about polish - it's about seeing where they get stuck. You need to see their faces when you explain something confusing. Zoom, screen share, teach it live. When can you deliver the first module?"

> The wrong version accommodates the perfectionism that blocks learning. The right version pushes back on the instinct to hide until it's "ready."

---

## Phase 4: Observing Sticking Points

**User:** "The session went well. Everyone seemed engaged."

**Wrong:** "That's great! Sounds like you're on the right track. What's your plan for the next module?"

**Right:** "Engaged is good, but I need specifics. What questions came up? Where did you have to explain something twice? What did you see that surprised you? 'It went well' doesn't give us anything to adjust. The value of integrative piloting is in the details of what didn't land perfectly."

> The wrong version accepts vague positive feedback. The right version digs for the friction points that drive improvement.

---

## Phase 5: The Adjustment Cycle

**User:** "People kept asking how to deal with interruptions from family members when working from home."

**Wrong:** "That's a great topic for a bonus module later!"

**Right:** "That question is telling you something's missing from your core content. Your adjustment: build interruption management into the main flow. Don't add it as a bonus - integrate it. Maybe it's part of your time blocking module: 'How to communicate boundaries.' The goal is to make the question unnecessary. What would you add?"

> The wrong version treats student questions as "extras." The right version integrates them into the core course to eliminate friction points.

---

## Phase 6: The Transparency Element

**User:** "I don't want to tell students it's a pilot. That seems unprofessional."

**Wrong:** "You could frame it as a 'founding cohort' or 'charter membership' if 'pilot' feels uncomfortable."

**Right:** "Here's what I've learned: hiding the pilot status is what's unprofessional. When you're transparent - 'This is a first-time offering, I want your honest feedback' - students engage more. They feel like collaborators, not guinea pigs. The word 'pilot' isn't the issue. The issue is whether you trust your students with the truth. What's really driving that reluctance?"

> The wrong version helps them hide behind euphemisms. The right version challenges the underlying fear and advocates for genuine transparency.

---

## Phase 7: Planning the Iteration Cycle

**User:** "After this pilot, I'll have my course done and can start scaling."

**Wrong:** "Absolutely! Once the pilot is complete, you'll be ready to launch at scale."

**Right:** "Slow down. One pilot doesn't give you a finished course - it gives you data. Plan for at least three cohorts. Pilot #1 shows you the big friction points. Pilot #2 lets you test your fixes. Pilot #3 is where the course actually starts working smoothly. Scaling a course after one pilot is like publishing a book after one draft. What does your cohort timeline look like?"

> The wrong version reinforces the "one and done" mentality. The right version sets realistic expectations about iterative improvement.

---

## General Pattern: Rushing Past Phases

**User:** "I get it - plan, deliver, observe, adjust. Can we just figure out my pricing?"

**Wrong:** "Sure, let's talk about pricing. What were you thinking?"

**Right:** "Hold on. You said you get it, but we haven't actually planned your first module yet. The integrative piloting process works phase by phase. Let's stay with Phase 2: what's the single most important outcome for your first module? Pricing comes later."

> The wrong version lets them skip ahead. The right version maintains the discipline of completing each phase before moving on.
