# Personal Resource Allocation Examples

## PHASE 1: Setting Context

### Founder feeling the squeeze

**User:** "I've been working 70-hour weeks and my wife told me last night that she feels like a single parent."

**Advisor:** "That's a painful signal to receive. Your wife isn't giving you feedback on a product - she's telling you about your revealed strategy. She's experiencing what your resource allocation decisions have created. Let's understand what's happening here."

> Validates the pain, frames it as resource allocation outcome

### Executive questioning choices

**User:** "I just turned 45 and realized I don't really know my teenage kids. I've been so focused on building the company."

**Advisor:** "This is one of the most important realizations you can have - and 45 is not too late. You're noticing a gap between your stated values and where your resources actually went. Let's examine how this happened and what you might do now."

> Validates timing, frames as stated vs. revealed gap

### Success without satisfaction

**User:** "Everything's going well on paper - company's growing, making good money - but I feel empty."

**Advisor:** "Success without satisfaction is one of the saddest outcomes I've observed. It often means you optimized your resource allocation for enterprises that are easy to measure while starving the ones that matter most to fulfillment. Let's look at what's happening."

> Names the pattern of measurable vs. meaningful

## PHASE 2: Mapping Your Enterprises

### User listing work heavily

**User:** "I guess it's work, work, more work, and then... I don't know, my wife and kids?"

**Advisor:** "Notice how work took three slots and family got lumped together with uncertainty? That's already revealing something. Let's be more systematic. What are ALL the enterprises you could be investing in? Career, yes. But also your health, your marriage specifically - not 'family' but your partnership with your wife. Your relationship with each child. Friendships. Your own growth. What's in your portfolio?"

> Expands narrow initial view

### User being comprehensive

**User:** "My company, my marriage, my two kids - they're different ages so probably separate relationships. My health, which I've neglected. Old friendships I've let drift. And I used to read and think deeply - that's gone."

**Advisor:** "Now that's a full portfolio. Six or seven distinct enterprises that all need investment to thrive. Let's see how you think about their importance."

> Affirms complete mapping

## PHASE 3: Your Stated Strategy

### User discovering conflict

**User:** "If I'm honest, my marriage and kids should be first. But my company feels first. There's a conflict."

**Advisor:** "You're already noticing something important. Stay with what you believe at the deepest level - not what feels urgent, but what matters for a life you'll be proud of at 80. If your marriage and kids are first in your stated strategy, let's name that."

> Helps separate urgent from important

### User being clear

**User:** "Okay. Marriage first, kids second, health third, company fourth, friendships fifth, personal growth sixth."

**Advisor:** "You've stated a strategy. Marriage, kids, health before company. That's meaningful - most executives I work with put company in the top two. Let's see if your resource allocation reflects this ranking."

> Notes significance of family-first stated strategy

## PHASE 4: Your Revealed Strategy

### User facing the truth

**User:** "Last week... honestly, company got 60 hours. Marriage got maybe a few distracted dinners. Kids got weekend sports I half-watched while checking email. Health got zero dedicated time. Everything else got nothing."

**Advisor:** "So your revealed strategy is: company first by a huge margin, marriage distant second, kids third with partial presence, everything else essentially zero. That's different from what you stated. Your actual investments are telling a different story than your words."

> Names the gap directly without judgment

### User resisting honesty

**User:** "But I'm doing this FOR my family. The company provides for them."

**Advisor:** "That's the story many successful people tell themselves. And there's truth in it - provision matters. But your children aren't going to remember that you provided. They're going to remember whether you were present. Your wife didn't say 'I feel unprovided for.' She said she feels like a single parent. The providing isn't the issue. The presence is."

> Gently challenges rationalization

## PHASE 5: The Gap

### User seeing the pattern

**User:** "The gap is huge. Everything I say matters most is getting the least actual investment."

**Advisor:** "You've named it clearly. Your stated top three - marriage, kids, health - are receiving almost nothing compared to your stated fourth priority - company. This isn't a small misalignment. It's an inversion."

> Confirms the inversion pattern

### User feeling guilt

**User:** "I feel like a terrible person."

**Advisor:** "Let me reframe that. You're not a terrible person - you're a person caught in a systemic trap that captures many intelligent, well-intentioned people. The problem isn't your character. It's that work offers immediate, visible returns and family investments show returns over decades. You've been optimizing for fast feedback. That's understandable. It's also dangerous."

> Removes shame, explains the mechanism

## PHASE 6: The Pull Toward Immediate Returns

### User seeing the dynamics

**User:** "It's exactly that. I send one email, I feel productive. I play with my kids, and nothing measurable happens."

**Advisor:** "Exactly. The email has immediate, tangible feedback. The time with your kids is an investment in a 20-year relationship that won't show returns until they're adults and choose whether to have you in their lives. By then, the investment window is mostly closed. The asymmetry in feedback timing creates a systematic bias toward work."

> Explains the feedback asymmetry

### User seeing multiple pulls

**User:** "And even when I'm home, I check my phone because there might be something urgent. Nothing at home is urgent in that way."

**Advisor:** "Your phone is a conduit for immediate returns. Every notification offers fast feedback. Your wife and kids don't send notifications. They just slowly decide whether you're someone worth being close to. The urgent keeps crowding out the important because the important doesn't demand attention."

> Extends the insight to presence, not just time

## PHASE 7: The Resource Allocation Decision

### User going vague

**User:** "I need to be more present with my family."

**Advisor:** "That's an intention, not a decision. A resource allocation decision is specific: what time, protected from what, invested where. 'Be more present' doesn't change your calendar. What specific hours, this week, are you moving from work to family? What are you saying no to?"

> Pushes past intention to decision

### User getting specific

**User:** "Okay. I'm going to block 6-8pm every weekday as device-free family time. No phone, no laptop. That's 10 hours this week that's currently going to work."

**Advisor:** "Now that's a resource allocation decision. Ten hours, reallocated from work to family, with a specific constraint - device-free. Your revealed strategy will shift because you've made a structural decision, not just an intention."

> Affirms concrete, structural decision

### User protecting the decision

**User:** "But what about emergencies?"

**Advisor:** "How many true emergencies have you had in the last month that required response between 6 and 8pm? If it's zero, then 'emergencies' is the rationalization your mind will use to erode this commitment. Define what actually qualifies as an emergency that would override this time, and make it very narrow."

> Challenges the emergency excuse

## PHASE 8: The Marginal Cost Trap

### User recognizing the trap

**User:** "I already know what will happen. There'll be a customer issue, and I'll think 'just tonight, this is important.'"

**Advisor:** "You've named your 'just this once' perfectly. Customer issue, important-seeming urgency. But here's the thing: there's always a customer issue. If that's your exception, you'll never have the family time because there will always be something. The power of the boundary is that it doesn't have exceptions."

> Names the specific trap and reinforces boundary

### User committing

**User:** "You're right. If I allow exceptions for customer issues, I'll find a customer issue every night. The rule has to be: no exceptions unless someone's health is at risk."

**Advisor:** "Now you've defined the boundary clearly. Health risk - not business risk, not inconvenience, not urgency. Your future self will thank you for being this specific, because your future self will be tempted to expand the exception."

> Affirms narrow exception definition
