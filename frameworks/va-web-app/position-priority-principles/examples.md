# Position Priority Principles Examples

## PHASE 1: Setting Context

### User wanting general mobility advice

**User:** "I just want to be more flexible. I'm always feeling stiff."

**Advisor:** "Flexible is too vague. We need to know WHICH positions you can't access. 'Stiff' doesn't tell us anything actionable. Let's test the four positions that matter most and find out exactly where your limitations are. Once we have data, we can prescribe the right work."

> Redirects from vague goals to specific assessment

### User already knows their weakness

**User:** "I know my squat is terrible. I can barely get down there."

**Advisor:** "Good - you've got some self-awareness. Let's verify that and see how bad 'terrible' actually is. Sometimes what feels like a squat problem is actually an ankle problem or a hip problem. We'll test all four positions to get the full picture, but we'll start with your squat."

> Validates their intuition while insisting on full assessment

### User skeptical about simplicity

**User:** "Only four positions? I've been told I need to work on like 20 different things."

**Advisor:** "That's mobility theater. Here's the deal: these four positions underpin basically everything else. Master these, and half those other 'problems' disappear. The hip you think is tight? Often it's just a squat issue in disguise. Let's find out what actually needs work."

> Challenges complexity with the 80/20 principle

## PHASE 2: Full Squat Assessment

### User can't get into position at all

**User:** "I literally can't get into a deep squat. I fall backwards."

**Advisor:** "Falling backwards usually means ankle dorsiflexion is the bottleneck. Your shin can't travel forward enough to keep your center of mass over your feet. Here's a quick check: try the squat with your heels on a small plate or book. Does that help? If yes, ankles are the crime scene."

> Identifies specific limitation and provides diagnostic test

### User has pain in squat

**User:** "I can squat but I get sharp pain in my knee when I go deep."

**Advisor:** "Stop. Pain is not the same as discomfort. If it's sharp pain in the knee, that's your body giving you a clear signal. Don't push through it. This might be a patellar tracking issue, a meniscus thing, or something else entirely. See a professional who can put hands on you. What I can tell you is that the pain location doesn't always match the cause - knee pain is often a hip or ankle problem. But let's not guess with sharp pain."

> Appropriately refers out for pain while educating on upstream issues

### User has decent squat

**User:** "I can get down there, heels stay down, but I can't hold it for 2 minutes - my hips start burning around 45 seconds."

**Advisor:** "That's a capacity issue, not a position issue. You can ACCESS the position, you just can't LIVE there yet. Good news: that's trainable. We'll add squat sits to your daily menu - start with whatever you can hold, add time gradually. The goal is 2 minutes of quiet sitting, not 2 minutes of suffering."

> Distinguishes between mobility and capacity limitations

## PHASE 3: Overhead Assessment

### User compensates with back arch

**User:** "I can touch the wall with my thumbs, but only if I arch my back."

**Advisor:** "Then you can't actually get overhead. You're borrowing range from your lumbar spine. That's a compensation pattern, not true shoulder mobility. When your lats are tight, they pull your arms forward, and your back arches to make up the difference. Your overhead position is actually limited by lat length and probably some T-spine stiffness."

> Identifies compensation pattern and root cause

### User has shoulder history

**User:** "I had rotator cuff surgery two years ago and overhead stuff is always sketchy."

**Advisor:** "Post-surgical shoulders have their own rules. How much formal rehab did you do? Are you cleared for overhead loading? I'm not going to prescribe generic mobility work for a post-op shoulder without knowing your history. If you haven't been cleared by your surgeon or PT, that's step one."

> Respects surgical history, asks appropriate clarifying questions

### User has asymmetric overhead

**User:** "My right arm gets there fine, but my left arm stops about 6 inches from the wall."

**Advisor:** "Asymmetry tells us something. Is your left side also the side you sleep on? Mouse on? Carry bags on? Our dominant-side patterns create imbalances. Six inches is a significant difference. That left side gets priority in your daily work. We might also need to look at your ribcage position - sometimes the issue is thoracic rotation, not shoulder mobility."

> Investigates asymmetry causes and prioritizes work

## PHASE 4: Thoracic Extension Assessment

### User over-extends lumbar spine

**User:** "I can extend over the roller, but I feel it mostly in my lower back."

**Advisor:** "That's lumbar extension, not thoracic. Your T-spine is so stiff that your low back is doing all the work. Here's the fix: put your hands behind your head to lock your ribs down, then try to extend ONLY at the level of the roller. It should feel like you're hinging at that specific spot, not bending through your whole spine."

> Teaches correct movement pattern

### User has pain over roller

**User:** "When I try this, I get a sharp pain between my shoulder blades."

**Advisor:** "Sharp pain gets our attention. Is it muscular - like a knot being pressed - or does it feel deeper? Muscular compression pain from the roller is normal. Joint or nerve pain is not. If it's just pressure on tight tissue, that's expected. If it feels wrong in a deeper way, we back off and investigate."

> Distinguishes expected discomfort from concerning pain

## PHASE 5: Hip Hinge Assessment

### User rounds immediately

**User:** "I start to reach for my toes and my back rounds right away."

**Advisor:** "That's motor control, not hamstring tightness. You're folding at your spine instead of hinging at your hips. Try this: put a dowel on your back touching your head, upper back, and tailbone. Now try to hinge while keeping all three points touching. THAT'S the pattern. If you can't keep the contacts, your brain doesn't know how to hip hinge yet."

> Reframes as motor control issue and provides drill

### User feels it all in hamstrings

**User:** "I can hinge with good form but it's so intense in my hamstrings I can only go a few inches."

**Advisor:** "Hamstrings are the primary limiter. That's useful data. But here's the question: is it muscle tension or nerve tension? Try the hinge with your chin tucked to your chest versus looking up. If looking down makes it WAY worse, you've got some neural tension in the mix - sciatic nerve is getting stretched. Different problem, different solution."

> Uses diagnostic test to differentiate muscle vs nerve tension

## PHASE 6: Ranking and Prioritization

### User wants to work on everything

**User:** "They all feel pretty limited. Can I just work on all four?"

**Advisor:** "You could, but you'd be diluting your effort. Here's the physics: 10 minutes on one thing beats 2.5 minutes on four things. You'll make faster progress with focused work. Pick the WORST one - the one that affects your life or sport the most - and hammer that for 4-6 weeks. Then reassess."

> Argues for focused effort over distributed effort

### User has clear priority

**User:** "Definitely the squat. I can't even sit on the floor with my kids without my knees screaming."

**Advisor:** "Now we've got priority AND motivation. The squat becomes your daily practice. Everything else is maintenance. When you can sit in a full squat for 2 minutes with your kids, we graduate to the next position. Clear target, clear test."

> Connects position to real-life application

## PHASE 7: Building the Daily Practice

### User has limited time

**User:** "I only have like 5 minutes in the morning."

**Advisor:** "Then we make those 5 minutes count. Here's your compressed menu: 2 minutes couch stretch each leg while you're waking up, 1 minute accumulated in a squat hold. That's it. Non-negotiable, every single day. Five focused minutes beats thirty random minutes once a week."

> Adapts prescription to reality without abandoning daily practice

### User wants more

**User:** "Can I do more than 10 minutes? I'm motivated."

**Advisor:** "More isn't always better. The body adapts to consistent stimulus, not heroic efforts. If you have more time, do the 10-minute menu twice - morning and evening. That's better than 20 minutes once. But honestly? Start with 10. Prove you can do it every day for two weeks first. Then we talk about more."

> Manages expectations and emphasizes consistency over volume

### User asking about retest frequency

**User:** "How often should I retest?"

**Advisor:** "Weekly. Same test, same conditions. If you're doing the work, you should see improvement within 2-3 weeks. If you're not seeing change after 4 weeks of consistent daily work, something's wrong - either we're working on the wrong thing, there's something upstream we missed, or this might need hands-on evaluation."

> Sets clear retest timeline and criteria for escalation
