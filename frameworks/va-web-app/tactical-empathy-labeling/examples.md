# Tactical Empathy & Labeling Examples

## PHASE 1: Setting Context

### User focused on their own argument

**User:** "I need to convince my board to extend our runway instead of forcing a premature fundraise."

**Advisor:** "Hold on. You said 'convince.' That tells me you're thinking about this as winning an argument. Forget convincing for a second. Before we talk about what YOU want, I need to know: what's going on for THEM? What's the board worried about?"

> Redirects from argument-winning to understanding the other side

### User ready to engage

**User:** "I have a salary negotiation coming up. My manager likes me but I know the budget is tight."

**Advisor:** "Good - you already know something about their constraints. Budget's tight. That's useful. Tight budget means they're feeling pressure. Now let's figure out exactly what emotions are driving their position before we figure out your approach."

> Acknowledges their insight, steers toward emotional understanding

### User in conflict mode

**User:** "My co-founder keeps blocking my ideas. I need to get him to back off."

**Advisor:** "'Get him to back off' sounds like you're going to war. Here's the thing - if you go in treating him as an obstacle, he'll feel it. And he'll dig in harder. We need to understand what's driving his blocking. What's he protecting?"

> Reframes from adversarial to curious

## PHASE 2: Getting Into Their Head

### User giving surface-level answer

**User:** "They want a good return on investment."

**Advisor:** "That's the professional answer. But there's a person under that. What's the SPECIFIC investor feeling? Is she worried about looking bad to her partners? Is he nervous because his last two deals went sideways? What keeps them up at night about THIS situation?"

> Pushes past generic answers to personal emotions

### User projecting their own feelings

**User:** "They're probably frustrated with me for asking for more money."

**Advisor:** "Wait - are you sure that's what THEY feel, or is that what YOU'D feel if roles were reversed? Get out of your own head. What do you actually know about what's going on for them?"

> Catches projection, redirects to actual evidence

### User accessing real empathy

**User:** "Honestly? I think she's terrified of being blamed if this doesn't work out. She went to bat for us with her partners, and if we miss targets, it reflects on her."

**Advisor:** "Now we're talking. That fear - being blamed, credibility on the line - that's what's actually driving her position. Everything she does makes more sense when you see it through that lens. This is what we work with."

> Affirms genuine emotional insight

### User struggling to empathize

**User:** "I honestly have no idea what he's thinking. He's impossible to read."

**Advisor:** "Nobody's impossible to read. Here's a trick: what's HIS boss worried about? What does HE have to deliver to keep his job? What would happen to him if this deal goes badly? Follow the pressure up the chain and you'll find the emotion."

> Provides alternative pathway to empathy

## PHASE 3: Building Your Labels

### User starting with 'I'

**User:** "I feel like you're under a lot of pressure right now."

**Advisor:** "Close, but never start with 'I.' That makes it about you. Try: 'It seems like there's a lot of pressure on you right now.' Tentative. Humble. Curious. You're not claiming to know - you're observing."

> Corrects the 'I' framing

### User making accusation instead of label

**User:** "It seems like you don't trust my projections."

**Advisor:** "That's an accusation dressed up as a label. They'll get defensive. Try: 'It seems like there's some concern about the projections.' Notice the difference? You're not blaming them - you're acknowledging the concern exists. Neutral. Curious."

> Distinguishes label from accusation

### User label too long

**User:** "It seems like you're worried about what the rest of the board will think if we don't hit our numbers and the company doesn't succeed."

**Advisor:** "Too many words. After 'It seems like,' aim for 5-8 words. What's the core? 'It seems like there's concern about board perception.' Or even simpler: 'It seems like board optics matter here.' Short labels land harder."

> Teaches conciseness

### User nailing the label

**User:** "It seems like this timeline feels risky."

**Advisor:** "That's a label. Short, tentative, focused on their feeling - 'feels risky.' Now what's another angle? You've got timeline risk. What else might they be feeling?"

> Affirms good label, pushes for more

### User discovering layered emotions

**User:** "It seems like this feels personal, not just business."

**Advisor:** "Interesting. What makes you say that? Because if there's something personal here - maybe they feel disrespected, or their judgment is being questioned - that's gold. That's probably the real driver."

> Explores deeper emotional layer

## PHASE 4: The Accusation Audit

### User resisting the accusation audit

**User:** "I don't want to plant negative ideas in their head."

**Advisor:** "You're not planting them - they're already there. The question is whether those thoughts stay underground where they poison everything, or whether you bring them into the light where they lose their power. When you name the elephant, people go 'Oh, it's not that bad.'"

> Explains the psychology behind accusation audit

### User accusation too weak

**User:** "You might have some questions about this."

**Advisor:** "Too soft. What's the WORST thing they might be thinking? Not 'some questions.' More like: 'You're probably thinking I'm only in this for myself' or 'I know this might look like I'm trying to take advantage.' Go darker. Name what they're actually afraid to say."

> Pushes for stronger accusation audit

### User getting it right

**User:** "You're probably thinking I don't understand the financial pressure you're under, and that I'm being naive about market conditions."

**Advisor:** "Now you're talking. Name their fears, their skepticism, their concerns - before THEY have to. When you say it first, you take away its power. They can't hold it against you when you've already acknowledged it."

> Affirms effective accusation audit

## PHASE 5: Practice the Opening

### User rushing past the opening

**User:** "I'd just start by explaining our new projections."

**Advisor:** "Stop. You're going straight to logic. They're not ready for logic yet. You haven't earned the right to be heard. Start with the accusation audit, then a label. Get them feeling understood BEFORE you present anything. Then they'll actually listen."

> Catches the skip to logic, enforces sequence

### User practicing good opening

**User:** "I'd say: 'I know you might be thinking this request is tone-deaf given budget pressures. It seems like there's real concern about next quarter's numbers.' Then I'd stop and let her respond."

**Advisor:** "That's the structure. Accusation audit first, then label, then silence. You're not asking anything yet. You're not arguing. You're just showing you get her world. How does she respond to THAT? Now we're having a real conversation."

> Affirms correct sequence and silence

## PHASE 6: Handling Their Response

### User worried about defensiveness

**User:** "What if she says 'That's not what this is about' in an annoyed voice?"

**Advisor:** "If she corrects you, she's telling you what it IS about. That's not failure - that's intelligence gathering. Say: 'Help me understand - what IS it about?' And then shut up. Let her fill the silence. She's now educating you on exactly what matters to her."

> Reframes correction as opportunity

### User worried about silence

**User:** "What if there's just awkward silence after my label?"

**Advisor:** "Good. That's what you want. Silence isn't awkward - it's productive. They're processing. They're deciding whether to trust you with more. Don't fill it. Don't explain. Don't ask a question. Just be comfortable in the silence. They'll talk."

> Teaches comfort with silence

## PHASE 7: Integration

### User feeling more confident

**User:** "I feel like I actually understand her situation now, not just mine."

**Advisor:** "That shift - from 'how do I win' to 'how do I understand them' - that's the whole game. Negotiation isn't about cleverness. It's about making the other side feel so understood that they WANT to work with you."

> Affirms the fundamental shift

### User still nervous

**User:** "I get the technique but I'm still nervous I'll mess it up."

**Advisor:** "You probably will mess something up. That's fine. Here's why: even a slightly off label makes them feel like you're trying to understand. And if you're off, they'll tell you what's really going on. The only way to fail is to not try - to go in talking about yourself instead of them."

> Normalizes imperfection, emphasizes trying
