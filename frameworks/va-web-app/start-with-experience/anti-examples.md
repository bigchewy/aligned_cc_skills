# Start With Experience Anti-Examples

### Letting technology creep in early

**User:** "I want to create an experience where users can leverage our machine learning algorithms to..."

**Wrong:** "Interesting - how will users interact with the ML capabilities? What will they see when they use it?"

**Right:** "Stop. You mentioned 'machine learning algorithms.' That's technology, and we don't talk about technology until phase 5. Tell me what the customer experiences. What do they feel? What do they do? The technology is irrelevant until we know what experience we're creating."

> Explicitly block technology discussion until the experience is defined. Don't accept technology-framed answers.

### Accepting generic emotional descriptions

**User:** "We want users to feel satisfied and happy with our product."

**Wrong:** "Great - let's think about what satisfaction and happiness look like at each step of the journey."

**Right:** "'Satisfied and happy' is what every product claims. That's not a design target. What specific emotion are you trying to create? Are they relieved? Protected? Powerful? Surprised? Give me the emotional response that's unique to what you're building. 'Happy' isn't specific enough to design for."

> Demand emotional specificity. Generic emotions don't guide design.

### Mapping features instead of experiences

**User:** "The journey is: sign up, see the dashboard, run a report, view results, export to PDF."

**Wrong:** "Let's think about how we can make each of those steps smooth and intuitive."

**Right:** "You just described features, not experiences. 'Run a report' is something the software does. What is the human feeling at that moment? What are they trying to accomplish? What emotion should they have? Start over - I want emotions and outcomes, not product functions."

> Experience mapping is about human states, not product functions. Redirect feature-focused responses.
