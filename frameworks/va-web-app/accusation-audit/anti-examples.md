# Accusation Audit Anti-Examples

### Accepting surface-level negatives

**User:** "They might have some concerns about the timeline."

**Wrong:** "Good, concerns about the timeline. Let's turn that into an accusation audit item."

**Right:** "That's too rational and safe. What might they think about YOU? Not the timeline - you. Are you unreliable? Are you making excuses? Are you in over your head? Go to what makes you uncomfortable to say."

> Surface concerns don't have emotional power. The accusation audit works because it names the personal fears and judgments that people hold but don't say. 'Concerns about timeline' is a professional euphemism - we need the raw fear underneath.

### Letting the user defend during the audit

**User:** "I'd say: 'You're probably thinking I dropped the ball, but actually the vendor was late.'"

**Wrong:** "That's a good start. The 'but' helps clarify that it wasn't your fault."

**Right:** "Stop at 'dropped the ball.' No 'but.' No defense. The accusation audit is ONLY naming their fear - you don't explain or defend in the same breath. That's a different conversation. Right now, just: 'You're probably thinking I dropped the ball.' Period. Let it sit."

> When you add 'but' and defend, you undercut the whole technique. The power comes from naming the elephant without immediately trying to chase it away. The defense, if any, comes later after they've responded.

### Accepting weak phrasing

**User:** "You might possibly have some thoughts about my performance."

**Wrong:** "That's a start. Let's think about what thoughts they might have."

**Right:** "Too vague, too hedged. 'Might possibly have some thoughts' - there's no power in that. What's the SPECIFIC fear? 'You're probably wondering if I'm the wrong person for this job.' Direct. Specific. That's what moves people."

> Vague, hedged language signals you're not really willing to name the elephant. The accusation audit requires courage - stating the worst thing they might think in plain language. Multiple qualifiers ('might possibly have some thoughts') drain all the power.

### Letting the user skip to solutions

**User:** "After I do the accusation audit, I'll immediately explain my plan to fix everything."

**Wrong:** "Good thinking - having a plan ready shows you're proactive."

**Right:** "Not so fast. After the accusation audit, you PAUSE. You wait for their reaction. Maybe they say 'No, we don't think that.' Maybe they reveal what they're REALLY worried about. If you jump straight to your fix, you miss the whole point - which is to hear what they actually think before you start solving."

> The silence after the accusation audit is where the real work happens. Jumping to solutions robs you of the information you need and signals that the audit was just a preamble, not a genuine invitation to respond.

### Accepting accusation audit as reassurance-seeking

**User:** "I'd say: 'You're probably disappointed in me, right? Please tell me you're not too upset.'"

**Wrong:** "That shows you care about the relationship. Let's refine the wording."

**Right:** "That's not an accusation audit - that's fishing for reassurance. Hear the difference: 'You're probably disappointed in me' (statement, dropped with confidence) versus 'Please tell me you're not too upset' (plea for comfort). The audit names the fear matter-of-factly. It doesn't ask to be made to feel better."

> The accusation audit must be delivered from a place of emotional groundedness, not neediness. When it becomes a request for reassurance, it puts the other person in the position of managing your emotions rather than engaging with the substance.
