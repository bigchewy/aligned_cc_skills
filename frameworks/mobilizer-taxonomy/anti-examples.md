# Mobilizer Taxonomy Anti-Examples

### Prefixing generic role labels with "Mobilizer profile:"

**User:** "Here's my stakeholder list: Sarah is the economic buyer, Dave is the technical gatekeeper, Priya is the legal reviewer, and Mike is FYI."

**Wrong:** "Got it. Let's profile:
- Sarah → Mobilizer profile: Economic Buyer
- Dave → Mobilizer profile: Technical Gatekeeper
- Priya → Mobilizer profile: Legal Reviewer
- Mike → Mobilizer profile: FYI"

**Right:** "Stop — 'Economic Buyer,' 'Technical Gatekeeper,' 'Legal Reviewer,' and 'FYI' are not Dixon profiles. They're role or function labels. The Mobilizer Taxonomy has exactly seven profiles: Go-Getter, Teacher, Skeptic (the three Mobilizers) and Guide, Friend, Climber, Blocker (the four Talkers).

We'll track both pieces of information, but we'll keep them separate:
- **Role** — their org function (economic buyer, technical lead, legal reviewer, etc.)
- **Profile** — one of the seven Dixon archetypes, based on their actual buying behavior

So the right first pass looks like:
- Sarah — Role: Economic Buyer. Profile: ? (need to know what she's done in the process)
- Dave — Role: Technical Gatekeeper. Profile: ? (same)
- Priya — Role: Legal Reviewer. Profile: ? (same)
- Mike — Role: FYI. Profile: ? — though if he's truly only FYI, he may not warrant profiling at all

Now tell me what each of them has actually done in this buying process, and we'll assign profiles — observed where we can, inferred with behavioral tests where we can't."

> The seven profiles are a closed set. Generic org-function labels are not Dixon terms. Conflating the two is the failure that prompted this framework's creation. Keep role and profile on separate axes.

### Assigning unqualified profiles to stakeholders the user has never met

**User:** "We haven't talked to the CFO yet, but based on their title and the industry, I'd guess they're a Blocker."

**Wrong:** "CFO → Blocker. CFOs in this industry tend to block non-infrastructure SaaS purchases."

**Right:** "That's a guess, not an observation — and the distinction matters. We assign unqualified profiles only when we've seen the buying behavior. When we haven't, we tag it Inferred with a specific test.

CFO → **Inferred: Blocker**. Based on title + industry pattern + no observed engagement. **Behavioral test:** Set up a 30-minute direct meeting with the CFO in the next two weeks. Watch whether they engage substantively with the business case or push back reflexively on cost. Also ask your internal contact whether the CFO has privately weighed in — silence on shared threads but private support flips this to Inferred Guide or Teacher. Silence everywhere with a track record of blocking similar deals confirms Blocker.

This matters because an unqualified 'Blocker' tag will shape your strategy — you'll start routing around them — and if you're wrong, you'll route around someone who would have supported the deal. The Inferred tag keeps your posture diagnostic, not defensive."

> Never let a guess carry the weight of an observation. Inferred tags protect strategy from misreads.

### Upgrading a Friend to Mobilizer because they feel like a champion

**User:** "Jamie is definitely our champion. She takes every call, she forwards us internal docs, she's told me she's 'rooting for us.' I want to profile her as a Go-Getter."

**Wrong:** "Great — Jamie is your Go-Getter. She's the one driving the deal internally."

**Right:** "Hold on — let me run the test before we tag Jamie as a Mobilizer. Answer these three:

1. Has Jamie rallied another stakeholder on the committee? Not 'been in meetings with' — actually lobbied someone to support the deal.
2. Has Jamie put any political capital on the line — made an ask of a higher-up, challenged someone's objection, staked her reputation?
3. Has Jamie taken an action on your behalf when you weren't there — prepared a peer before a call, sent a summary to the CFO, pushed the timeline?

If the answer is yes to any of them, she may be a Go-Getter or a Teacher. If the answer is no to all three — which is what I suspect based on what you've described — she's a **Friend**, not a Mobilizer. 'Rooting for us,' taking calls, forwarding docs, being warm — those are Friend behaviors. They feel like championing. They are not championing.

This is the #1 misdiagnosis in the Challenger Customer data, and it's correlated with losing deals to no-decision. Friends are fine people. They're not your internal engine. You can keep the relationship and still run your actual deal strategy through someone who's actually mobilizing — which, based on this committee, is Priya."

> The Friend-as-Mobilizer misdiagnosis is the foundational mistake this framework exists to prevent. Apply the test rigorously every time.

### Reading tough questions as Blocker behavior

**User:** "The Head of Security has been brutal — she's raised twelve concerns about our architecture across three calls. She's definitely a Blocker."

**Wrong:** "Head of Security → Blocker. The volume of objections is the signal."

**Right:** "Volume of objections alone isn't the signal. Form of objections is. Let me ask: when you've addressed her concerns with substantive answers, what does she do?

- If she accepts the answer and moves to the next concern, or escalates with another real question → **Skeptic**. Skeptics raise hard questions because they're the kind of people who don't approve things lightly. Once you win them, they carry more weight inside the org than anyone.
- If she ignores your answers, resurfaces the same objection in different forms, or recruits peers to object alongside her → **Blocker**.

Twelve concerns across three calls sounds like a Skeptic doing their job, not a Blocker. Skeptics are the single most misdiagnosed profile as Blockers. The research finding: Skeptics are gold. Lose them and you lose a future advocate. Treat them as a Blocker, try to route around them, and you'll either confirm their suspicion that you're not serious, or you'll alienate the one person whose endorsement would have carried the room.

What has she done after you've answered her questions? That's the behavior we need to profile on — not the fact that she asks hard ones."

> Tough questions are a feature, not a bug, in the Skeptic profile. The Skeptic-as-Blocker misread kills deals the same way the Friend-as-Mobilizer misread does — different direction, same type of error.

### Skipping a stakeholder because you "don't know them"

**User:** "Let's skip Anjali. I've never talked to her, I don't know anything about her."

**Wrong:** "Okay, we'll skip Anjali and come back to her later once you have more information."

**Right:** "We don't skip her — we use the Inferred pathway. The whole point of this framework is that 'I don't know them yet' is handled explicitly: we assign a best-guess profile based on what we do know (role, how they got added to the thread, any secondhand info) and we attach a specific behavioral test.

So for Anjali — what do we know? Chief of Staff role. Added to the thread two weeks ago. What prompted the addition? Anything Priya said about her?

Inferred-with-test does two things that skipping doesn't. First, it gives you a lens for the first interaction — you'll walk into that conversation knowing what to watch for, instead of forming a baseline from scratch. Second, it forces the question 'how would I find out?' — which becomes your next move. If we can't even construct a behavioral test, that tells us we need to engineer an interaction first.

Who ARE you skipping when you skip her? Chiefs of Staff can be Teachers with CEO authority behind them. They can also be Guides who just need visibility. Those two profiles would route your strategy completely differently. We can't afford to leave that blank."

> Skipping a stakeholder is a silent failure mode. Inferred tags with behavioral tests are specifically designed to prevent it. Always profile every stakeholder — even if the profile is a question.

### Tagging the whole committee as Mobilizers

**User:** "Honestly, the whole committee has been great. I'd say most of them are Go-Getters."

**Wrong:** "Agreed — sounds like a strong deal. Let's tag the committee as mostly Go-Getters and move on."

**Right:** "That's almost certainly not what's happening. The research shows most complex deals have one, maybe two actual Mobilizers — the rest are Talkers (Guides, Friends, Climbers) with one or two Blockers. A committee that's 'mostly Go-Getters' is not a realistic distribution.

What's more likely is that most of the committee is friendly and responsive, and you're reading responsive-and-friendly as mobilizing. Those feel identical from the rep's seat. They behave completely differently inside the org.

Let's go person by person and run the Mobilizer test on each: has this person specifically rallied someone else on the committee, put political capital on the line, or taken an action on your behalf when you weren't in the room? If the answer isn't a clean 'yes,' they're not a Mobilizer. They might be a helpful Guide or a warm Friend — those are different profiles with different implications for the deal.

If at the end of this we genuinely do have five Mobilizers on one committee, that's a remarkable deal and we'll verify. Much more likely we find the usual distribution: two Mobilizers, four Talkers, and one wildcard. That's a realistic picture, and it's the one we can actually work with."

> Distribution realism is a calibration check. A rep who tags most of a committee as Mobilizers is usually reading engagement-with-them as engagement-on-their-behalf. Different signals, different profiles.

### Running the Taxonomy on a simple transactional deal

**User:** "Can you help me map the buying committee for this $8K/year SMB deal? The business owner is the only person I've talked to, but his bookkeeper might need to approve the expense."

**Wrong:** "Sure — let's profile the business owner and the bookkeeper using the seven Dixon profiles."

**Right:** "The Mobilizer Taxonomy is calibrated for complex B2B enterprise purchases — multiple stakeholders across functions, long cycles, high perceived risk, consensus-building required. An $8K SMB deal with a business owner and a bookkeeper doesn't fit that shape, and forcing the framework onto it produces false precision.

Two people, one with veto, one with authority, is a two-person decision. It's not a committee purchase and it doesn't stall on consensus dynamics — if it stalls, it's probably budget timing, unclear value, or the owner wasn't actually qualified.

If this is your whole pipeline, let's talk about the fit between your sales motion and this deal shape — the Challenger and Mobilizer methodologies aren't going to be the right tools for SMB transactional deals. If this is a one-off alongside larger enterprise deals, work it on qualification fundamentals, not stakeholder mapping."

> The research base is enterprise. Don't force the framework onto deal shapes it wasn't designed for. Name the mismatch and redirect.
