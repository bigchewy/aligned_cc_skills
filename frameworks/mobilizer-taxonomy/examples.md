# Mobilizer Taxonomy Examples

A running example: a $750K ARR enterprise SaaS deal in month four. The rep has observed behavior from three stakeholders on the buying committee and has limited/secondhand information on four more.

---

## PHASE 1: Frame the Deal and List the Committee

### User provides a realistic committee

**User:** "It's a $750K ARR deal with Meridian Health. Four months in. Here's who's involved:
1. Priya Shah, VP of Revenue Operations — she runs every call, owns the evaluation process
2. Marcus Chen, Director of IT — on most technical calls, has asked hard questions
3. Lisa Whitmore, Senior Manager of Sales Ops — originally sourced us, super responsive
4. Kevin Park, CFO — copied on major threads, hasn't joined a call yet
5. Diana Reyes, Head of Legal — will run the contract review
6. Tom Brennan, VP of Sales — the end-user business owner, shows up intermittently
7. Anjali Kapoor, Chief of Staff to the CEO — got added to the thread two weeks ago"

**Advisor:** "Good — that's a realistic complex-deal committee. Seven stakeholders, close to the research average of 6.8. We'll go through all seven.

Two things to pay attention to as we do this. First, most deals have one or maybe two actual Mobilizers. Most of this committee is going to land as Talkers or Blockers. That's normal — the exercise isn't about finding a lot of Mobilizers, it's about correctly identifying the few you have.

Second, I'm going to ask the same question for each person: have you been in a buying conversation with them, and what did they do? If you have observed behavior, we assign a profile. If you don't, we infer with a behavioral test.

Let's start with Priya."

> Accepts a realistic committee. Pre-frames the distribution so the user isn't disappointed when most stakeholders come back as Talkers. Names the observation-vs-inference rule explicitly.

### User lists only two stakeholders

**User:** "I've been mostly working with the champion, Lisa, and her boss Priya."

**Advisor:** "That's a thin committee for a $750K deal. The research average is around 6.8 stakeholders in complex enterprise purchases. Either this deal is genuinely simpler than average — possible, but unusual at this price point — or there are stakeholders you haven't surfaced yet.

Think about what you've heard on calls. Has anyone said 'we'll need IT to sign off on the integration'? 'Legal will have questions about data residency'? 'The CFO wants to see ROI before we sign'? Anyone copied on threads who hasn't spoken? Anyone your champion has referenced by name?

Let's surface them before we profile. A map with two people on it isn't a map."

> Pushes back on a suspiciously small committee. The work of profiling is useless if half the committee is missing.

---

## PHASE 2: Profile Each Stakeholder — Observed

### Stakeholder 1 — Priya Shah, VP of RevOps (OBSERVED)

**Advisor:** "Tell me about Priya. What have you observed her do in the context of this purchase?"

**User:** "She's the one who set up the evaluation process. She built the scoring rubric, she scheduled the vendor demos, she's sent agendas in advance for every call. Two weeks ago she pulled together a 15-minute sync with just her and me to walk through where the internal team was aligned and where there were still gaps — she'd done her own temperature check of the committee and wanted to debrief."

**Advisor:** "Priya → **Go-Getter**. Building her own evaluation rubric, scheduling the process, doing her own internal temperature checks and then proactively debriefing you — that's self-motivated improver behavior all the way down. She's not waiting for you to run the process; she's running it, and she's bringing you into her process. That's classic Go-Getter.

One note: Go-Getters can sometimes over-own the process and lose peers in the committee. Watch that Priya isn't getting out ahead of the rest of the team. If nobody else on the committee has the same level of engagement, the deal is on her shoulders alone. But this is a strong profile. Next — Marcus Chen, the Director of IT. What have you seen him do?"

> Grounds the assignment in the specific behaviors the user described. Adds a forward-looking risk so the profile is operational, not just a tag. Moves directly to the next stakeholder.

### Stakeholder 2 — Marcus Chen, Director of IT (OBSERVED)

**User:** "Marcus was tough for the first three calls. He raised every possible concern — integration complexity, data residency, what happens when our API changes, security posture of our subprocessors, the works. We worked through each one, brought in our security team for a detailed review, and gave him full API documentation. Two calls ago he said 'okay, I think I'm satisfied on the technical risk side' and since then he's actually been pushing the timeline — he told Priya on the last call that IT readiness shouldn't be the blocker."

**Advisor:** "Marcus → **Skeptic**. Textbook pattern. Hard questions for the first three calls — real, substantive technical questions, not generic blocking — and once the evidence landed, he flipped into pushing the timeline. That's the thing Skeptics do that Blockers don't: they accept the answer and change position.

The research finding here is important: most reps dread Skeptics early and misread them as Blockers. They're the opposite. The Skeptic's toughness IS their value — once they're convinced, they carry more weight internally than anyone because the rest of the committee knows Marcus doesn't approve things lightly. Keep him close. If the deal hits another technical snag, he's now the person who'll help you clear it, not block you on it.

Next — Lisa Whitmore. You mentioned she originally sourced you and is 'super responsive.' What specifically have you seen her do in the buying process?"

> Names the Skeptic pattern and contrasts with Blocker explicitly. Flags the common misread. Frames the forward-looking use of the Skeptic as an internal asset.

### Stakeholder 3 — Lisa Whitmore, Senior Manager Sales Ops (OBSERVED — Friend)

**User:** "Lisa found us at a conference, she's taken every single call, she forwards us relevant info from the internal side, she's friendly, she says things like 'I can't wait for us to be using this.' She tells me I'm doing great. Last week she sent me a meme about procurement taking forever."

**Advisor:** "Slow down on this one. Let me ask the test question. Has Lisa rallied anyone on the committee? Has she made a specific ask of Priya, Marcus, or anyone else — 'please prioritize this,' 'please champion this,' 'please come to this meeting'? Has she put political capital on the line?"

**User:** "Hmm. Not really. She's responsive with me, but she hasn't pushed anyone else internally that I know of."

**Advisor:** "Lisa → **Friend**. That's the Friend signature. Accessible, warm, responsive, never moves the deal internally. She feels like a champion because she's the one who talks to you the most, but consensus-building is something she hasn't done. That doesn't make her bad news — Friends are often fine Guides if you ask them specifically for internal intel. But do not run your deal strategy through her as if she's a Mobilizer.

The common mistake here is promoting a Friend to 'champion' status in your internal deal reviews because the relationship feels strongest with them. In the Challenger Customer data, this is the single most common misdiagnosis and it's correlated with losing-to-no-decision. Stay friendly with Lisa, use her for org intel, but the consensus push has to come from Priya, Marcus, or someone we haven't yet identified.

Next — Kevin Park, the CFO. You said he's copied on major threads but hasn't joined a call. That's different from observed behavior. What do we actually know about him from the buying context?"

> Uses the Friend test explicitly (has she rallied anyone?). Names the research finding on Friend-as-champion misdiagnosis. Explicitly redirects to the next stakeholder with a note that we're moving from observed to inferred territory.

## PHASE 2 continued: Profile Each Stakeholder — Inferred with Test

### Stakeholder 4 — Kevin Park, CFO (INFERRED)

**User:** "Kevin has been cc'd on every proposal version and every stakeholder-wide email. He's never replied. Lisa mentioned once that 'Kevin has to sign off on anything over $500K' and that he's 'conservative on non-infrastructure SaaS.'"

**Advisor:** "Kevin → **Inferred: Blocker**. Based on two things: secondhand info that he's 'conservative on non-infrastructure SaaS' — that's a values signal that doesn't align with this purchase — and silence on threads where engaged participants would have chimed in. He's at the financial approval gate, and nothing we've observed suggests he's leaning in.

But we don't actually know. He could be disengaged until the final review, at which point he may approve routinely. Or he could be quietly preparing to push back.

**Behavioral test:** The next stakeholder-wide email with a substantive update, watch whether Kevin responds, asks a question, or stays silent. More revealing — ask Priya specifically whether Kevin has weighed in privately with her. If he has and it's positive, he may be an Inferred Guide or even Teacher and we've misread the silence. If he hasn't and Priya seems nervous about his eventual response, that's Blocker signal. Schedule a direct meeting with Kevin within the next two weeks — you can't diagnose a CFO from thread silence.

Next — Diana Reyes, Head of Legal. What do we have on her?"

> Gives a best-guess profile, tags it clearly as inferred, and gives a specific, observable test that converts inference into observation. Names the action the rep must take (direct meeting).

### Stakeholder 5 — Diana Reyes, Head of Legal (INFERRED)

**User:** "Haven't interacted with her yet. Lisa told me Legal blocked two prior vendor evaluations — a different CRM and a security tool — saying the contract language was 'too vendor-friendly.'"

**Advisor:** "Diana → **Inferred: Blocker**. Based on the track record Lisa described — blocking two prior vendors on contract language is a pattern, not a coincidence. Legal functions often map to Blocker when the person in the role is rewarded for stopping bad deals rather than shipping good ones.

However — and this matters — some Heads of Legal are Skeptics, not Blockers. Skeptics raise every concern and then help you solve them. Blockers raise concerns and use them as stoppers. The track record so far sounds more like Blocker, but we don't know how she approaches OUR contract.

**Behavioral test:** On the first legal review call, pay attention to the FORM of her objections. 'Here are three problems we need to solve together — can your legal team turn these around by Friday?' → Skeptic. 'Here are three problems and this contract isn't going to work for us' with no path forward → Blocker. Also: does she propose alternate language, or does she only reject? Proposal = Skeptic. Only rejection = Blocker.

If she's a Skeptic, Marcus's success pattern works — bring the evidence, resolve concerns substantively, and she becomes an advocate. If she's a Blocker, you'll need Priya or an exec sponsor to carry the deal around her.

Next — Tom Brennan, VP of Sales, the end-user business owner."

> Distinguishes the two most-commonly-confused profiles (Skeptic vs. Blocker) for a role that can be either. Gives concrete language cues as the behavioral test.

### Stakeholder 6 — Tom Brennan, VP of Sales (INFERRED)

**User:** "Tom is the end-user business owner — this tool supports his sales org. He's been on two of our seven calls. When he's on, he's engaged and positive, but he mostly defers to Priya on process questions and hasn't independently driven anything."

**Advisor:** "Tom → **Inferred: Guide or Friend**. This is the murky middle. Engaged-when-present but not driving anything could be a Friend (pleasant, inert) or a Guide (gives internal intel when asked, doesn't build consensus).

The real question is whether Tom COULD be a Mobilizer if activated, because as the business owner of the end-user team, he has natural standing to rally adoption. Right now he's being passive.

**Behavioral test:** In your next interaction, ask him directly: 'Tom, if we close this in the next six weeks, what would rollout look like for your team? Who on your team do I need to talk to before launch?' A Mobilizer answers specifically — names people, sketches the rollout, offers to make intros. A Guide gives you names and process info but won't offer to act. A Friend stays vague and pleasant without committing to anything.

If he responds like a Mobilizer, upgrade him — and notice that your 'Mobilizer count' just jumped from two to three, which is a different deal. If he stays passive, stop running strategy through him and route all end-user-adoption questions through Priya.

Next — Anjali Kapoor, Chief of Staff to the CEO. She was added two weeks ago."

> Handles the murky middle case explicitly. The behavioral test is a question the rep should actually ask, with calibrated response interpretations.

### Stakeholder 7 — Anjali Kapoor, CoS to CEO (INFERRED)

**User:** "Anjali got added to the thread two weeks ago by Priya. Priya said 'looping in Anjali so she's aware' and gave no more context. Anjali hasn't said anything."

**Advisor:** "Anjali → **Inferred: Guide**. Chief of Staff roles often map to Guide because CoS by job description knows everyone and every process but doesn't build consensus — they help the principal (in this case the CEO) build consensus instead. The two-weeks-ago addition with 'so she's aware' framing reads as 'the CEO wants visibility' rather than 'she's driving anything.'

But watch out — some Chiefs of Staff are active Teachers on behalf of the principal. They show up, frame the problem, shape the room, and others defer because the principal's authority flows through them.

**Behavioral test:** In the next interaction where Anjali is present, watch whether she speaks. If she asks a clarifying question and then goes quiet → Guide. If she reframes the discussion or synthesizes where the committee stands → Teacher (and you've got a Mobilizer flowing from the CEO's office, which changes everything). If she stays silent and exits the thread in a few weeks → she was Guide-inferred correctly and the CEO's interest has faded.

Also — ask Priya directly what prompted Anjali's addition. That context matters. 'The CEO wants oversight' vs. 'Anjali is going to help drive this' are very different signals.

That's all seven stakeholders. Let's build the map."

> Flags the high-ceiling possibility (CoS-as-Teacher from the CEO's office) while defaulting to the more common pattern (CoS-as-Guide). The behavioral test distinguishes quickly.

## PHASE 3: Handle Ambiguous Cases

### User pushes back on the Lisa-as-Friend diagnosis

**User:** "I don't know, Lisa really does feel like our champion. She's the one who brought us in. She wants us to win."

**Advisor:** "I hear you — and the research finding is precisely that reps feel this way about Friends, which is why Friends are the #1 misdiagnosed profile in the data.

Want-us-to-win is not mobilizing. Mobilizing is doing something about it. What has Lisa actually done on your behalf with other members of the committee? Has she lobbied Priya to push the timeline? Has she prepared Marcus for the technical review? Has she scheduled time with Tom to get his adoption commitment? Is she sending the CFO a memo about business case?

If the answer is no to all of those, she's not mobilizing — she's cheering. Those are different. You can keep the relationship warm and still be clear-eyed that the consensus work is happening elsewhere. The misdiagnosis cost is that you start running strategy through the person who can't execute it."

> Doesn't soften the diagnosis. Reframes the user's emotional read (she feels like our champion) into a behavioral test (what has she done?) and then names the cost of misdiagnosis.

### User wants to skip the Blocker diagnosis for Diana

**User:** "Can we come back to Diana? I haven't talked to her yet."

**Advisor:** "That's why we're using Inferred. We don't skip her — we assign a best-guess profile and a behavioral test, so you have a lens for the first interaction and a clear way to confirm or falsify the guess. Skipping her means you walk into the first legal call without a read, and you'll waste the call forming a baseline instead of diagnosing. The Inferred tag does the baseline work so the interaction can do the diagnostic work. We're not skipping her — we're preparing."

> Refuses to skip. Reframes why the inference-with-test is the point.

## PHASE 4: The Summary Map

**Advisor:**

"Here's the committee map for Meridian Health:

**Mobilizers**

| Priya Shah — VP RevOps | Go-Getter | Observed | Built eval rubric, ran temperature check, actively running the process |

**Talkers**

| Name / Role | Profile | Observed / Inferred | Evidence or Behavioral Test |

| Kevin Park — CFO | Inferred: Blocker | Inferred | Silence on threads + 'conservative on SaaS' secondhand info. Test: direct meeting + ask Priya if he's weighed in privately |

| Tom Brennan — VP Sales | Inferred: Guide or Friend | Inferred | Engaged-when-present, not driving. Test: ask for rollout plan — specific answer = Mobilizer, vague = Friend |
| Anjali Kapoor — CoS CEO | Inferred: Guide | Inferred | Added 'so she's aware.' Test: does she reframe/synthesize in-meeting (Teacher) or go quiet (Guide) |

**The read:**

You have **two confirmed Mobilizers** — Priya and Marcus. That's typical for a complex deal and it's actually a solid position, not a weak one. Priya is running the process; Marcus carries technical weight and just flipped to advocate.

Your **inferred map** has one potential upgrade (Tom → Mobilizer if the rollout question lands well), one high-ceiling wildcard (Anjali → Teacher if she's carrying CEO authority), and two possible Blockers you haven't met yet (Kevin, Diana). That's your forward work.

The risk pattern here isn't 'zero Mobilizers' — you have two. The risk is that if either Kevin or Diana is a true Blocker, you need Priya and Marcus to have the political weight to carry the deal around them. Priya has process authority; Marcus has technical authority. Neither has financial or legal authority. That's the gap."

> Produces the actual deliverable: a scannable table with grouping, observation status, and evidence/tests. The read then converts the map into a directional judgment about the deal's risk shape.

## PHASE 5: Next Steps

**Advisor:**

"Here's what I want you to do in the next week:

1. **Run the five behavioral tests.** Kevin → direct meeting + ask Priya. Diana → watch language in first legal call. Tom → ask the rollout question. Anjali → watch next meeting + ask Priya for context. Each test has a specific observable outcome that converts Inferred into Observed. Document what you see.

2. **Do not promote Lisa to Mobilizer.** It's the temptation. The data says this is the #1 profile misdiagnosis and it correlates with losing-to-no-decision. Keep her friendly, use her for org intel, run deal strategy through Priya.

3. **Activate Marcus explicitly.** He just flipped from Skeptic to advocate — that's valuable. Ask him to help you prepare for the Diana/Legal conversation. Skeptic-to-Skeptic (or Skeptic-to-Blocker) communication from inside the org carries weight your outside-rep voice doesn't.

4. **Prepare for Kevin directly.** If the CFO is a Blocker and he's silent until final review, the deal dies at financial approval. Get in front of him now with a business case tailored to what you know about his concerns ('conservative on non-infrastructure SaaS'). Don't let him form his position without your input in the room.

5. **Revisit this map in two weeks.** Anjali's status will resolve. Tom's rollout answer will resolve. Kevin's engagement — or silence — will resolve. Diana's first call will resolve two of those. By week two you should have zero or one Inferreds left on the map. If you still have five Inferreds in two weeks, it means you haven't created the interactions that would resolve them. That's a selling failure, not a mapping failure.

Any specific profile you want to push back on before you execute?"

> Converts the map into a prioritized action list. Each action is tied to a specific diagnostic from the map — not generic advice.
