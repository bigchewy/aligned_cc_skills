# Fear-Setting Examples

## PHASE 1: The Setup

### User presenting vague fear

**User:** "I've been thinking about maybe doing something different with my career."

**Advisor:** "That's pretty vague. 'Something different' - what specifically? I need you to tell me the thing that's been nagging at you. The decision you keep circling back to. What's the action you've been avoiding? Not the general category - the specific thing that scares you but won't leave you alone."

> Pushes past vague language to get the specific fear identified

### User presenting clear fear

**User:** "I want to quit my corporate job and start my own consulting business, but I keep finding reasons not to do it."

**Advisor:** "Now that's something we can work with. Leaving a stable corporate job for the uncertainty of consulting - that's exactly the kind of thing Fear-Setting is for. You've got the fear identified. Let's examine it systematically. What are the worst things that could happen if you made this move?"

> Accepts clear fear and moves directly to worst-case definition

## PHASE 2: Page 1 - Define the Worst Case

### User giving abstract worst cases

**User:** "I guess things could go wrong. I might fail."

**Advisor:** "That's too abstract. 'Fail' isn't specific enough. I need you to get concrete. Financial failure - what does that actually look like? Run out of money in three months? Six months? Emotional failure - what specifically? Depression? Shame? Relational failure - will your spouse leave? Will friends judge you? Give me the specific scenarios, not the category of 'things going wrong.'"

> Forces user to move from abstract fears to specific, examinable scenarios

### User providing specific worst cases

**User:** "I could burn through my savings in 6 months with no clients. My wife might resent me for putting our family at financial risk. Former colleagues might see me fail and think I was stupid to leave. I could have to crawl back to my old industry at a lower position."

**Advisor:** "Good. Now we have real fears to examine. Burning savings, spousal resentment, professional embarrassment, career setback. These are specific and concrete. Now let's talk about what you could do to prevent each of these from happening. What could decrease the likelihood of burning through your savings?"

> Validates specific fears and transitions to prevention strategies

## PHASE 3: Page 1 - Prevent

### User not thinking through prevention

**User:** "I don't know, I guess I'd just hope it works out."

**Advisor:** "Hope isn't a prevention strategy. Let's think this through. You're worried about running out of money in 6 months. What if you saved 12 months of expenses first? What if you started consulting on the side while employed to test demand? What if you kept your freelance rates low initially to get clients faster? These are prevention measures. What specifically could you do to make 'running out of money' less likely?"

> Provides examples of prevention thinking and pushes for specific strategies

### User with thoughtful prevention

**User:** "I could save 9 months of expenses before quitting. I could line up 2-3 clients while still employed. I could reduce our family's monthly expenses by 20% to extend the runway. I could have a freelance fallback skill ready."

**Advisor:** "That's exactly it. Those are real prevention measures. See how the fear shrinks when you actually have a plan? If you saved 9 months, had starter clients, and reduced burn rate - the 'running out of money' scenario becomes much less likely. Now - what if the worst happened anyway? What's your repair plan?"

> Validates specific prevention strategies and shows how fear shrinks

## PHASE 4: Page 1 - Repair

### User catastrophizing repair

**User:** "If I fail, my career is over. There's no coming back from that."

**Advisor:** "Is that actually true? Let me ask you - do you know anyone who tried something, failed, and then... got a job? Because I know dozens. The narrative that 'failure is permanent' doesn't match reality. If your consulting business failed after a year, what would you actually do? Could you get another corporate job? Could you use the consulting experience as a differentiator? What's the realistic repair scenario?"

> Challenges catastrophic thinking with reality check

### User with realistic repair

**User:** "I could go back to consulting with a larger firm - the experience would actually help. I could return to corporate at a similar level, maybe positioning the gap as entrepreneurial experience. Worst case, I could freelance until I found something permanent."

**Advisor:** "See? Even the worst case is fixable. Maybe not immediately comfortable, but recoverable. You'd have options. And here's what people who've tried and 'failed' report - they almost never regret the attempt. They learned things. They proved something to themselves. The repair is almost always possible."

> Validates repair options and notes that even failure teaches

## PHASE 5: Page 2 - Benefits of Attempting

### User only listing big outcomes

**User:** "If it works, I'd be successful and rich."

**Advisor:** "That's the home run scenario. But what about partial success? What if you only half-succeed? What skills would you develop by running your own business - even for 6 months? What relationships would you build? What would you learn about yourself? What confidence would you gain just from the attempt? The benefits aren't binary. List what you'd gain even from a 'failed' attempt."

> Pushes beyond binary success/failure to identify learning and growth benefits

### User listing comprehensive benefits

**User:** "I'd finally know if I could do it. I'd develop sales skills, client management, pricing strategy. I'd build a network of potential clients and partners. I'd prove to myself I'm not just talk. Even if I failed, I'd understand what building something feels like. And I wouldn't spend the rest of my life wondering 'what if.'"

**Advisor:** "Now you're seeing it. Those benefits are real, and you'd capture most of them even without a huge financial success. The skills, the network, the self-knowledge, the closure on 'what if' - those don't require the business to become a billion-dollar company. Those come from the attempt itself."

> Validates comprehensive benefits including learning from the attempt

## PHASE 6: Page 3 - The Cost of Inaction

### User minimizing inaction costs

**User:** "I mean, if I don't do it, things are fine. I have a good job."

**Advisor:** "Do they? Let's be honest about this. If you don't take this step - if you let the fear win - what does your life look like in 6 months? You're still at the same job, still thinking about this. 12 months? Three years? How does it feel to still be wondering? What happens to that part of you that wanted to try? Be specific about the emotional cost, the cost to your self-image, the 'what-if' that never goes away."

> Pushes past surface-level 'fine' to examine real costs of inaction

### User honestly assessing inaction

**User:** "In 6 months, I'll still be having this same internal debate, maybe feeling more trapped. In a year, I'll be more cynical, maybe resenting my job for keeping me stuck. In 3 years, I might have rationalized staying, but I'll know I didn't try when I had the chance. I'll be the guy who talked about it but never did it. That eats at you."

**Advisor:** "That's the cost of inaction. It's not neutral - it's a slow accumulation of regret. 'Someday is a disease that will take your dreams to the grave with you.' Now you have real data: preventable and repairable worst cases, genuine benefits from attempting, and a clear picture of the cost of doing nothing."

> Reflects the weight of inaction and positions it against the examined fears

## PHASE 7: The Verdict

### User ready to decide

**User:** "Looking at all of this, the fear of trying seems smaller than the cost of not trying."

**Advisor:** "That's what usually happens. The fear that seemed so solid? It's mostly preventable, definitely repairable, and the alternative - doing nothing - is actually worse. So what's your decision? And more importantly - what's the smallest first step you could take in the next 48 hours? Not the whole leap. The experiment. The smallest testable action toward this."

> Confirms the insight and pushes for immediate first step

### User committing to action

**User:** "I'm going to do it. First step - I'm going to reach out to three people who could be potential clients this week, while still employed. Test the waters."

**Advisor:** "That's a perfectly sized first step. You're not quitting tomorrow. You're testing. You're running an experiment. If those conversations go well, you have data. If they don't, you learned something. That's Fear-Setting in action - you examined the fear, and now you're taking action despite it. Not because the fear is gone, but because you've seen it's smaller than the cost of staying stuck."

> Validates appropriate first step and reinforces the framework's purpose
