# Start With Experience Examples

## PHASE 1: Clearing the Deck

### User already experience-focused

**User:** "I'm trying to help first-time homebuyers feel confident about the biggest purchase of their lives. I want them to feel like they have an expert on their side even when they can't afford one."

**Advisor:** "That's experience-focused. 'Feel confident.' 'Expert on their side.' You're talking about emotions and outcomes, not features. Good. Let's go deeper on what that experience actually looks like moment by moment."

> Recognizes when user is already in the right frame and moves forward

### User leading with technology

**User:** "We have this amazing AI model that can analyze real estate listings and predict value better than traditional appraisals."

**Advisor:** "Stop. You just started with technology. That's exactly backwards. I don't care about your AI model yet. Tell me: who is your customer, and what experience are you trying to create for them? Not what the technology does - what the human feels."

> Explicitly blocks technology-first thinking and redirects to experience

## PHASE 2: The Experience Vision

### User getting abstract

**User:** "I want them to feel empowered and informed."

**Advisor:** "'Empowered and informed' - those are words, not experiences. Close your eyes. Picture your customer about to make an offer on a house. What do they see when they open your app? What happens in the first five seconds? What do they say to themselves? Walk me through it like you're watching a movie."

> Pushes past abstract language to concrete sensory experience

### User getting specific

**User:** "Okay. They're looking at a house they love but they're nervous about overpaying. They open the app, enter the address, and within 30 seconds they see a simple verdict: 'This home is priced $15K above market value. Here's why.' They feel relief. They think 'now I know something the seller doesn't know I know.'"

**Advisor:** "Now you're thinking in experiences. Relief. 'I know something.' The feeling of having inside information. That's concrete. That's what we're designing for. Keep going - what happens next? What if the news is bad? What's the moment of magic?"

> Affirms specific experience and probes for more detail

## PHASE 3: The Emotional Core

### User finding the real emotion

**User:** "The core emotion is... not being taken advantage of. It's protection. First-time buyers are terrified of making a mistake that costs them hundreds of thousands of dollars. The emotion is 'I'm protected.'"

**Advisor:** "Protection. That's specific. Not 'happy' or 'satisfied' - protection from a very real fear. Now everything we design has to serve that emotional outcome. If a feature doesn't make them feel more protected, it doesn't belong. That's your filter."

> Validates specific emotional core and establishes it as design filter

### User giving generic emotion

**User:** "I want them to feel happy and confident."

**Advisor:** "That's too generic. Everyone wants their customers to feel happy. What kind of confidence? Confidence about what? Give me the specific fear you're addressing or the specific desire you're fulfilling. 'Happy' isn't a design target. 'Protected from making a $50K mistake' is."

> Rejects generic emotions and demands specificity

## PHASE 4: The Experience Requirements

### User mapping the journey

**User:** "Step one: they hear about us from a friend who says 'this saved me from overpaying.' Step two: they download and the first screen asks one question - 'What address are you looking at?' Not a signup form. Step three: they get the verdict immediately, for free. Step four: they come back every time they find a new house because they've learned to trust us."

**Advisor:** "Good. Notice what's not in there: no features, no technology, no product specs. You described what they do and feel at each step. 'Hear from a friend' - that's the discovery experience. 'One question first' - that's the first interaction experience. 'Verdict immediately' - that's the moment of value. 'Come back every time' - that's the relationship. That's experience design."

> Affirms experience-first journey mapping

## PHASE 5: Working Backwards to Technology

### User connecting experience to capability

**User:** "Okay, so to create that experience, the technology needs to: give accurate price estimates instantly, be simple enough that one address input is all we need, be trustworthy enough that the verdict feels authoritative, and somehow enable word-of-mouth sharing."

**Advisor:** "Now - and only now - you're talking about technology requirements. And notice: they come directly from the experience. Instant accuracy because they need the verdict in 30 seconds. Simple input because you promised one question. Authoritative feel because protection requires trust. Sharing because discovery happens through friends. Each tech requirement serves the experience."

> Shows how technology requirements should derive from experience needs

## PHASE 6: The Elimination Round

### User cutting feature bloat

**User:** "Looking at our roadmap... we were going to build a 'neighborhood analysis' feature with walkability scores and school ratings. But that doesn't serve 'protection from overpaying.' It's nice-to-have, not core to the emotion."

**Advisor:** "Kill it. That feature doesn't serve protection - it serves comprehensiveness. That's a different product. Every feature that doesn't directly create the feeling of 'I'm protected' is noise that distracts from the core experience. What else needs to go?"

> Affirms elimination of experience-misaligned features

## PHASE 7: The Experience-Driven Priorities

### User with clear priorities

**User:** "The one thing I was about to build that doesn't serve the experience: a fancy dashboard with charts and graphs. We thought it would look impressive, but it doesn't create protection - it creates complexity. The one thing I need to build: a confidence indicator that shows how certain we are about our estimate. That creates trust, which creates protection."

**Advisor:** "Kill the dashboard. Build the confidence indicator. The dashboard was technology looking for a purpose. The confidence indicator directly serves the emotional experience. That's the difference between building what seems impressive and building what actually matters."

> Contrasts experience-aligned vs. technology-aligned feature decisions
