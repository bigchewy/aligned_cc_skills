# The Big Idea and Takeaway Title — Examples

One running example, made up to show the moves. Priya runs analytics at a small meal-kit company. Every month she sends the head of operations, Dan, a dashboard with four charts: "Weekly orders," "Cancellations by reason," "Delivery on-time rate," and "Orders by region." Dan reads it, says thanks, and nothing changes. This month the on-time rate in the North region fell from 96% to 81%, and cancellations that give "late delivery" as the reason doubled.

---

## Phase 1: Exploring or Explaining?

### Golden path

**User:** "It's our monthly ops dashboard. Dan clicks around it, but this month I need him to do something about North deliveries."

**Say something like:**
"Then you have two jobs on one page. The dashboard is exploratory: Dan clicks around and looks things up. What you need this month is explanatory: one point, one action. Amy Esselman on our team wrote about exactly this, going from dashboard to story. Keep the dashboard for exploring. Put the North story in its own section at the top, or in a short note that goes with it."

**Why it works:** It names both uses instead of picking one, and it credits the from-dashboard-to-story practice to Amy Esselman.

### Struggle: wants to show the whole analysis

**User:** "I should probably walk him through how I found it. I checked every region, then every carrier, then the warehouse logs."

**Say something like:**
"Resist this urge. You are making your audience reopen all of the oysters! Concentrate on the pearls, the information your audience needs to know. Dan needs to know North is late and it's costing customers. Keep the carrier and warehouse checks ready in case he asks."

**Why it works:** The quote is Cole's own, from chapter 1, and it's used for the exact problem it was written about.

---

## Phase 2: Who and What

### Golden path

**User:** "Dan. He knows the business well but doesn't read charts closely. I want him to approve switching the North carrier."

**Say something like:**
"Good. Who: Dan, knows the business, skims charts. What: approve the carrier switch for North. How: you send it, so the page has to make the point without you in the room. That means the words on the page do the work."

**Why it works:** It repeats who, what and how back in one line each, and draws the consequence for the page: no presenter, so the words carry the point.

### Struggle: no action named

**User:** "I just want him to be aware of the numbers."

**Say something like:**
"If you simply present data, it's easy for your audience to say, 'Oh, that's interesting,' and move on to the next thing. That might be what's happening every month. If Dan read this and did one thing, what would you want it to be?"

**User:** "Honestly? Switch carriers in North."

**Why it works:** It ties the user's own problem (nothing changes) to the missing action, then asks for the action instead of supplying it.

---

## Phase 3: The 3-Minute Story

### Golden path

**User:** "North on-time fell from 96 to 81 percent after we moved to the new carrier in August. Late-delivery cancellations doubled, mostly North. Other regions are fine. The old carrier would take us back at about the same price. If we switch now, we're back on time before the holidays."

**Say something like:**
"That's a story. What changed, why it matters, what to do, and when. Notice you didn't mention weekly orders or orders by region at all."

**Why it works:** It points out which charts the story never needed, which sets up the title phase.

### Struggle: a tour of the charts

**User:** "First there's weekly orders, which are flat. Then cancellations by reason. Then on-time rate…"

**Say something like:**
"That's the order of the page, not the story. Start from what's wrong. Then why Dan should care. Then what you want him to do. The charts come in as proof."

**Why it works:** It gives the three-part order to follow, not just a correction.

---

## Phase 4: The Big Idea in One Sentence

### Golden path

**Say something like:**
"Here's my first try: 'Switching North back to our old carrier before the holidays will stop the late deliveries that doubled our cancellations there.' Point of view: switch. What's at stake: cancellations, and the holidays. One sentence. What would you change?"

**User:** "Say 'this month' instead of 'before the holidays.' Dan likes dates."

**Why it works:** The draft is written from the user's own story and numbers, and it's checked against both tests (point of view, what's at stake) out loud.

### Edge case: the sentence is a topic

**User:** "How about 'North region delivery performance'?"

**Say something like:**
"That's the subject, not the idea. What about North deliveries? They're late. So what for Dan? Customers are leaving. Put both in."

**Why it works:** It turns a label into a sentence by asking the two questions the Big Idea has to answer.

---

## Phase 5: Every Title Says the So What

### Golden path

**Say something like:**
"Too often we underutilize this space with a descriptive title. Here are yours rewritten:

| Chart | Current title | Takeaway title |
|---|---|---|
| On-time rate | Delivery on-time rate | North on-time fell from 96% to 81% after the August carrier change |
| Cancellations | Cancellations by reason | Late-delivery cancellations doubled, almost all in North |
| Weekly orders | Weekly orders | Orders held steady, so the drop isn't demand |
| Orders by region | Orders by region | (doesn't support the Big Idea: move to the exploring section) |

Which ones say more than the data shows?"

**Why it works:** Every title is a sentence with a verb and a number, each supports the Big Idea, and the chart that doesn't is named and moved instead of given a forced title.

### Edge case: a page people check every day

**User:** "The dashboard itself updates daily. A title saying 'fell to 81%' will be wrong next week."

**Say something like:**
"For a page people check every day, name the question and today's answer. 'Is North on time? Not yet: 81% this week, goal 95%.' The point still goes in the title. The note you send Dan this month can use the fixed sentence."

**Why it works:** It keeps the takeaway in the title for an exploratory page without pretending the takeaway never changes.

### Struggle: the deck goes out without her

**User:** "Dan forwards this to the CEO sometimes."

**Say something like:**
"Then add a summary at the top, the way Alex Velez teaches it on our team. The Big Idea first, then the takeaway titles as plain sentences. The CEO might read only that."

**Why it works:** It credits the executive-summary practice to Alex Velez and ties it to a real reader who will skim.

---

## Phase 6: The Big Idea and the Titles

### Golden path

**Say something like:**
"Here's what we have.

**Explaining, for:** Dan, **so that he:** approves switching the North carrier this month.

**Big Idea:** Switching North back to our old carrier this month will stop the late deliveries that doubled our cancellations there.

| Chart | Was | Now |
|---|---|---|
| On-time rate | Delivery on-time rate | North on-time fell from 96% to 81% after the August carrier change |
| Cancellations | Cancellations by reason | Late-delivery cancellations doubled, almost all in North |
| Weekly orders | Weekly orders | Orders held steady, so the drop isn't demand |

Orders by region moves to the exploring part of the dashboard.

Read just the titles top to bottom. They tell the story without the charts. Next, the on-time chart shows four regions in four colors. Run `declutter-and-focus` to make North the one accent color and gray out the rest."

**Why it works:** The result is the Big Idea plus one new title per chart, and it names the next framework for the one chart that still doesn't point at its title.
