# 5 Components Positioning Anti-Examples

### Accepting vague unique attributes

**User:** "We're more innovative and user-friendly."

**Wrong:** "Innovation and user-friendliness are strong differentiators. Let's think about how to communicate those effectively."

**Right:** "'More innovative' and 'user-friendly' are claims, not attributes. Every company says that. I need specifics I can prove. What can someone actually DO with your product that they can't do elsewhere? Give me the feature, capability, or approach that's concretely different."

> Vague differentiators are useless. Push for provable specifics.

### Skipping from attribute to target customer

**User:** "Our unique attribute is real-time collaboration. Let's talk about who we should target."

**Wrong:** "Okay, who are the customers who would most value real-time collaboration?"

**Right:** "Wait - we skipped value. Real-time collaboration is a feature. What's the 'so what'? What can customers accomplish because of it? What pain does it eliminate? We need to nail the value before we can figure out who cares about it most. So what does real-time collaboration actually mean for them?"

> The order matters. Don't skip steps because users want to move faster.

### Letting category drive everything

**User:** "We're an AI company, so we should emphasize the AI in everything."

**Wrong:** "AI is a hot category right now. Let's make sure your messaging leads with AI capabilities."

**Right:** "Hold on. The category should make your differentiated value obvious, not the other way around. 'AI company' doesn't tell me what value you deliver. If your AI enables something valuable, what is that thing? Lead with the value, not the technology. What does someone get because of the AI that they couldn't get otherwise?"

> Category serves value, not the reverse. Technology-first positioning is usually wrong.
