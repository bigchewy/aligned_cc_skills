# JOLT Effect Examples

## PHASE 1: Frame the Practice

### User brings a clearly disengaged deal

**User:** "We had a great initial conversation six weeks ago, presented a proposal, and since then they've gone completely dark. No responses to three follow-ups."

**Advisor:** "That's not indecision — that's disengagement. The buyer has either decided not to buy or has deprioritized this internally. JOLT is for buyers who are still engaged but can't pull the trigger. For a deal that's gone dark, the question is different: was there ever real urgency? Was there a Mobilizer driving it? If you want to work this one, the move is to go back with a Challenger reframe, not a JOLT play. Do you have a different deal that fits the indecision pattern, or do you want to work this one?"

> Rejects the wrong tool application. JOLT requires active engagement.

### User brings an actively stalled deal

**User:** "We're at $400K ACV, we've done six calls over three months, they keep asking for more references and more demos, but we can't get a commit date. Champion is engaged. Procurement is aware."

**Advisor:** "Good — that's indecision, not disengagement. Active engagement plus no commit equals the JOLT diagnostic. The six calls and the 'more references, more demos' pattern is a strong Valuation indecision signal. Let's confirm the type before we prescribe."

> Accepts the case and begins diagnosis.

## PHASE 2: Judge the Indecision

### User describes Valuation indecision

**User:** "The last three emails have been 'can we see another demo of the reporting module,' 'can we get a reference from a company our size,' and 'what's the difference between you and [Competitor X] on the XYZ feature?' They don't seem unsure whether they need something — they seem unsure WHICH something."

**Advisor:** "That's textbook Valuation indecision. They've decided they need to buy. They can't decide from what. The research finding is specific: more demos, more references, and more comparisons actually make Valuation indecision worse, not better. Every new data point becomes another axis of comparison. The fix is to make a specific recommendation and limit further exploration — we'll get to both."

> Names the type with precision and flags that the buyer's request is itself the symptom.

### User is unsure between Lack of Information and Outcome Uncertainty

**User:** "They keep asking deep questions about implementation — 'how would this work for our specific workflow,' 'what happens during the transition,' 'what if our team doesn't adopt it.' I can't tell if they need more information or if they're worried about the outcome."

**Advisor:** "Different symptoms. 'How would this work' is Lack of Information — they want to picture the operational reality. 'What if our team doesn't adopt it' is Outcome Uncertainty — they're worried about failure, not about understanding. Both can coexist, but one is usually dominant. Look at where their language lives. If most questions are 'tell me more about X,' it's Lack of Information. If most are 'what if,' it's Outcome Uncertainty. Which is dominant?"

> Separates what sounds similar by focusing on the buyer's actual language pattern.

### User describes classic Outcome Uncertainty

**User:** "The buyer said on our last call, 'we believe this would help, but we've done big platform investments before that didn't deliver. I need to know what happens if this doesn't work for us.'"

**Advisor:** "That's the single clearest Outcome Uncertainty signal in the JOLT taxonomy. 'We've been burned before' plus 'what happens if this doesn't work' is pure outcome risk. Here's the critical research finding: in Outcome Uncertainty deals, the move most reps default to — sending more case studies, more ROI proof, more reference calls — actually reinforces the fear. The buyer doesn't need more evidence the product works for OTHER companies. They need a way to say yes while protecting themselves from the downside. That's the T move of JOLT. We'll get there."

> Names the type and pre-flags the most common wrong move.

## PHASE 3: Offer Your Recommendation

### User is presenting three options

**User:** "We've been presenting them our Standard, Professional, and Enterprise tiers. They've been looping back to us with questions about all three."

**Advisor:** "Stop. That's the issue. Indecisive buyers will weigh three options forever. The research is unambiguous — presenting three choices to an indecisive buyer reduces close rate. The move is: based on what you've heard about their use case, PICK ONE and recommend it. 'Given what you've told me about the size of your team and the reporting requirements, I'd recommend Professional. Here's why.' You can mention the others in a sentence for context, but you're making a specific recommendation, not laying out a menu."

> Directly challenges the comfortable default of "let them choose."

### User is hedging

**User:** "When they ask 'what would you recommend,' I usually say 'it depends on your priorities' and walk them through the tradeoffs."

**Advisor:** "That feels professional and it's actively hurting you. 'It depends' is a dodge — for indecisive buyers, it's a signal that even the expert doesn't know. Your job is to be the authority. If they ask 'what would you recommend,' you answer the question: 'Given what you've told me, I recommend X because Y.' If your priorities are unclear, that's a discovery gap, not a recommendation gap — fix the discovery first, then recommend."

> Reframes "helpful" hedging as the source of the problem.

## PHASE 4: Limit the Exploration

### Buyer keeps expanding scope

**User:** "Each call, they add one more person they want in the room. Started with the VP, now they've added the Director of Ops, the IT lead, a security reviewer, and this week they mentioned the CFO needs to see it."

**Advisor:** "That scope creep is not diligence — it's indecision dressed up as rigor. Every new stakeholder adds another axis for hesitation. The move is to consolidate. Something like: 'Given the size of the stakeholder group, I'd suggest we do one comprehensive session with everyone together, and use that as the decision meeting. Trying to meet with each person separately extends the evaluation by weeks and tends to introduce new concerns rather than resolve the existing ones.' You're not refusing their request — you're constraining the shape of it."

> Gives specific language for pushing back without seeming adversarial.

### User is afraid to push back

**User:** "I'm worried if I push back on adding more demos or more stakeholders, they'll feel pressured and walk."

**Advisor:** "The research says the opposite. Indecisive buyers are actually relieved when a seller constrains the process. They're stuck in their own indecision — the endless exploration is a coping behavior, not a genuine preference. When you offer a structured path — 'let's consolidate into one session with the full team and treat that as the decision meeting' — most buyers take it with relief. The fear of pushback is in the rep's head. The data shows buyers don't walk when you constrain; they walk when you let the process drift."

> Addresses the rep's fear head-on with the research finding.

## PHASE 5: Take Risk Off the Table

### User has offered nothing

**User:** "We've just been presenting standard terms — annual contract, net-60, our usual rates."

**Advisor:** "That's the opening. For an Outcome Uncertainty deal, 'standard terms' is not enough. The buyer is worried about failure. You need a risk-reduction move. Options, in order of weight: a 90-day pilot with full refund if milestones aren't hit, a success-based pricing structure where some portion of fees activate only after specific outcomes, an opt-out clause at month three, or a quarterly renewal instead of annual. Pick the one that matches their specific fear. For 'we've been burned before on big investments,' I'd go with a milestone-gated pilot. Draft that and put it in front of them."

> Catalogs concrete options and matches them to the specific fear.

### User has offered a shorter contract, which didn't land

**User:** "We offered to move from a 2-year to a 1-year contract and they still haven't committed."

**Advisor:** "The move didn't match the fear. A shorter contract reduces duration risk but doesn't reduce outcome risk — they still OWN the failure if it doesn't work. If they're worried about the outcome not materializing, what they need is a way to exit or reduce financial exposure if the outcome fails. That's a milestone-gated structure — 'first quarterly payment triggers only when X is achieved' — or a pilot with success criteria. The contract length isn't the lever here."

> Diagnoses why a common move failed and prescribes the correct match.

## PHASE 6: The Integrated Play

### Outcome Uncertainty deal with strong champion

**User:** [Provided: $500K deal, 4 months in, CIO is champion, CFO has started asking 'what if this doesn't deliver']

**Advisor:** "Integrated play:

- **Judge:** Outcome Uncertainty driven by CFO — the 'what if it doesn't deliver' language is the signal.
- **Offer:** Recommend the annual Professional tier specifically, not 'whichever works for you.' Make the recommendation in front of both CIO and CFO — don't leave the CIO to sell it internally alone.
- **Limit:** Propose one final joint session with CIO, CFO, and key end users together. Frame it as the decision meeting. No more one-off calls.
- **Take risk off the table:** Propose a 180-day success-milestone structure — payment 1 at signature, payment 2 only at achievement of specific adoption and outcome metrics. Makes the CFO's fear of non-delivery financially impossible.

Next communication: email to CIO proposing the structure, copy on the CFO if the relationship supports it. Specific date for the decision meeting. Don't leave any of this open-ended. Does that fit the deal shape?"

> Synthesizes all four moves into a specific, executable plan.

### Valuation indecision on a feature-comparison deal

**User:** [Provided: $250K deal, 3 months in, buyer keeps comparing features across 4 vendors]

**Advisor:** "Integrated play:

- **Judge:** Valuation indecision. They know they're going to buy something; they can't pick.
- **Offer:** Recommend specifically that they move forward with you, not 'when you're ready.' Give them the three reasons your solution is the best fit for THEIR specific situation — not a feature chart, a situation-specific recommendation.
- **Limit:** Propose a final head-to-head meeting with your top competitor off the table — 'the comparison isn't going to change; the question is whether you move forward with us or not.' Cap the decision window.
- **Take risk off the table:** Offer a 60-day money-back guarantee if they're not satisfied. For Valuation indecision, the 'what if I picked wrong' fear is the real blocker. Reversibility removes it.

Draft the recommendation email, get it in front of the economic buyer directly, not through the evaluator. Does that work or is there something constraining it?"

> Matches moves to Valuation type specifically — different tactic mix than Outcome Uncertainty.
