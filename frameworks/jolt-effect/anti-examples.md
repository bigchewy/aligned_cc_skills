# JOLT Effect Anti-Examples

### Applying JOLT to a status-quo preference deal

**User:** "The buyer keeps saying 'we're happy with how we do this today' and 'change is hard for our organization.'"

**Wrong:** "Let's run the JOLT play. Which type of indecision do you think this is — Valuation, Lack of Information, or Outcome Uncertainty?"

**Right:** "Stop. That's not indecision. That's a status-quo preference. The buyer isn't stuck between options — they've decided they don't want to change. JOLT is the wrong tool. For a status-quo problem, you need the Challenger teach — reframing the cost of inaction, showing them what the business is losing by standing pat. JOLT assumes the buyer wants to move. This one doesn't."

> JOLT is calibrated for active indecision. Don't force it onto a fundamentally different problem.

### Treating "more case studies" as the fix for Outcome Uncertainty

**User:** "The buyer is worried the platform won't deliver. I'm going to send them three more case studies from similar-sized companies."

**Wrong:** "Good plan — social proof is the move for Outcome Uncertainty."

**Right:** "Stop. That's the most common wrong move, and the research is explicit that it reinforces the fear rather than reducing it. Outcome Uncertainty buyers are not skeptical that the product works for others. They're worried it won't work for THEM, and they'll be accountable. More proof of 'it worked elsewhere' confirms the success is possible but doesn't give them personal protection. The correct move is risk reduction — a milestone-gated pilot, a success-based fee structure, an opt-out at 90 days. Give them a way to say yes without owning the full downside. Don't send more case studies."

> Outcome Uncertainty is the most mis-prescribed type. Evidence is not the fix — reversibility is.

### Presenting "three great options" to an indecisive buyer

**User:** "They asked what we'd recommend. I gave them our Standard, Professional, and Enterprise packages and laid out the tradeoffs so they could pick."

**Wrong:** "Good — walking them through the tradeoffs respects their autonomy."

**Right:** "That's what feels professional and it's exactly wrong for an indecisive buyer. The research finding is consistent: presenting three options to an indecisive buyer reduces close rate by a significant margin versus recommending ONE. Choice overload is an active driver of indecision. The move is to PICK ONE based on what they've told you, recommend it specifically, and briefly mention the others for context. 'Given what you've told me about your team size and reporting needs, I recommend Professional because X. Standard would leave you short on Y, and Enterprise has capacity you won't use for 18 months.' Specific recommendation, not a menu."

> The "helpful" move is the destructive move. Authority requires a recommendation.

### Agreeing to every scope expansion the buyer requests

**User:** "They want to add three more stakeholders to the evaluation — their head of security, their head of compliance, and their COO. I've agreed to set up separate calls with each."

**Wrong:** "Makes sense, get those calls on the calendar and keep the process moving."

**Right:** "Stop. Three new stakeholders at the decision stage is not diligence — it's indecision expanding the surface area. The research shows every new stakeholder added late in the cycle adds weeks to the timeline and often introduces NEW concerns rather than resolving existing ones. The move is to consolidate, not to add: 'Given the size of the group now involved, I'd suggest we do one comprehensive session with all stakeholders together and treat it as the decision meeting, rather than serialized calls that will extend the timeline by a month.' You're not refusing — you're constraining. The buyer will usually accept because it's actually more respectful of their time."

> Indecisive buyers expand scope as coping. The rep's job is to push back, not comply.

### Offering a shorter contract to a buyer worried about the outcome

**User:** "They're nervous about whether the platform will deliver ROI. I'm going to offer to move from a 3-year to a 1-year contract."

**Wrong:** "Good — a shorter commitment reduces their risk."

**Right:** "That move doesn't match the fear. Outcome Uncertainty is 'what if it doesn't work.' A shorter contract reduces duration but the buyer still owns the full failure if it doesn't deliver — and at the 1-year mark, they're left with 'did we get any value out of this.' What they actually need is a way to tie payment or commitment to outcomes. A milestone-gated pilot where payment 2 activates only when specific adoption or outcome metrics are hit. Or a 90-day refundable trial period with success criteria. Or a success fee structure. Match the move to the actual fear — for Outcome Uncertainty, reversibility of outcome, not duration of commitment, is the lever."

> Risk-reduction is not a single move. The specific move must match the specific fear.

### Confusing a Friend with a Mobilizer as part of JOLT work

**User:** "Our champion has been very responsive. They always take our calls, they've been friendly, and they clearly like our product. But the deal keeps stalling."

**Wrong:** "Let's work the JOLT play with your champion — have them carry the recommendation internally."

**Right:** "Hold on. Describe the champion. Do they have internal political capital, can they put pressure on the CFO or other stakeholders, have they mobilized others to advocate for this — or are they accessible and friendly but not actually moving the deal? Because if it's the latter, that's not a champion. That's a Friend, in Challenger Customer terms. Friends feel great — they always respond, they like the product, they go to bat in meetings that don't matter — but they don't drive consensus. Running JOLT through a Friend doesn't move the deal. You need a Mobilizer: a Go-Getter, a Teacher, or a Skeptic with internal weight. Who on their side fits that description?"

> JOLT tactics are only as good as the internal advocate who carries them. Diagnose the stakeholder first.

### Running JOLT on a simple transactional deal

**User:** "We have a $5K/year SaaS subscription deal, small business buyer, and they've been kicking the tires for three weeks."

**Wrong:** "Let's diagnose which type of indecision is at play and apply the JOLT play."

**Right:** "JOLT is calibrated for complex B2B enterprise deals — multiple stakeholders, high perceived risk, long cycles. A $5K SMB subscription doesn't fit that shape, and forcing the framework onto it is going to feel like overkill to the buyer. Small transactional deals usually stall for different reasons — weak qualification, unclear pain, budget timing, or the prospect just wasn't ready. Walk me through the original qualification and the urgency driver. The fix here is probably upstream of JOLT."

> The JOLT research base is enterprise. Don't force it onto deal shapes it wasn't designed for.

### Accepting "we can't change the contract" as a reason not to offer risk reduction

**User:** "I'd love to offer a milestone-gated pilot, but our legal team doesn't allow non-standard terms."

**Wrong:** "Understandable — let's work on a different angle, maybe more references or another demo."

**Right:** "Have you actually asked legal, or is this a 'we usually don't' rather than 'we literally can't'? In my experience, 'non-standard terms are not allowed' is almost always 'we haven't bothered to get them approved.' A $500K deal at risk of walking is worth an escalation to the CRO and a 48-hour exception review. If the answer is genuinely no — rare — then the T move becomes non-financial: a dedicated implementation manager with success accountability, an executive sponsor who stays in the deal through first outcome, a guaranteed QBR cadence. Something that reduces the buyer's felt risk even if the contract can't change. Don't skip the T move — find an alternate form."

> "We can't" is usually "we haven't asked." And if it's real, find a non-financial risk-reduction move.
