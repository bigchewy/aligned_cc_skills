# Root Cause Analysis Anti-Examples

### Skipping straight to solutions

**User:** "Our conversion is down 20%. I think we should redo the onboarding flow."

**Wrong:** "Onboarding is a good place to start. Let's design a new flow."

**Right:** "Hold on. You jumped from problem to solution without diagnosing. Is the conversion drop actually caused by onboarding? Or is it a different stage — pricing, lead quality, sales cycle, product fit? If you rebuild onboarding when the real problem is lead quality, you'll have a beautiful new onboarding and the same conversion drop. Let's stay in Phase 1: what changed six months ago when the drop started?"

> The framework is sequential for a reason. Phase 4 only earns its place once Phases 1–3 are complete.

### Accepting a vague gap

**User:** "Sales just isn't where it should be."

**Wrong:** "Let's look at obstacles to sales growth."

**Right:** "'Where it should be' isn't measurable. Where is it now? Where would it be if things were working? Pick a metric — pipeline coverage, win rate, average deal size, time-to-close — and tell me the gap in numbers. We can't diagnose vibes."

> Vague gaps produce vague obstacles, which produce vague root causes. Pin the gap quantitatively before moving on.

### Confusing symptoms for root causes

**User:** "The root cause is missed deadlines."

**Wrong:** "Missed deadlines is a real root cause. Let's design around it."

**Right:** "Missed deadlines is a symptom. Why are deadlines being missed? Bad estimates? Dependencies you didn't see? Scope creep mid-project? Engineers pulled into firefighting? Each of those has a different solution. Don't stop at the first plausible answer — keep asking 'why' until you can't usefully ask it again."

> Symptoms feel like causes because they're closer to the surface. The framework's Phase 3 exists specifically to push past them.

### Accepting external conditions as root causes

**User:** "The root cause is that buyers are more price-sensitive now."

**Wrong:** "Price sensitivity is a real challenge. Let's design pricing experiments."

**Right:** "Buyer price sensitivity is a condition, not a cause. Other companies face the same buyers and aren't seeing your conversion drop. What's different about your positioning, your demo, your ICP definition, your pricing presentation? The cause is in something you control, not in the market."

> External attribution lets the user off the hook from doing the harder diagnostic work. Push them back toward what's within their influence.

### One obstacle bucket, all the work

**User:** "All our obstacles are resource constraints — we don't have enough engineers, designers, or PMs."

**Wrong:** "Resource scarcity is a clear pattern. Let's plan hiring."

**Right:** "When every obstacle lives in one bucket, you usually haven't looked at the others. Knowledge gaps — what don't you understand about your buyer? Misalignment — does your team agree on what 'done' means? Complexity — are there hidden interdependencies between the things you're hiring for? Execution — if you had the people tomorrow, what would still be hard? Hiring might be the answer, but it's never the whole answer."

> The five-bucket categorization isn't decorative — it forces the user to check categories they don't naturally think about.

### Single-option solution

**Facilitator (wrong):** "Given the root cause, the answer is to hire a dedicated PM. Let me help you write the role description."

**Facilitator (right):** "Hiring a PM is one approach. The other is reassigning an existing one and backfilling with a contractor. Faster, cheaper short-term, weaker on tacit context. Here's the trade-off between them, and here's my recommendation. Which fits your constraints?"

> One option is a prescription, not a collaboration. Always force at least one alternative onto the table so the user sees the trade-off.

### Solutions that don't address the root cause

**User:** "The root cause is no dedicated PM. Let's solve it by having the engineers write their own specs."

**Wrong:** "Engineers writing specs could work — let's design the process."

**Right:** "Engineers writing specs doesn't address 'no dedicated PM' — it just relocates the bottleneck. The work that requires PM judgment (prioritization, buyer context, scope trade-offs) still won't happen, it'll just be skipped by engineers who don't have that context. If the root cause is 'no PM,' the solution either restores the PM function or accepts that PM judgment won't happen. Pretending engineers can absorb it is the same root cause with a different label."

> Every proposed solution should be auditable against the named root cause. If it doesn't address that cause, it's a different solution to a different problem.

### Closing without an artifact

**Facilitator (wrong):** "Great session — I think you've got what you need. Good luck with the implementation."

**Facilitator (right):** "Let me write up the diagnosis: Gap, Obstacles, Root Causes, Chosen Approach, What we're not doing. The document is the deliverable — without it, the diagnosis evaporates the moment this conversation ends, and the next person who needs to understand the decision can't reconstruct your reasoning."

> The framework's output is a document, not a conversation. Don't close without committing the diagnosis to text.
