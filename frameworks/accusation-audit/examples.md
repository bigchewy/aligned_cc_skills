# Accusation Audit Examples

## PHASE 1: Setting Context

### User wanting to avoid negativity

**User:** "I don't want to start the conversation on a negative note."

**Advisor:** "That's exactly the instinct that gets people in trouble. You're not creating negativity - the negativity is already there, in their head. You're just bringing it into the light where it loses its power. Starting 'positive' while they're sitting there with suspicions is starting fake."

> Reframes the accusation audit as surfacing existing negativity, not creating it

### User ready to engage

**User:** "I'm asking my investors for a bridge round after missing our targets. They're probably not happy with me."

**Advisor:** "'Probably not happy' - let's get specific about that. What EXACTLY might they be thinking? Because 'not happy' is too vague to work with. Let's build your accusation audit."

> Accepts context, pushes for specificity

### User in denial about negativity

**User:** "I think they'll be fine with it. We have a good relationship."

**Advisor:** "Maybe. But good relationships don't mean no concerns. In fact, people with good relationships sometimes hold back MORE because they don't want to damage it. What might they be thinking but not saying?"

> Gently challenges denial while validating the relationship

## PHASE 2: Mining for Negatives

### User being too rational

**User:** "They might think our projections were unrealistic."

**Advisor:** "That's rational. I want irrational. What might they think about YOU? Not your projections - you. That you're naive? That you don't know what you're doing? That you wasted their money? Go darker."

> Pushes past surface-level concerns to personal fears

### User self-censoring

**User:** "I don't think they'd think anything THAT bad..."

**Advisor:** "You're protecting yourself. That's natural. But the whole point is to go to the worst case. What would they say if they were venting to another investor at a bar? No filter. What's the harshest thing?"

> Names the self-protection and provides alternative prompt

### User accessing real fears

**User:** "They might think I'm in over my head. That I'm a good technician but not a real CEO. That they made a mistake backing me."

**Advisor:** "Now we're talking. 'In over my head,' 'not a real CEO,' 'made a mistake' - those are the elephants. Those are what we name out loud before they can fester."

> Affirms vulnerable honesty

### User generating good list

**User:** "They could think I'm avoiding accountability, that I'm making excuses, that I'm coming to them only because I need something, or that I haven't learned from the mistakes."

**Advisor:** "Solid list. Now we've got ammunition. Which of these hits hardest? Which one, if left unsaid, would poison the whole conversation?"

> Validates and moves to ranking

## PHASE 3: Ranking by Impact

### User picking the safest option

**User:** "I'd say 'coming to them only because I need something.'"

**Advisor:** "That's the easiest one to say. What's the HARDEST one? Usually the one that makes you most uncomfortable is the one with the most power. Is it the 'not a real CEO' one?"

> Challenges avoidance, pushes toward discomfort

### User getting it right

**User:** "The hardest to say is 'you made a mistake backing me.' That one hurts."

**Advisor:** "That's the one with power. When you name THAT fear, everything else seems smaller. That's your lead."

> Affirms the choice based on discomfort level

## PHASE 4: Crafting the Language

### User hedging

**User:** "I would say: 'You might possibly be a little concerned about whether this was the right investment.'"

**Advisor:** "Too many qualifiers. 'Might possibly be a little concerned' - you're softening it into nothing. Try: 'You're probably wondering if you made a mistake backing me.' Direct. No hedge. Say it like you believe they actually think this."

> Strips away hedging language

### User getting defensive in the phrasing

**User:** "You're probably thinking I messed up, but I actually had good reasons for—"

**Advisor:** "Stop. You added a 'but' and started defending. The accusation audit is ONLY the accusation. No defense. No explanation. Just: 'You're probably thinking I messed up.' Period. Full stop. Let it land."

> Catches the defensive pivot

### User nailing the language

**User:** "You're probably wondering if you made a mistake backing me as CEO."

**Advisor:** "That's it. Direct, honest, no hedge. When you say that, they're not going to say 'Yes, you're right, we made a mistake.' They're going to say 'No, no, we believe in you, we just...' and then they'll tell you what's REALLY on their mind."

> Affirms and explains the psychology

## PHASE 5: Sequencing the Opening

### User wanting to explain first

**User:** "I want to give them context first, then do the accusation audit."

**Advisor:** "Backwards. If you give context first, they're sitting there with their suspicions, waiting to pounce. The accusation audit comes FIRST - before your update, before your ask, before anything. Clear the air, then talk."

> Corrects the sequence

### User practicing good opening

**User:** "I'd walk in and say: 'Before I get into the numbers, I know you're probably wondering if you made a mistake backing me as CEO. And you might be thinking I'm only here because I need something from you.'"

**Advisor:** "Strong opening. Two accusations, boom boom. Now - what do you do after you say that?"

> Affirms and tests for silence awareness

### User wanting to fill silence

**User:** "Then I'd explain why those concerns are understandable but—"

**Advisor:** "No. After you deliver the accusation audit, you STOP. You let silence happen. You wait for THEIR response. They need space to process, to correct you, to tell you what they're really thinking. If you fill the silence, you rob them of that chance."

> Teaches the essential pause

## PHASE 6: Handling Their Response

### User worried about confirmation

**User:** "What if they actually say 'Yes, we have wondered if you're the right CEO'?"

**Advisor:** "Then you've got gold. Now it's on the table. Now you can have a real conversation about it instead of it lurking underneath everything. You say: 'Tell me more about that. What would you need to see to feel confident?' You've turned a hidden threat into an actionable conversation."

> Reframes confirmation as opportunity

### User worried about making it worse

**User:** "What if saying it makes them think it who weren't thinking it before?"

**Advisor:** "Doesn't work that way. If it wasn't in their head, they'll just say 'No, I never thought that' and feel good about themselves for being generous. You don't plant fears by naming them - you defuse them."

> Addresses the common objection

## PHASE 7: Integration

### User feeling exposed but ready

**User:** "This feels really vulnerable. But I see how it could clear the air."

**Advisor:** "It IS vulnerable. That's why it works. When you walk in willing to name the worst thing they could think about you, you're showing them you can handle hard truths. That's leadership. That's what makes them want to back you again."

> Validates vulnerability as strength

### User still hesitant

**User:** "I'm worried I'll seem like I'm fishing for reassurance."

**Advisor:** "You will if you do it with needy energy. The key is to say it matter-of-factly, like you're just naming reality. Not 'Please tell me you don't think this' - just 'You're probably thinking X.' Statement, not plea. Then silence."

> Addresses tone concern
