# 6 Elements of Sales Development Examples

## PHASE 1: Frame the Diagnostic

### User gives a clear trigger and org shape

**User:** "We're at $8M ARR, we have 7 AEs and 4 SDRs reporting to one SDR manager who was our top SDR last year. Our trigger is pipeline — we're at 60% of coverage heading into Q3 and the CRO wants to hire three more SDRs."

**Advisor:** "Good — that's a specific shape. Seven AEs, four SDRs, one manager who was recently your top rep, eight million ARR, 60% coverage, and pressure to hire more. My initial hypothesis before we walk the six: the weakest elements are probably Leadership (a freshly promoted top rep running four reps with no coaching framework) and Specialization (four SDRs at eight million ARR is a shared pool — that split needs to happen soon). Your CRO's instinct to hire three more is understandable and probably wrong. Let's test the hypothesis by walking all six."

> Establishes operator-level specificity, forms an evidence-based hypothesis, and signals the "hire more SDRs" instinct will be challenged.

### User gives a vague trigger

**User:** "We just want to make the SDR function better."

**Advisor:** "Too vague. Give me one of these: pipeline is short, reps are churning, or we're about to scale. I need the actual pain to know which elements to weight. Which is it?"

> Insists on a specific trigger. The elements don't reveal themselves without a pain point to diagnose against.

---

## PHASE 2: Element 1 — Strategy

### User can't articulate the strategy clearly

**User:** "Our SDRs do a mix of outbound and inbound. They cover everything — cold accounts, inbound leads, and they also work with marketing on some ABM campaigns."

**Advisor:** "Stop there. 'Cover everything' is not a strategy — that's an accidental role. If I asked each of your four SDRs to describe their job in one sentence, I'd get four different answers. And if I asked your marketing team what SDRs do with their leads, it'd be a fifth answer. That's a Strategy gap, and it's going to cascade into at least Specialization and Execution. Note it as weak. The fix — which we'll get to — is to make an explicit choice: are they outbound-led, inbound-response, or account-based. You can run multiple motions, but each SDR needs ONE job."

> Calls out ambiguity as the gap and flags the cascade into other elements.

### User has a clear, shared strategy

**User:** "We're outbound-led. SDRs work named accounts off an ICP target list. Marketing generates some inbound which goes to AEs directly, not through SDRs."

**Advisor:** "That's a clean strategy. Everyone on the team would say the same sentence back to you?"

**User:** "Yes, and it's on every SDR's scorecard."

**Advisor:** "Good. Strategy is solid. Noted as strong. Move on to Specialization."

> Accepts clarity, probes for team alignment, and doesn't belabor strength.

---

## PHASE 3: Element 2 — Specialization

### User still has AEs prospecting at $10M ARR with 9 AEs

**User:** "We have nine AEs and each of them is expected to self-source 30% of their pipeline. We have two SDRs but they mostly support the most senior AEs."

**Advisor:** "That's broken. Nine AEs self-prospecting at ten million ARR means your AE team is probably underperforming on closing — because any hour an AE spends prospecting is an hour they're not working their top deals. And 'two SDRs supporting the most senior AEs' means the junior AEs get nothing, which compounds the problem. The standard split at your stage is: AEs don't prospect. You have a full shared SDR pool with at least one SDR per AE or close to it. Note this — Specialization is weak, and it's probably a bigger revenue lever than hiring more SDRs."

> Names the specific broken pattern and contextualizes the revenue impact.

### User has a premature outbound/inbound split

**User:** "We have three outbound BDRs and two inbound-response SDRs, with separate managers. We're at $4M ARR."

**Advisor:** "That's early. The outbound/inbound split usually happens around ten million ARR. At four million you probably don't have enough inbound volume to justify dedicated inbound-response, and you're paying for two managers when one would do. Note as weak — the fix is probably to re-merge the pool and promote one of the managers, or keep both and broaden scope. We'll come back to the specifics."

> Calibrates to the stage-appropriate model and names the cost of prematurity.

---

## PHASE 4: Element 3 — Recruiting

### User doesn't have an explicit profile

**User:** "We hire people with a year or two of SDR experience at other SaaS companies. It's pretty standard."

**Advisor:** "That's not a profile, that's a filter. And it's the wrong filter. Research on SDR success shows the predictors are coachability, work ethic, and curiosity — not 'prior SaaS SDR experience.' When you filter for 'prior SaaS SDR,' you fish in a pond where half the candidates are leaving their last role because it wasn't working, and you pay more for them. Note this — Recruiting is weak. The fix is a rewritten profile focused on traits, not pedigree, and a structured interview that tests for those traits."

> Distinguishes a filter from a profile and references the research base.

### User has a strong hit rate

**User:** "We've hired eight SDRs in the last year. Seven are still here and performing above quota, one washed out at month three."

**Advisor:** "That's a strong ratio — 87% hit rate, which is well above the benchmark of around 60%. Your profile and process are working. Note Recruiting as strong, move on."

> Uses benchmarks to calibrate what "strong" means and doesn't over-coach a working system.

---

## PHASE 5: Element 4 — Retention

### User's retention math doesn't work

**User:** "Ramp to full quota is about six months for us. Average tenure — let me think — probably eleven or twelve months."

**Advisor:** "That's a treadmill. Six months ramp, eleven to twelve months tenure means you get five to six productive months per hire. Subtract the fully-loaded cost of the ramp period and you're barely net-positive on the investment. Every three months a rep leaves, and the new hire is back at month one. The function never compounds. This is a serious weak spot — note it. The fix is a combination of Execution (shorten ramp) and Leadership and comp (extend tenure). We'll prioritize."

> Walks the math out loud and frames it as treadmill vs. compounding function.

### User has strong retention

**User:** "Ramp is four months and average tenure is eighteen months before promotion or departure."

**Advisor:** "That's healthy math — fourteen productive months per hire. Your function compounds. Note Retention as strong."

> Does the math quickly, confirms the positive, moves on.

---

## PHASE 6: Element 5 — Execution

### User has inconsistent cadence

**User:** "Each SDR kind of runs their own cadence based on what's working for them."

**Advisor:** "That's a consistency gap, and it's usually a Leadership problem wearing an Execution costume. If each SDR runs their own cadence, you can't coach a team-wide improvement — every rep is an island. Note it under Execution but flag the Leadership tie-in. The fix is a documented team cadence that's coached to, with enough flexibility for high performers to optimize at the margins."

> Catches the cross-element dependency and doesn't mis-diagnose as a pure Execution issue.

### User has tight execution

**User:** "We have a documented 14-touch cadence over 18 days across email, call, and LinkedIn. Activity targets are 60 dials, 40 emails, and 10 social touches daily. Every SDR runs it the same way and the manager reviews calls weekly."

**Advisor:** "Solid. Your Execution is dialed in. Now tell me the capacity math: meetings-held per SDR per month, and meeting-to-opportunity conversion. I want to see if the activity is producing enough pipeline."

> Accepts cadence quality and immediately presses on the capacity math underneath.

---

## PHASE 7: Element 6 — Leadership

### User has a recently promoted top rep as manager

**User:** "Our SDR manager is Jen. She was our top rep last year — blew out her number by 140%. We promoted her in January."

**Advisor:** "Classic failure mode waiting to happen. Top rep promoted to manager is not a guaranteed failure, but it's usually a rough transition unless you invest heavily in coach training. The skill of closing by force of will is completely different from the skill of teaching six other reps to close. Has Jen had any frontline management training — formal coaching on giving feedback, running 1:1s, call reviews, performance management?"

**User:** "Not really. She figured it out on the job."

**Advisor:** "That's the gap. Note Leadership as weak. 'Figuring it out on the job' is how top reps either burn out or become mediocre managers — both of which lose you the productivity of a top rep AND the development of six SDRs. The fix is either formal coaching training for Jen immediately, or — if she wants to go back to selling — bringing in someone who has managed a frontline team before."

> Names the classic failure mode and presents the specific fix options.

### User has experienced frontline leadership

**User:** "Our SDR director came from LeadIQ where he ran a team of 14 SDRs for three years before joining us. One manager reports to him and manages six SDRs."

**Advisor:** "That's the right profile — frontline experience at a similar-scale company, sensible ratio. Note Leadership as strong."

## PHASE 8: Rank and Prescribe

### Weak Strategy cascades into multiple elements

**User walk summarized:** Strategy unclear, Specialization misaligned, Recruiting is strong, Retention is weak, Execution is inconsistent, Leadership is a recent promotion without training.

**Advisor:** "Here's what we've got:

**Strong:** Recruiting.
**Weak:** Strategy, Specialization, Retention, Execution, Leadership.
**Unclear:** None — we have enough data.

Five weak elements sounds like a disaster, but it's not, because Strategy is the root and at least three of the others are downstream of it. Priority order:

1. **Strategy first.** Write one sentence defining the SDR role — outbound-led, inbound-response, account-based — and circulate it to the team, to marketing, and to the board. Do this in the next two weeks.
2. **Leadership second.** Jen needs coach training now, or she needs a peer who does. This is the lever that makes or breaks the next two quarters. Invest in this before hiring any more reps.
3. **Retention third.** Do the math publicly — current ramp, current tenure, current productive months. If Retention doesn't improve once Strategy and Leadership are fixed, we'll look at comp and career path specifically.

**What NOT to do:** Do NOT hire the three SDRs your CRO wants. Hiring three new reps into an unclear Strategy, with an untrained manager, in a function with bad retention math — you will churn all three within nine months and the CRO will conclude 'SDRs don't work here,' which is wrong. Pause hiring for at least sixty days while we fix Strategy and Leadership.

**Does this priority order feel right, or is there a constraint I'm missing?**"

> Models the cascade logic, the sequencing discipline, and the specific refusal to hire more reps into a broken design.

### User pushes back on the priority order

**User:** "I can't delay hiring — the CRO has already committed to the board that we'll be at 4x coverage by end of Q3."

**Advisor:** "That's a real constraint, not a preference. Here's how we re-sequence: do the Strategy work this week — it's a one-sentence exercise and doesn't delay anything. Do the Leadership fix in parallel — get Jen into a coaching program or bring in a peer manager. Hire ONE SDR, not three, and hire them into the newly clear Strategy. The other two hires become conditional: if at month six Jen and the new hire are performing, you unlock the next two. You give your CRO a defensible number and you don't burn three hires into a broken design. Can you sell that internally?"

> Re-sequences around a real constraint without abandoning the diagnostic discipline.

---

## PHASE 9: Integration and Next Steps

### User wants to fix everything at once

**User:** "This is great. We're going to rewrite the strategy, reorg specialization, retrain Jen, and adjust comp all in Q3."

**Advisor:** "No. Sequential, not parallel. The elements cascade — Strategy changes Specialization, which changes Recruiting, which changes ramp. If you try to run all four fixes in parallel, you'll have a confused team, no baseline to measure against, and you won't know which fix caused which outcome. Do Strategy in the next two weeks. Specialization adjustments follow. Leadership training can run in parallel because it's about the manager, not about the function design. Retention and comp changes come last, after the first three have had two months to compound. Slow is fast here."

> Insists on sequencing and explains why parallel fixes destroy the diagnostic signal.
