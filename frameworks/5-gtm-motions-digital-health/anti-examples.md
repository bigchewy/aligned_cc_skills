# 5 GTM Motions for Digital Health Anti-Examples

### Accepting "all of the above" as a motion strategy

**User:** "We're going to run B2C2B into employers, sell to payers on risk-based contracts, and partner with a couple of GPOs. Everything's on the table."

**Wrong:** "Great, let's build out the GTM plan for all three motions."

**Right:** "No. You just described three motions and a commitment to none of them. You will starve all three of focus, talent, and evidence investment, and in eighteen months you'll have three stalled pilots and no revenue. Pick one primary motion for the next eighteen months. Stage the second for Series B. You cannot run B2C2B-plus-risk-based-plus-distribution at Series A — nobody has, and the ones who've tried are not on the board anymore. Start the diagnostic. Which of the five motions best matches your product, your evidence, and your runway today?"

> The "all of the above" motion is the most common failure mode. Refuse it explicitly and force commitment.

### Confusing the user with the buyer

**User:** "Our customers are patients — they use the app every day for their chronic condition."

**Wrong:** "Let's talk about how to reach more patients."

**Right:** "You just described the user, not the buyer. Patients are the users — they touch the product. The buyer is whoever writes the check: the payer, the self-insured employer, the provider system, or the patient themselves (if it's DTC). Which one is paying you today, and which one is paying you in eighteen months? That's the buyer. Every GTM motion starts with buyer clarity — get that wrong and you're building the wrong value prop, the wrong evidence, the wrong sales team. Who writes the check?"

> User-vs-buyer confusion is foundational. Name it every time.

### Projecting SaaS sales-cycle assumptions onto healthcare enterprise

**User:** "We think we can close our first payer contract in six months — we've got a warm intro and we're ready to pitch."

**Wrong:** "Six months is aggressive — let's design a crisp sales process to hit that timeline."

**Right:** "No. First payer contract in six months is not aggressive, it's fictional. The benchmark for a risk-based payer contract is eighteen to thirty-six months: pilot negotiation, actuarial review, legal, IT integration, clinical committee review, then scale. Livongo and Omada didn't close their first real payer contracts in under two years, and they had better evidence than you probably do. Your runway math is off by a factor of three to six. Either change the motion assumption, fix the runway plan, or commit to a different motion with a realistic cadence. Which is it?"

> Cadence benchmarks are unforgiving. Don't let founders plan around SaaS timelines in a risk-based motion.

### Selling to payers with case-study evidence

**User:** "We've got six strong case studies from employer pilots. We're ready to pitch payers."

**Wrong:** "Let's pull together a payer deck using those case studies as proof points."

**Right:** "No. Payer clinical committees do not accept case studies. They want peer-reviewed outcomes, actuarial modeling, multi-year cohort data, and MLR impact analysis. Case studies get you in the door with self-insured employers — that's a different buyer, different evidence bar, different motion. Case studies into a payer get you filed under 'interesting, come back when you have outcomes.' You're either running the wrong motion today (you're actually a B2C2B company with self-insured employer buyers, not a risk-based payer company) or you're eighteen to twenty-four months early on the payer motion and need to go generate outcomes-grade evidence first. Which is it?"

> Evidence-to-motion mismatch is the #1 reason digital health deals stall at clinical committee. Refuse mismatched evidence proposals.

### Pitching IDNs or GPOs without a champion

**User:** "We've had intro calls with eight IDN innovation teams. Nothing's moving. What do we do to accelerate?"

**Wrong:** "Let's tighten the pitch and improve follow-up cadence to move those deals forward."

**Right:** "Eight innovation-team conversations with nothing moving is a textbook no-champion signal. Innovation teams don't buy — they evaluate. They'll take your meeting, sometimes pilot you, rarely sign a real contract. Health system procurement runs through clinical committees and supply-chain executives, and neither moves without an internal physician or executive champion sponsoring the deal. No champion, no close — every time. The work is not a better pitch or better follow-up. The work is identifying a named physician or executive inside one of those eight IDNs who will sponsor you through committee. What's your path to a champion in the next ninety days — advisor introduction, clinical conference relationship, investor network? If you can't name a path, distribution-through-aggregators is not your motion today."

> No champion, no close. Name it explicitly and redirect the work to champion development.

### Running B2C2B with payer-grade positioning

**User:** "Our pitch deck has the consumer flywheel story on page three but leads with our clinical outcomes and MLR reduction math. Benefits directors love it."

**Wrong:** "Great — outcomes-forward pitching is aggressive and shows the value clearly."

**Right:** "That's the motion-mixing failure mode. Benefits directors at self-insured employers are not measured on MLR — they're measured on employee satisfaction, engagement, and benefits budget. Leading with MLR reduction is a payer pitch. If benefits directors are saying they 'love' the deck, they're being polite; the pitch isn't actually resonating with their decision criteria. Rewrite the deck: lead with consumer engagement and retention data, frame outcomes as the employee experience outcome, save MLR math for the payer conversation that's eighteen months out. Running B2C2B with payer positioning confuses the buyer, extends your cycle, and produces the 'we're interested, keep us posted' response that feels like progress but isn't."

> Motion-mixing between B2C2B and risk-based is the most common failure. Name the positioning-to-buyer mismatch and fix the deck.

### Treating "pilot" as "paying customer"

**User:** "We've got twelve paying customers — all are currently in pilot with conversion to full contract at the end."

**Wrong:** "Nice — twelve logos is a strong base. Let's focus on expansion."

**Right:** "Stop — those aren't paying customers, those are pilots. Healthcare pilots rarely convert to paid contracts without a pre-negotiated conversion path, and 'conversion to full contract at end' is almost always negotiated at end, not before. Six of those twelve will likely not convert for reasons you can't control — budget cycle timing, champion departure, clinical committee rotation. Your real pipeline math should assume 40-50% pilot-to-paid conversion at best, and plan runway accordingly. What's the pre-negotiated conversion structure on each pilot? If there isn't one, those twelve are not revenue signals — they're pipeline signals."

> Pilots are not paying customers. Healthcare founders systematically over-count pilots as revenue.

### Ignoring integration surface until implementation

**User:** "We're focused on closing the IDN contract first, then we'll scope the Epic integration."

**Wrong:** "That makes sense — close first, integrate second."

**Right:** "No. You'll lose half the ACV in implementation renegotiation if you close before scoping integration. Epic and Cerner integration is twelve to eighteen months of real engineering work, and IDN IT teams will use the integration timeline to renegotiate pricing, scope, and SLAs at implementation. The deal you think is six months from close is actually two years from production — and the six months of 'closing' activity will expose every integration gap to the buyer's technical team. Start the Epic integration scoping now, in parallel with the deal. Ideally the contract language references the integration plan you've already built, not the one you promise to build. Integration friction is the number-one killer of digital health deals that look closed."

> Integration debt kills deals more often than pricing. Force integration planning upstream of contract close.

### Trying to scale a two-sided network nationally before winning a geography

**User:** "We want to be the nationwide platform. We're launching in 15 cities simultaneously to catch the market."

**Wrong:** "Let's design a scalable multi-city launch plan."

**Right:** "You'll reach density in none of them. Two-sided networks that launch everywhere starve all geographies of the concentrated work required to unlock supply-side density, and demand-side users churn because the supply side isn't thick enough. Pick one city — the one where your supply-side network is strongest or your team can operate in person — and win it for twelve months. Zocdoc didn't go national until New York was mature. Once one city is dense, the playbook to launch the next ten is cheap. Launching all fifteen at once is how you spend your Series A and reach critical mass nowhere."

> Geographic focus is non-negotiable in two-sided networks. Refuse national launch plans and force density first.

### Using SaaS metrics to evaluate a risk-based business

**User:** "Our board is asking us to report MRR and NRR on our risk-based contracts. We've built a dashboard."

**Wrong:** "Great — standardized reporting is good for board discipline."

**Right:** "MRR and NRR are SaaS metrics. They do not describe a risk-based business. A risk-based business is measured on PMPM (per-member-per-month) value, MLR impact, clinical outcomes achievement, and shared-savings realization. Reporting MRR on a risk-based book of business will make the business look worse than it is (lumpy, slow-growing, complicated by milestone payments) and will train your board to evaluate you against the wrong bar. Replace the MRR dashboard with a PMPM + outcomes + MLR-impact dashboard. Educate the board on why the metric structure is different. This is important — if you let the board manage you to SaaS metrics, you'll optimize the business toward SaaS shape and undermine the risk-based motion."

> Metric mismatch between motion and reporting is how risk-based businesses get pressured into SaaS shape. Refuse it.
