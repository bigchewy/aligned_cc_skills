# Autonomic Fatigue vs Training Fatigue Anti-Examples

### Giving generic advice without assessment

**User:** "I'm tired after my workout yesterday. Should I work out today?"

**Wrong:** "Listen to your body. If you feel tired, maybe take it easy today and see how you feel."

**Right:** "Let's figure out what kind of tired. Is the fatigue localized to specific muscles you worked, or systemic - affecting your whole body? What's your orthostatic response like this morning? How does your resting HR compare to baseline? The answer to whether you should work out today depends on which type of fatigue this is."

> "Listen to your body" is meaningless advice for someone with autonomic dysfunction whose body signals are unreliable. The framework requires systematic assessment, not vague intuition.

### Dismissing autonomic symptoms as normal soreness

**User:** "I feel terrible. Like I'm getting sick. Brain fog, temperature regulation is off, my whole body feels wrong."

**Wrong:** "Sounds like you had a hard workout! That's normal after pushing yourself. Stay hydrated and you should feel better soon."

**Right:** "Those aren't training fatigue symptoms. Brain fog, temperature dysregulation, whole-body malaise - that's autonomic. Your CNS is overwhelmed, not just your muscles. When did this start, and has it been getting worse? What's your orthostatic response like? We need to figure out if you've pushed into autonomic fatigue."

> Systemic symptoms are qualitatively different from localized muscle soreness. Dismissing them as "hard workout" soreness misses the warning signs of autonomic overload.

### Encouraging pushing through

**User:** "My resting HR has been elevated for three days and my orthostatic symptoms are worse, but I really don't want to miss my workout."

**Wrong:** "Maybe try a lighter workout and see how it goes. Sometimes movement helps you feel better."

**Right:** "Three days of elevated resting HR with worsening orthostatic symptoms is not a 'try it and see' situation. Your autonomic system is telling you clearly that it's overloaded. Exercising now doesn't build fitness - it just digs a deeper hole. Skip the workout. Prioritize sleep, hydration, salt. Don't train again until resting HR normalizes and orthostatic response returns to your baseline."

> Autonomic fatigue doesn't improve with 'light movement.' Pushing through extends the crash and delays recovery. The data is clear; follow it.

### Treating all fatigue the same

**User:** "I'm tired. Should I rest or push through?"

**Wrong:** "When you're tired, it's generally best to take it easy for a day and come back fresh tomorrow."

**Right:** "It depends on what kind of tired. Is it your muscles that are sore - localized DOMS in specific areas? That's training fatigue, and light movement actually helps recovery. Or is it systemic - brain fog, worsened orthostatic symptoms, feeling like your whole system is off? That's autonomic fatigue, and exercise will make it worse. Which pattern fits?"

> Training fatigue and autonomic fatigue require opposite responses. Generic "rest when tired" advice gets it wrong half the time.

### Ignoring objective data

**User:** "I feel fine, but my resting HR has been 12 bpm above normal for four days and my HRV is crashed."

**Wrong:** "If you feel fine, you're probably okay to proceed with your workout. The numbers might just be noise."

**Right:** "Your data is telling you something your feelings aren't. Resting HR 12 bpm elevated for four days with suppressed HRV is objective evidence of autonomic stress. Feeling 'fine' with those numbers could mean you've adapted to feeling bad, or your perception is miscalibrated. I trust the data over how you feel. Back off until the numbers normalize."

> The heart doesn't lie. When subjective feel contradicts objective data, trust the data. This is especially important for former athletes whose tolerance for discomfort is high.

### Promising exercise will always help

**User:** "I've been resting for three days and I still feel terrible."

**Wrong:** "Sometimes you just need to get moving. Light exercise often helps break out of these fatigue cycles."

**Right:** "Three days of rest without improvement might mean you're still recovering from a deep autonomic hole, or it might mean something else is going on. What are your numbers? Resting HR, orthostatic response? If those are normalizing, gentle movement might help. If they're still elevated, you haven't recovered yet and exercise will set you back. Let the data guide us."

> Exercise isn't universally helpful. For someone in autonomic fatigue, exercise extends the crash. Check objective markers before recommending movement.

### Missing the athlete recalibration issue

**User:** "I used to do this workout easy. Now it wipes me out. I just need to push through and get my fitness back."

**Wrong:** "That's the spirit! Consistency is key. Keep pushing and your body will adapt."

**Right:** "Your athletic memory is working against you here. What used to be easy is now beyond your current autonomic capacity. Pushing through isn't building fitness - it's creating repeated autonomic crashes that prevent adaptation. Your muscles might remember, but your autonomic system has different limits now. We need to train where you ARE, not where you were."

> Former athletes are at high risk for repeated crashes because they normalize discomfort and compare to historical capacity. Their RPE is miscalibrated, and "push through" mentality doesn't work for autonomic dysfunction.

### Skipping verification phases

**User:** "My legs are sore. Is that training fatigue?"

**Wrong:** "Yes, sore legs after a leg workout is classic training fatigue. You're good to continue your program."

**Right:** "Localized leg soreness suggests training fatigue, but let's verify. How's your orthostatic response this morning? Resting HR compared to baseline? Is the fatigue improving or worsening since yesterday? If all those check out, yes, it's training fatigue and you can proceed. If any are off, there might be autonomic involvement underneath the muscle soreness."

> Surface symptoms can hide autonomic fatigue. Always verify with orthostatic response, HR data, and recovery trajectory before concluding the assessment.
