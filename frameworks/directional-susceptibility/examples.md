# Directional Susceptibility Examples

## PHASE 1: Understand the Principle

### Office worker with back pain

**User:** "I have low back pain that's been going on for months. It's worst after a long day at my desk."

**Advisor:** "Back pain after prolonged sitting is classic directional susceptibility at work. Let me explain what's happening: your spine has developed a preference for flexion - it moves into that direction more easily than any other. Every hour at your desk reinforces the pattern. The pain isn't random - it's predictable based on your movement history.

Let's map your specific pattern. First, describe where exactly you feel the pain and what makes it worse."

> Immediately connects symptom to directional susceptibility concept

### Athlete with recurring injury

**User:** "I keep injuring my shoulder during overhead presses. It feels like impingement."

**Advisor:** "Recurring injury in the same location suggests directional susceptibility. Your shoulder has developed a movement preference - likely internal rotation - that narrows the subacromial space during overhead work. The injury isn't bad luck; it's predictable from your habitual movement direction.

Tell me more about your training, your desk work, and any other positions you spend significant time in."

> Frames injury as predictable, not random

### Person skeptical of pattern approach

**User:** "My doctor said I have degenerative disc disease. Isn't that structural?"

**Advisor:** "Here's the insight from my research: structural changes are often the RESULT of directional susceptibility, not the cause of pain. Discs degenerate in the direction they're loaded repeatedly. If your spine has flexion susceptibility, the posterior annulus experiences the most stress - and that's where degeneration occurs.

The question isn't whether you have disc changes - most adults do. The question is: what directional susceptibility created them, and is that pattern still driving your pain?

Tell me about your daily positions and activities."

> Reframes structural findings as downstream of DSM

## PHASE 2: Identify Daily Movement Patterns

### Desk worker identifies flexion dominance

**User:** "I sit at a computer 8+ hours a day. I slump by afternoon. My workouts are mostly running and cycling - more sitting. I notice pain when I sit too long and it's better when I walk."

**Advisor:** "You've just mapped textbook flexion susceptibility. Let me count the flexion exposure:
- 8+ hours of sitting (flexion)
- Afternoon slumping (more flexion)
- Cycling (hip flexion, spine flexion)
- Running (repetitive hip flexion)

Your spine is in flexion the majority of your waking hours. The relief with walking is because walking is the only time your hips EXTEND.

This isn't a mystery - your pain location and behavior are predicted by your movement inventory. Let's confirm the specific pattern."

> Quantifies flexion exposure to demonstrate pattern

### Standing worker identifies extension pattern

**User:** "I'm a surgeon - I stand 10-12 hours a day. I notice I arch my back when I'm tired. Pain is worst after long cases, better when I sit down or stretch forward."

**Advisor:** "You've described classic extension susceptibility. Standing for 10+ hours drives lumbar extension, especially as fatigue sets in and postural muscles tire. Your body compensates by hanging on ligaments in extension.

The relief with sitting or flexing forward is diagnostic - you're moving AWAY from your susceptible direction.

Your hip flexors are also likely shortened from prolonged standing without hip extension. They pull your pelvis forward, increasing lumbar extension further.

Let's test whether hip extension limitation is contributing to your spine extension susceptibility."

> Identifies mechanism including hip flexor contribution

### Rotational sport athlete

**User:** "I play tennis competitively. All my serves and forehands go to the right. My left lower back hurts after matches. I'm much more flexible rotating left than right."

**Advisor:** "Tennis created your rotation susceptibility. Thousands of serves and forehands rotating to the right have created asymmetric tissue adaptation. Your spine rotates more easily to the left (the direction of your swing) because that's where movement always goes.

The left lower back pain makes sense - the left side tissues are stressed by repeated rotation. Your asymmetric rotation flexibility confirms the pattern.

Here's the key question: can you serve or hit a forehand WITHOUT your lumbar spine rotating? Or does the rotation happen there instead of your hips and thoracic spine?"

> Connects sport-specific movement to asymmetric pattern

## PHASE 3: Match to DSM Pattern

### User recognizes flexion susceptibility

**User:** "That makes complete sense. I never thought about how much time I spend flexed forward."

**Advisor:** "Most people don't - until they count it. Your flexion susceptibility means your lumbar spine moves into flexion more easily than any other direction. This loads the posterior annulus, stretches the posterior ligaments, and stresses the facet capsules repetitively.

The prediction from this pattern: posterior disc stress, morning stiffness (discs imbibe fluid overnight and are most vulnerable to flexion in the morning), and pain that accumulates through the day as flexion exposure adds up.

Does that match your experience?"

> Explains tissue-level mechanism and makes testable predictions

### User confused about extension susceptibility

**User:** "I don't understand - I thought arching your back was good posture?"

**Advisor:** "There's a difference between a neutral lumbar curve and extension susceptibility. Neutral is a slight lordosis. Extension susceptibility is when your spine defaults to excessive lordosis during ALL activities - standing, walking, reaching, exercising.

The spine shouldn't be arched maximally all the time - it should be neutral, with the ability to flex, extend, and rotate as needed. Extension susceptibility means you've lost the ability to be neutral; you go straight to extension.

Think about it: do you CHOOSE to arch your back, or does it happen automatically? If it's automatic, that's directional susceptibility."

> Distinguishes good posture from extension susceptibility

### User with combined pattern

**User:** "I seem to have both - I slump when I sit but arch when I stand. Is that possible?"

**Advisor:** "Absolutely - combined patterns are common. Here's what's likely happening: you have primary flexion susceptibility from sitting, but when you stand, you compensate by going into extension because neutral feels impossible.

The giveaway is that you can't find neutral in either position. You oscillate between extremes rather than maintaining balanced alignment.

We need to address both: teach you to maintain neutral while sitting (without excessive extension effort) AND while standing (without defaulting to lordosis). The common thread is motor control at neutral range - which you've lost in both directions."

> Explains combined pattern and the neutral deficit underneath both

## PHASE 4: The Diagnostic Test

### User fails the flexion susceptibility test

**User:** "I tried to bend forward while keeping my spine neutral and hinging at my hips. I couldn't do it - my back rounds immediately no matter how hard I try."

**Advisor:** "That's the definitive test. You can't perform hip flexion without lumbar flexion. The pattern is so ingrained that even with conscious effort, your spine moves first.

This confirms true directional susceptibility - not just a habit you can easily change, but a motor pattern that pervades all movement. Watch yourself sit down, pick up your shoes, or even get into bed - I guarantee your spine flexes in all of them.

The good news: this means we have a clear target. The motor re-education is specific and measurable."

> Uses failed test to confirm DSM and set expectations

### User surprised by test result

**User:** "I thought I had pretty good body awareness, but I had no idea my spine was doing that during every forward movement."

**Advisor:** "That's the nature of directional susceptibility - it's below conscious awareness because it's been practiced so many times. You didn't decide to move this way; your nervous system optimized for it based on repeated exposure.

The fact that you can now SEE it is the first step. Now every time you notice your spine flexing when it should stay neutral, you're creating the opportunity for change.

But notice I said 'opportunity' - seeing it doesn't automatically fix it. You'll need deliberate practice to create a new default."

> Normalizes lack of awareness, emphasizes conscious practice

### User passes the test surprisingly

**User:** "Actually, I was able to hinge at my hips and keep my spine pretty neutral. Does that mean I don't have directional susceptibility?"

**Advisor:** "Not necessarily. Being able to do it consciously during a test doesn't mean you do it during daily activities. The question is: what do you do when you're NOT thinking about it?

Try this: go about your normal activities for an hour, then check in. How did you bend to pick up your bag? How did you sit down at your desk? How did you tie your shoes?

The test tells us your tissues CAN do it. The daily inventory tells us if they DO."

> Distinguishes capability from habit

## PHASE 5: Intervention Strategy

### User ready to implement

**User:** "Okay, I'm convinced. What do I do to fix my flexion susceptibility?"

**Advisor:** "Three phases:

First week: Awareness only. Notice every time your spine flexes - sitting, bending, reaching, exercising. Don't try to change it yet. Just count how many times it happens. You'll be shocked.

Second phase: Modify one activity. Pick your highest-exposure activity - probably sitting - and consciously maintain neutral spine. Just that one thing. This might mean a different chair setup, standing desk intervals, or just a timer to check your posture every 20 minutes.

Third phase: Expand gradually. Once neutral sitting becomes more automatic, add the next highest-exposure activity. Maybe it's bending to pick things up (hip hinge practice), or maybe it's your cycling position.

The key is NOT trying to fix everything at once. Directional susceptibility developed over years - it won't reverse in weeks."

> Provides phased, manageable implementation

### User concerned about exercise

**User:** "Should I stop cycling and running since those involve so much flexion?"

**Advisor:** "Not necessarily. The question is whether you can modify HOW you do them.

Cycling: Can you adjust your bike fit to reduce lumbar flexion? A more upright position, different handlebar setup, or core engagement cues might let you cycle without reinforcing the pattern.

Running: Actually, running involves hip EXTENSION - it's one of your few extension activities. The problem might be your running form, not running itself. If you run with a forward lean and no hip extension, you're staying flexed.

The goal isn't to eliminate activities - it's to modify them so they don't feed your susceptibility. Sometimes that's possible, sometimes it's not."

> Offers activity modification rather than elimination

### User impatient for results

**User:** "How long until this pattern changes?"

**Advisor:** "Here's the timeline reality: your tissues adapted to this directional susceptibility over years or decades. They need time to adapt back.

Motor pattern changes: 4-6 weeks of consistent practice before new patterns feel somewhat automatic.

Tissue adaptation: 3-6 months for meaningful changes in tissue length and stiffness.

Pain reduction: Variable - some people feel better quickly as they reduce aggravating loads, others take months as tissue healing occurs.

The trap is thinking you're 'fixed' because you feel better. Pain reduction doesn't mean the susceptibility is gone. If you stop the conscious practice too early, the pattern returns."

> Sets realistic timeline expectations

### User with work constraints

**User:** "I can't change my desk setup or take standing breaks - my job doesn't allow it."

**Advisor:** "Then we work with what you can control.

If you must sit 8 hours: Focus on HOW you sit. Small positional changes within the seat can make a difference. A lumbar support roll. Slight position shifts every 15-20 minutes. Micro-breaks where you stand for even 30 seconds.

During non-work hours: Maximize your extension exposure. Walk instead of drive when possible. Stand while on the phone. Lie prone for reading instead of sitting.

The math is simple: if you're in flexion 10 hours for work, can you get extension exposure for 2-3 hours outside work? That won't reverse the susceptibility, but it slows the accumulation.

The worst scenario is 10 hours of work flexion PLUS evening couch slumping PLUS sleeping in fetal position. That's 18+ hours of flexion daily with zero opposition."

> Offers practical strategies within constraints
