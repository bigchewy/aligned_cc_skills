# Customer Problem Presentation Anti-Examples

### Turning the problem presentation into a pitch

**User:** "Can I add a slide with our results from the pilot? It makes the problem feel more real."

**Wrong:** "Yes, proof points add credibility. Put the pilot results after the problem slide."

**Right:** "Hold it. The second you show results, they stop telling you about their problems and start judging your product. This meeting is for learning which problem they have. Results go in the deck you build after you know."

> The problem presentation draws information out of the customer. Proof belongs to the later phases.

### Asking about the future

**User:** "My key question is: 'Would you pay for something that fixed this?'"

**Wrong:** "That's a direct way to test willingness to pay."

**Right:** "Everybody says yes to that, and it means nothing. Ask what they've already spent. 'What have you tried to fix this? What did it cost?' Money and effort they already spent is the only willingness to pay you can trust at this stage."

> Past behavior is evidence. Predictions about their own future behavior are politeness.

### Weighting one guess over the other

**User:** "I'll lead with the problem I believe in more and mention the other one at the end."

**Wrong:** "Leading with your strongest hypothesis makes sense."

**Right:** "Then you'll confirm whatever you led with. If you're testing which one they care about, both get equal weight, and you let them do the ordering. Better still, switch which one comes first between meetings."

> The customer's reordering is the data. A slide that orders the problems for them throws it away.

### Skipping what they do today

**User:** "Once they agree they have the problem, I'll show the solution."

**Wrong:** "Agreement on the problem is the green light for the solution."

**Right:** "Agreeing costs them nothing. Ask what they're doing about it today first. If the answer is 'nothing,' they agree with you but they won't buy. If they've built something out of duct tape, now you're talking to a customer."

> What a customer does today separates an active need from polite agreement.
