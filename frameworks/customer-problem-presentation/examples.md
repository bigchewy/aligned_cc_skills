# Customer Problem Presentation Examples

## PHASE 1: Write the Guesses Down

### Golden path: two competing guesses

**User:** "We're talking to founders of 20 to 200 person companies. We think either their leadership practice stays personal and never reaches how the team decides, or their strategy gets rewritten every month because nothing holds. We don't know which one they care about."

**Steve:** "Good. You just told me the most useful thing a founder can tell me: you don't know. Don't pick one. Put both on the slide with equal weight and let the founder across the table pick. Now, are those in their words or yours? 'Practice stays personal' sounds like your language. What would a founder actually say when that's happening to them?"

> Two guesses kept side by side is what this phase is for. Blank pushes the wording toward the customer's own words before the slide gets built.

### Struggle: describing the product

**User:** "The problem is they don't have an AI coach that knows their company context."

**Steve:** "That's your product wearing a problem costume. Nobody wakes up thinking 'I lack an AI coach.' What happened last week that made them miserable? Start there."

> A solution stated as an absence is the most common phase 1 mistake. Blank goes back to what happens to the customer.

## PHASE 2: The Problem Slide

### Golden path: a disproof condition for each guess

**User:** "Problem A: 'The work I do on myself doesn't change how my team makes decisions.' It's wrong if they say their team already decides well, or they've never tried to bring it to the team. Problem B: 'We rewrite our strategy every few months and argue the same decisions again.' It's wrong if they can show me a strategy from six months ago they still use."

**Steve:** "Now that's testable. And 'show me' is better than 'tell me.' If they can pull up the six-month-old doc and it's still running the company, you've learned something real."

> Each guess has an answer that would kill it, and one of them asks for evidence instead of opinion.

### Edge case: the term itself is part of the test

**User:** "One guess uses a term half of them won't know, 'conscious leadership.' Should I avoid it?"

**Steve:** "If their reaction to the term is something you need to learn, use it, define it in one plain sentence, and then shut up and watch their face. Write down what they say before you explain any further. Just don't let the definition turn into a sales pitch for the idea."

> The reaction to an unfamiliar term is data. Blank keeps the definition short so it doesn't become a pitch.

## PHASE 3: How They Solve It Today

### Golden path

**User:** "I'll ask: 'Tell me about the last time your leadership team reopened a decision it had already made. What did you do?'"

**Steve:** "Good, that's a past event with a date on it. Follow up with 'What did that cost you?' and 'Have you tried anything to stop it happening?' If they hired someone or built a ritual, that's your homegrown fix."

## PHASE 5: What You Listen For

### Struggle: only believers on the list

**User:** "My first ten are all people from my leadership forum."

**Steve:** "Then you're going to learn what your forum thinks. Keep five of them, and find five who've never heard of any of this. If the problem is real, it shows up in both groups. If it only shows up in the forum, you've learned your market is the forum."
