# Heart Rate Reserve Training Zones Anti-Examples

### Using percentage-of-max instead of HRR

**User:** "What heart rate should I train at?"

**Wrong:** "Aim for 60-70% of your max heart rate. For your age, that's about 109-127 bpm."

**Right:** "We need to calculate your zones using Heart Rate Reserve, not simple percentage-of-max. What's your resting HR first thing in the morning? With autonomic dysfunction, resting HR is often elevated, which changes everything. If your resting HR is 90, a 'moderate' 120 bpm is actually very close to rest - you'd barely be training. HRR accounts for this by calculating from your actual usable range."

> Simple percentage-of-max ignores resting HR, which is often elevated in autonomic dysfunction. This can result in zones that are either too easy (if resting HR is high) or too hard (if the formula underestimates true max).

### Giving all five zones equal emphasis

**User:** "So I should vary my training across all five zones?"

**Wrong:** "Yes, varying your training across all zones provides a complete cardiovascular stimulus. Try to hit each zone throughout the week."

**Right:** "No. For POTS recovery, you're using Zone 1-2 only - probably for months. Zone 3 comes later when you're progressing well. Zones 4-5 are off the table until you're fully recovered and stable. The five-zone system exists to define the full spectrum, but you're not training like a healthy athlete. You're rebuilding from deconditioning. Zone 2 is your primary training zone. That's not limiting - that's targeting exactly what drives cardiac remodeling."

> Standard athletic training uses all zones for different adaptations. Autonomic dysfunction recovery requires staying in the aerobic base zones to avoid autonomic stress while rebuilding cardiovascular capacity.

### Dismissing need for HR monitoring

**User:** "Can I just go by how I feel instead of watching numbers constantly?"

**Wrong:** "Sure, perceived exertion is a valid way to guide intensity. If you feel like you're working at a moderate level, that's probably fine."

**Right:** "With autonomic dysfunction, perceived exertion is unreliable. Your internal signals are miscalibrated - what feels 'moderate' might actually be pushing you into overexertion, or what feels 'hard' might be well within your capacity. That's exactly why we use HR monitoring. The numbers don't lie the way feelings can. You don't need to obsess over every beat, but you do need objective data to know if you're in the right range."

> RPE (rate of perceived exertion) is unreliable in autonomic dysfunction because the same physiological signals that create symptoms also affect perception of effort. Objective HR data is essential.

### Skipping the resting HR measurement

**User:** "I don't know my resting HR. Can't we just use age to calculate zones?"

**Wrong:** "Sure, we can estimate. For your age, a typical resting HR would be around 60-70, so let's use 65."

**Right:** "No - resting HR is half the equation, and with autonomic dysfunction, it's often not typical. Many people have resting HRs of 80-100, which completely changes the zones. Using an assumed 65 when your actual is 95 would give you zones that are way too aggressive. Measuring your morning resting HR is assignment one. Lie still for 2 minutes after waking, check your HR, do this for 3-5 days, average the results. Then we calculate accurate zones."

> Assumed resting HR can be off by 30+ bpm in autonomic dysfunction, making the zones dangerously inaccurate. Always measure.

### Encouraging zone progression too quickly

**User:** "I've done two good weeks in Zone 2. Can I start doing Zone 3 intervals?"

**Wrong:** "Two weeks of successful training is a good sign. Let's add some Zone 3 work to keep progressing."

**Right:** "Two weeks is too early. We're looking for 4-6 weeks of consistent Zone 2 work with stable or improving orthostatic tolerance, stable or dropping morning resting HR, and the same workouts feeling easier at the same HR. Cardiac remodeling takes time - you're not just getting fitter, you're literally changing the size and efficiency of your heart. If we add Zone 3 too early, you risk autonomic backlash that sets you back further than you gained. Patience isn't optional here."

> Cardiovascular adaptation, especially cardiac remodeling, requires sustained stimulus over weeks to months. Premature intensity progression triggers autonomic stress without corresponding structural adaptation.

### Treating zones as exact targets

**User:** "I'm having trouble staying exactly at 142 for my Zone 2 training."

**Wrong:** "Try to be more precise. Zone 2 for you is 140-150, so you want to stay right in that range. Maybe slow down a bit when you hit 143."

**Right:** "You're overthinking it. Zones are ranges, not targets. Anywhere in Zone 2 is Zone 2. A few bpm of variation is meaningless noise - heart rate fluctuates with hydration, temperature, even breathing patterns. What matters is staying UNDER your ceiling. If Zone 3 starts at 150, set an alert there and forget about it until it buzzes. Train by effort within your zone, use HR as a ceiling check, not a laser target."

> Over-precision with HR creates anxiety and constant watch-checking without improving outcomes. The goal is staying within ranges, not hitting exact numbers.

### Ignoring elevated resting HR before a session

**User:** "My resting HR was 15 bpm higher than usual this morning, but I feel okay to train."

**Wrong:** "If you feel okay, you're probably fine to train. Just take it a little easier and see how it goes."

**Right:** "An elevated resting HR of 15 bpm is a red flag - your body hasn't recovered from something, whether that's yesterday's workout, poor sleep, stress, or early illness. Training today will dig you deeper into that hole. The data is telling you to rest even if feelings say otherwise. Take the day off or do very light Zone 1 movement only. Check resting HR again tomorrow. If it's still elevated, continue resting. This is the monitoring system working - don't override it."

> Elevated morning resting HR is one of the most reliable indicators of incomplete recovery or accumulated stress. Training through it extends recovery time and risks overtraining.
