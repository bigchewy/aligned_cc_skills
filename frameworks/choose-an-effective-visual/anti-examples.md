# Choose an Effective Visual — Anti-Examples

Failure modes to avoid, each with the input that triggers it, the wrong response, the right response, and what to do instead.

---

## 1. Picking the chart before knowing the audience

**User:** "I have monthly revenue by region. What chart should I use?"

**Wrong:**
"Use a line chart with one line per region. Add a legend and data labels so every value is visible."

**Right:**
"Before the chart, who's looking at it, and what do you need them to do? If the CFO needs to see that one region is falling behind, that's a different chart than if the sales team needs to see who's biggest this month."

**What to do instead:** Ask who the audience is and what they should know or do, then name the comparison. The chart type comes third.

---

## 2. Listing options without choosing

**User:** "I want to show how our five teams compare on hours logged this quarter."

**Wrong:**
"You could use a bar chart, a pie chart, a treemap or a bubble chart. Each has its strengths. It depends on your preference."

**Right:**
"That's a comparison across categories, so I'd use a horizontal bar chart, sorted biggest first, with the axis starting at zero. I'd rule out a pie, because hours logged by five teams aren't really shares of one whole your audience cares about, and a treemap, because it makes them compare areas instead of lengths."

**What to do instead:** Pick one chart, say why, and say what you ruled out and why.

---

## 3. Banning the pie

**User:** "I need to show that 70% of our signups come from one channel."

**Wrong:**
"Never use a pie chart. Pie charts are always wrong; use a bar chart instead."

**Right:**
"This is part-to-whole with one share that matters, which is where a pie can work. Keep it to a few slices, color the 70% slice and leave the rest gray, label the slices directly, and write the takeaway next to it. A single stacked bar would also work if the pie would sit next to bar charts."

**What to do instead:** Judge the pie by the comparison. Use it for part-to-whole with few slices, and draw it well.

---

## 4. Cutting the bar axis to make a difference look bigger

**User:** "My bars are 92, 94 and 95. Can I start the axis at 90 so the difference shows?"

**Wrong:**
"Sure, starting at 90 will make the differences easier to see."

**Right:**
"Bars start at zero, because the length is the value. Starting at 90 makes 95 look three times as big as 92. If a three-point gap is the point, show it on a dot plot, where the axis doesn't need to start at zero, and say on the chart what the gap means."

**What to do instead:** Keep bar axes at zero. When a small difference matters, switch to a chart where a cut axis is honest.

---

## 5. Leaving a spaghetti graph alone

**User:** "Here's my chart: twelve product lines over three years, all in different colors."

**Wrong:**
"Looks good. Add a legend with all twelve colors so people can tell them apart."

**Right:**
"Twelve crossing lines is a spaghetti graph. Nobody will follow one strand. Two fixes: split them into small panels with the same y-axis range on each, or make every line gray and color only the one you're talking about, one at a time. Which line is the story?"

**What to do instead:** Count the series. With more than a handful of lines, use panels with the same y-axis range or highlight one line at a time.
