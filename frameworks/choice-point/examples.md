# Choice Point Examples

## PHASE 1: Introducing the Choice Point

### User in defensive mode

**User:** "Look, I already know about 'choice points.' I've read all the ACT books. I just need practical tools, not another lecture."

**Advisor:** "Fair enough - sounds like you've done your homework. So let's skip the theory and get straight into your material. What's a specific moment from the last day or two where you felt that familiar pull toward reacting? A time when you knew in your gut you were at a choice point but maybe didn't navigate it the way you wanted?"

> Honors their knowledge without being defensive, immediately redirects to application

### User overwhelmed

**User:** "There are so many situations. I'm constantly reactive. I don't even know where to start - everything feels like one big trigger."

**Advisor:** "That makes sense - when we're stressed, it can feel like one continuous below-the-line experience rather than distinct moments. Let's zoom in on just one. Think about the last 24 hours. Was there one specific moment - maybe lasting just a few minutes - where something happened and you felt that hook? Even a small one counts."

> Reduces scope from "everything" to "one specific recent moment" - creates containment

### User intellectualizing

**User:** "I find the dichotomy between toward and away moves theoretically interesting. It seems similar to approach-avoidance motivation in behavioral psychology."

**Advisor:** "It is related - there's good research connecting these ideas. But ACT is fundamentally experiential, not theoretical. So let's get out of our heads and into lived experience. When was the last time you felt genuinely hooked - not thinking about hooks, but actually experiencing one? Something where you noticed that pull in your body, not just your mind?"

> Acknowledges intellectual observation, then pivots firmly to embodied experience

### User open and ready

**User:** "I had a moment yesterday with my business partner. He questioned a decision I'd made and I immediately got defensive."

**Advisor:** "Perfect example. That defensive surge when your judgment gets questioned - that's exactly the kind of hook we're talking about. Let's work with that. Before we explore the details, just take a moment to notice - even recalling it now, can you feel any of that activation coming back?"

> Affirms the example, begins deepening into the experience immediately

## PHASE 2: Identifying What's Hooking You

### User staying surface-level

**User:** "I guess I was just stressed about work."

**Advisor:** "Stressed is a start, but let's go deeper. In that specific moment with your partner, what thought went through your mind? Was it something like 'He doesn't trust me' or 'I'm going to look incompetent' or something else? And where did you feel that in your body - chest, stomach, throat?"

> Pushes past generic label to specific thoughts and body sensations

### User aware but judgmental

**User:** "I was being totally irrational. I know I overreacted. The thought was 'He's undermining me' which is ridiculous."

**Advisor:** "Hold on - let's take off the judgment for a moment. That thought isn't 'ridiculous' - it's information. Your mind produced 'He's undermining me' for a reason. The goal isn't to evaluate whether the thought was good or bad. Just notice it was there. Can you also notice what feeling came with that thought? Fear? Anger? Shame?"

> Removes self-judgment frame, returns to neutral observation of the hook

## PHASE 3: Mapping Away Moves

### User in denial

**User:** "I didn't really do any away moves. I handled it pretty well, I think."

**Advisor:** "Maybe you did handle some of it well. But let me ask more specifically: After that thought 'He's undermining me' showed up - did you get a little colder? More clipped in your responses? Did you find yourself building a case in your head for why you were right? Those are all subtle away moves. They don't have to be dramatic to count."

> Names specific subtle away moves that the user may have missed

### User overly harsh on themselves

**User:** "I completely shut down, attacked him, ruined the meeting, probably damaged the relationship permanently. Classic me."

**Advisor:** "Whoa - let's separate the away moves from the story you're telling about yourself. 'Classic me' and 'ruined permanently' - those are judgments on top of judgments. Just the facts: what did you actually do? Got quiet? Said something sharp? Left the room? Let's name the behaviors without the self-attack layer."

> Distinguishes actual behavior from catastrophizing narrative

## PHASE 4: Connecting to Values

### User struggling to name values

**User:** "I don't know... I guess I just wanted it to go well?"

**Advisor:** "Let's come at this differently. Forget abstract values for a moment. When you imagine yourself handling that moment exactly the way you'd be proud of - what does that version of you look like? What quality is that person bringing to the conversation? Patience? Honesty? Curiosity? What word comes up?"

> Shifts from abstract values to concrete visualization of ideal self

### User naming surface-level wants

**User:** "I just wanted him to respect my decision and back off."

**Advisor:** "That's what you wanted from HIM. I'm asking about you - who did YOU want to be in that moment? Set aside what he should or shouldn't do. If you could rewind and show up as your best self, what quality would be most important for you to embody? What's the value underneath wanting respect?"

> Redirects from wanting to control others to internal values

## PHASE 5: Identifying Toward Moves

### User going too big

**User:** "I should have just stayed completely calm and said everything perfectly."

**Advisor:** "That's a pretty high bar - and honestly, it's more about not feeling uncomfortable than about moving toward your values. A toward move can be small and still feel hard. What if the toward move was just to pause for three seconds before responding? Or to ask one genuine question instead of defending? What's a version that's actually doable while still feeling the discomfort?"

> Brings toward move back to realistic scale

### User confusing avoidance with toward moves

**User:** "I should have just let it go and not said anything."

**Advisor:** "Let me check something - would that be a toward move or just a different away move? Sometimes 'let it go' means acceptance and moving on. But sometimes it means suppressing what's true to avoid conflict. Which one would that have been for you? If you valued honesty, does staying silent actually move you toward that?"

> Helps distinguish genuine toward moves from disguised avoidance

## PHASE 6: Planning for the Next Choice Point

### User pessimistic about change

**User:** "I've tried to change before. It never sticks."

**Advisor:** "I hear that - and you're right that insight alone doesn't change behavior. That's why we're not stopping at insight. We're creating a plan. Specifically: when that hook shows up again - and it will - what's the one thing you'll try? Not a complete transformation, just one small toward move. What could you actually commit to attempting?"

> Acknowledges difficulty while redirecting to concrete commitment

### User overcommitting

**User:** "I'm going to completely transform how I handle criticism. I'll always pause, always stay curious, always respond thoughtfully."

**Advisor:** "I love the intention, but 'always' is a setup for failure. Let's make it smaller and more likely to succeed. Pick ONE of those - the pause, the curiosity, or the thoughtful response. Which one feels most doable as an experiment for the next week? We're building a new pattern, not achieving perfection."

> Constrains to single actionable commitment
