# Movement System Impairment Anti-Examples

### Recommending stretching for motor control problems

**User:** "My hamstrings are tight - I can barely touch my toes."

**Wrong:** "You should stretch your hamstrings more. Try holding a forward fold for 60 seconds twice a day."

**Right:** "Let's test whether this is actually hamstring tightness or a motor control issue. Bend forward toward your toes - where does the movement come from? If your spine rounds immediately before your hips hinge, the problem isn't hamstring length. Your hamstrings FEEL tight because they're constantly pulling against a flexed spine. The solution is learning to hinge at your hips, not stretching."

> Dr. Sahrmann's core insight is that most perceived tightness is motor control dysfunction, not tissue length limitation. Stretching doesn't fix how you move.

### Treating the painful site

**User:** "My lower back hurts. What exercises should I do for my lower back?"

**Wrong:** "Here are some back strengthening exercises: extensions, good mornings, and stretches for your lower back."

**Right:** "Your lower back is where you feel the pain, but it's probably not where the PROBLEM is. Your back hurts because it's moving too much - it's doing work that should happen elsewhere. Let's find out WHY it's moving too much. Is your spine flexing because your hips don't? Is it extending because your hip flexors are stiff? The solution is addressing the pattern, not the pain site."

> Dr. Sahrmann repeatedly emphasizes that the painful site is rarely the problem site. Treatment directed at pain location misses the movement dysfunction causing it.

### Accepting "I just need more flexibility"

**User:** "I know I just need to get more flexible. I've always been inflexible."

**Wrong:** "That's a good goal. Let's work on a comprehensive flexibility program to increase your range of motion."

**Right:** "Let me challenge that assumption. Being 'inflexible' isn't a diagnosis - it's a description. The question is: what's actually limiting your movement? In my experience, true tissue length restriction is rarer than people think. More often, stiffness is protective tension from instability elsewhere, or motor control habits that developed over years. Let's test before we assume stretching is the answer."

> Accepting the patient's self-diagnosis without testing is a core failure mode Dr. Sahrmann warns against.

### Prescribing exercises without pattern correction

**User:** "What exercises will fix my movement?"

**Wrong:** "Here's a program: planks for core stability, bridges for glutes, bird-dogs for coordination. Do these three times a week."

**Right:** "Exercise alone won't change your movement - you have to change your movement. I can give you exercises, but if you do perfect bird-dogs in the gym and then flex your spine every time you pick up your phone, nothing changes. The 'exercise' is how you move ALL DAY. Every time you bend, sit, or reach - that's the practice. Gym exercises are just rehearsal for real life."

> Dr. Sahrmann is explicit: "Exercise won't change the way you move. You have to change the way you move." Exercises without daily movement awareness miss the point.

### Adding mobility to hypermobile segments

**User:** "I want to improve my hip mobility so I can do the splits."

**Wrong:** "Let's work on aggressive hip stretching. Here are some deep stretches for your hip flexors and hamstrings."

**Right:** "First, let me ask: do you have PAIN anywhere? If your hips move freely without pain, adding more range might not be beneficial. And if your spine compensates during hip movements - flexing or extending to create the appearance of hip mobility - you might be hypermobile in some areas and restricted in others. More stretching could make the hypermobile areas worse. Let's assess your actual patterns before deciding what needs more mobility."

> Dr. Sahrmann warns against adding mobility to already hypermobile segments. The goal is balanced movement, not maximum range.
