# Stage the Picture — Anti-Examples

Failure modes to avoid, each with the input that triggers it, the wrong response, the right response, and what to do instead.

---

## 1. Writing the description in one shot

**User:** "Here's my friend's memory. Turn it into a scene description for the image model."

**Wrong:**
"Here you go: A cozy kitchen at night, two friends sharing hot chocolate by a camping stove, warm light, laughter, a feeling of deep friendship."

**Right:**
"Before we write a word of it, let's pick the picture. I see three moments in this story: her arriving at the door, her stirring the saucepan, and her handing you the mug. Which single instant would you paint?"

**What to do instead:** Start at Phase 1 and stop at the WAIT. The moment, the light, and the staging are decisions, and the person who knows the story makes them with you.

---

## 2. Mood words in place of light

**User:** "It was a really warm, loving night."

**Wrong:**
"Great, I'll describe it with warm, soft, loving lighting and a nostalgic glow."

**Right:**
"Warm how? Where's the light coming from: a lamp, the stove flame, a window? Which side is it on, and is it a small sharp source or a big soft one? The feeling comes through if we get the light right."

**What to do instead:** Turn every mood word into a source, a direction, a quality (hard or soft), and a color. An image model can paint a blue flame lighting hands from below. It can't paint "loving."

---

## 3. Inventing things the memory doesn't contain

**User:** "It was in her kitchen."

**Wrong:**
"I'll add a vase of sunflowers on the table, fairy lights in the window, and a cat curled by the stove to make it feel homey."

**Right:**
"The story doesn't mention anything on the table or in the window, so let's leave them in the shadow. If there's something you remember being there, tell me and it goes in."

**What to do instead:** Keep to the story's facts. Guess only at what the picture needs and the story leaves open (time of day, weather, where the light sits), label each guess, and get the user's yes.

---

## 4. Showing the whole afternoon

**User:** "Can the picture show the hike, the picnic, and the sunset?"

**Wrong:**
"Sure: in the foreground they're hiking, in the middle they've spread a blanket for lunch, and in the background the sun is setting."

**Right:**
"A single painting presents only one moment. If we show all three, the viewer gets a summary instead of being there. Which one would you keep if you could only keep one? The others can be other pictures."

**What to do instead:** One moment per picture, the one that holds the most. Offer the others as separate pictures.

---

## 5. Everything at the same volume

**User:** "I want you to be able to see her face, the view, the dog, the car, and the sign."

**Wrong:**
"Here's a description with all five clearly visible and evenly lit."

**Right:**
"Then nothing will stand out. Which one should the eye land on first? Let the light fall there, and let the rest become simple shapes in shadow. A simple tonal design reads from across the room."

**What to do instead:** Choose one center of interest, put the light and the strongest contrast there, and mass everything else into light or shadow.
