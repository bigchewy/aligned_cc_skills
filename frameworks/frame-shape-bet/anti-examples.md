# Frame, Shape, Bet — Anti-Examples

Failure modes to avoid, each with the input that triggers it, the wrong response, the right response, and what to do instead.

---

## 1. Scheduling the whole list in one reply

**User:** "Here's our feature list. Can you turn it into a roadmap for the next six months?"

**Wrong:**
"Sure! Q1: sign-in, signup, billing. Q2: reporting, mobile app, waitlist. Here's a Gantt chart."

**Right:**
"Before we look at the list, what dates can't move, and what has to be true on each one? Who builds, and how much of their time goes to project work? And what size are your bets?"

**What to do instead:** Start at Phase 1 and stop at the WAIT. The calendar and the project time come first. A roadmap built from a list with no appetites is a stack of estimates.

---

## 2. Accepting an estimate as an appetite

**User:** "Reporting dashboard, about eight weeks."

**Wrong:**
"Got it, eight weeks. I'll put it in Q2."

**Right:**
"Eight weeks is how long the version in your head takes. How much is it worth? If you had three weeks, is there a version that would still matter?"

**What to do instead:** Turn every estimate into an appetite by asking what the work is worth. Appetites start with a number and end with a design.

---

## 3. Moving the date to fit the work

**User:** "The show is March 10 but we won't have signup ready. Let's plan for April."

**Wrong:**
"Makes sense. I'll move signup to April and adjust the rest."

**Right:**
"Is March 10 fixed? If the booth is booked, it is. Then the scope moves, not the date. What's the smallest version of signup a clinic could use at the booth?"

**What to do instead:** Hold the fixed date and move the scope. If the date really can move, go back and relabel it in Phase 1.

---

## 4. Letting a promise go

**User:** "Google sign-in is a lot of work. Can we cut it?"

**Wrong:**
"If it doesn't fit, let it go. Important ideas come back."

**Right:**
"It's in the pilot contract, so it's a promise, not an idea. It stays on the calendar. What's the smallest version that keeps the promise by January 15?"

**What to do instead:** "Let it go" is for candidates, not for promises made to people outside the company. Frame the smallest version that keeps the promise.

---

## 5. Shaping everything on the roadmap

**User:** "Let's shape all six problem areas now so we know exactly what we're building."

**Wrong:**
Writing elements, rabbit holes, and no-gos for all six.

**Right:**
"Only the first stretch gets shaped now. By the time the others come up, you'll know things you don't know today. Shaping them now would be guessing. They stay framed."

**What to do instead:** Shape only the next bet. Later stretches hold framed problem areas with appetites.

---

## 6. Leaving a rabbit hole open

**User:** "We'll sort out the edge cases during the build."

**Wrong:**
"Sounds good. Moving on to the bet."

**Right:**
"Which edge cases? Name them. Each one gets solved now or written down as a no-go. An open rabbit hole is a time bomb that can churn the whole bet."

**What to do instead:** Every rabbit hole gets a decision during shaping.

---

## 7. Agile vocabulary and backlog grooming

**User:** "Should we put the rest in the backlog and groom it every sprint?"

**Wrong:**
"Yes, let's estimate story points and prioritize the backlog by velocity."

**Right:**
"No backlog. The candidates we didn't frame are let go, and they're listed on the page so you can see them. If one matters, you'll hear about it again, and we'll frame it then."

**What to do instead:** Use the book's terms. Let unframed candidates go and list them. Don't keep a pile that makes the team feel behind.
