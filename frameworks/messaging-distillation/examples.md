# Messaging Distillation Examples

*Running example: CareSync, an AI care coordination platform for health systems. Replaces care managers working from EHR task lists, email chains, and spreadsheets.*

---

## PHASE 1: Anchor in positioning

### User shares thin positioning

**User:** "We help health systems improve care coordination. Our target is hospital systems with more than 500 beds. We have a really sophisticated risk-scoring engine."

**Advisor:** "That gives me the size filter and a capability, but not the positioning. Let me ask the five questions directly. What do care managers at these hospitals do today when they don't have CareSync? Literally — what are the tools and workflows you're replacing? That's the competitive alternative, and it's the foundation everything else rests on."

> Redirects from demographic filters to the five-component logic; targets competitive alternatives as the correct starting point

### User shares complete five-component positioning

**User:** "Okay. The alternatives are EHR task lists, Excel trackers, and a lot of email. What we do that those can't: predictive risk scoring across the full patient record, automatic care-gap alerts before a patient falls through the cracks, and workflow routing that gets the right coordinator on the right patient. The value is fewer readmissions — our customers have cut 30-day readmissions by 18% in year one. Target is health system CMOs and VP of Care Management. Category — we've been calling ourselves a care coordination platform."

**Advisor:** "Good. Here's how I read your positioning: you replace the Excel-email-task-list combination that care managers are using today. You do three things those alternatives can't: predict which patients are high risk before something goes wrong, surface care gaps automatically, and route work to the right person. The business outcome is measurable — 18% fewer readmissions. The buyers are CMOs and VP Care Management at large health systems. The category you're currently using is 'care coordination platform.' That last one is what we're going to stress-test in PHASE 2, because it may be doing less work than you need. Does this match how you'd describe it?"

> Paraphrases five-component chain accurately and flags the category as the live question; validates the rest without inflating it

---

## PHASE 2: Category name string

### User picks a category name that commoditizes them

**User:** "We've been going back and forth between 'care coordination platform' and 'care management software.' Those are the terms health systems already use."

**Advisor:** "'Care coordination platform' and 'care management software' — both are terms the market recognizes, which is exactly why they're risky. Epic, Salesforce Health Cloud, and a dozen point solutions would describe themselves the same way. The category name has to make your differentiated value obvious before you've explained anything. Neither of these phrases signals 'predictive risk scoring' or 'automatic care gaps' — a buyer would hear them and assume you're another EHR module or task manager. What does the category name need to communicate about your unique mechanism?"

> Challenges category names that create competitive confusion rather than differentiation; names the mechanism that the category should surface

### User finds a sharper category frame

**User:** "If the mechanism is predictive — catching patients before they fall through the cracks — maybe the category is something like 'predictive care coordination'?"

**Advisor:** "'Predictive care coordination' — let me stress-test it. Does a CMO hearing that phrase immediately understand that you're different from the EHR task list? I think yes, because 'predictive' signals the system acts before the gap, not after. Does it put you in competition with Epic Healthy Planet? Less so — they're reactive workflow tools, not predictive ones. Could a direct competitor use the same phrase? They could claim it, but if you've built the category and own the proof, the phrase sticks to you. My recommendation: 'predictive care coordination platform.' The 'platform' is earned because you're doing risk scoring, alerting, and routing — not just one of those. Do you want to defend 'care management software' or does 'predictive care coordination platform' feel right?"

> Validates a category candidate against Dunford's three criteria; makes the recommendation with a clear rationale

---

## PHASE 3: Tagline

### User submits taglines that describe features

**User:** "Our current tagline is 'Smarter care coordination through AI.' We've also tried 'AI-powered care management for health systems.'"

**Advisor:** "'Smarter care coordination through AI' has a structural problem: 'smarter' is a comparative that assumes the reader already knows what baseline they're comparing against. 'AI-powered care management' is a feature label — it tells me the mechanism but not the outcome. Neither of these would make a VP Care Management feel anything. The tagline's job is to signal the category and imply the differentiated value in language a human would actually say. What outcome do your best customers feel most strongly — the one that made them buy you, not the one that made them interested?"

> Rejects feature-label taglines and focuses the user on emotional/outcome-level framing

### User finds a tagline grounded in outcome

**User:** "The thing they feel is relief — they stop worrying about who they're missing. The readmissions reduction is the proof, but the emotion is 'I'm not going to get a call on Friday about a patient who fell through the cracks.'"

**Advisor:** "That's the insight the tagline needs to carry. Here are three variants:

**Homepage hero:** *Catch every patient before the gap.*
*Why it works:* Signals the predictive mechanism and the care coordination category without naming either; the word 'catch' carries the urgency that 'coordination platform' never would.

**Deck cover:** *The care coordination platform that sees gaps before they become readmissions.*
*Why it works:* More explicit about the outcome for an audience that needs to justify the purchase internally — this is the sentence a VP Care Management emails to the CFO.

**Email signature:** *Predictive care coordination.*
*Why it works:* Owns the category name directly; works without context; repeats the phrase you want analysts and buyers to associate with you.

Which direction feels closest?"

> Produces three variants with positioning rationale; uses the emotional truth the user surfaced to anchor the copy

## PHASE 4: Elevator pitch variants (per audience)

### User conflates audiences into one generic pitch

**User:** "Our pitch is: 'CareSync is an AI care coordination platform that helps health systems reduce readmissions and improve patient outcomes.' We use that for everyone."

**Advisor:** "That pitch works for no one specifically. A CMO doesn't feel the same thing a VP Care Management feels, and neither of them feels what a care manager feels on a Tuesday afternoon. The CMO is accountable for readmission rates and CMS penalties. The VP Care Management is accountable for coordinator capacity and workflow chaos. The care manager is drowning in a task list that doesn't tell her which patient matters most right now. One pitch that addresses all three addresses none of them. Which of these audiences does your sales team talk to first — who's the economic buyer and who's the champion?"

> Identifies the one-pitch failure mode; separates economic buyer from champion; forces audience-specific framing

### Advisor surfaces the felt problem per audience

**Advisor:** "Here are the elevator pitches by audience:

**CMO:** 'Every health system is measured on readmission rates — and most care management programs can't tell you which patients are at risk until after they've been readmitted. CareSync scores every patient's risk across the full EHR, surfaces care gaps automatically, and routes each one to the right coordinator before the 30-day window closes. Health systems using CareSync have cut 30-day readmissions by 18% in year one.'

**VP Care Management:** 'Care coordinators are spending half their time triaging — figuring out who to call next from a task list that doesn't prioritize itself. CareSync replaces that triage work with automatic routing: each morning, every coordinator has a ranked list of patients who need attention today, with the clinical context already pulled. Coordinator capacity goes to patient contact, not to figuring out who deserves it.'

**Care Manager (champion):** 'CareSync shows you, every morning, exactly which of your patients is at risk of falling through the cracks — before they do. No more scanning EHR task lists. No more worrying about who you might have missed.'

Flag any pitch where the language feels off for that audience."

> Produces three audience-specific pitches anchored to felt problems, not product features; uses outcome language the user validated

---

## PHASE 5: Value-prop phrasings

### User writes attribute-first, not outcome-first

**User:** "Our value props are: predictive risk scoring, automated care gap detection, intelligent workflow routing, and EHR integration."

**Advisor:** "Those are attributes — capabilities your product has. They're not value props until they answer the customer's question: 'So what does that mean for me?' Predictive risk scoring means... what for a health system? Automated care gap detection produces... which business outcome? Walk me through the 'so what' for each one. I'll translate."

> Names the attribute-vs-value confusion; runs the 'so what' translation in real time

### User lands customer-language value claims

**User:** "Predictive risk scoring means you know who's going to be readmitted before they are, so you can intervene. Automated care gap detection means coordinators don't miss patients — the system catches the ones who fell off the radar. Intelligent routing means coordinators stop wasting time triaging and start spending it on patients."

**Advisor:** "Now those are value claims. Let me structure them:

**Attribute:** Predictive risk scoring
**Value claim:** Intervene with high-risk patients before the 30-day readmission window closes — not after.
**Evidence anchor:** Customers have reduced 30-day readmissions by 18% in year one.

**Attribute:** Automated care gap detection
**Value claim:** No patient falls off the radar. The system flags every gap; coordinators follow up.
**Evidence anchor:** [Placeholder — pull the number of care gaps surfaced per 1,000 patients from customer data.]

**Attribute:** Intelligent workflow routing
**Value claim:** Coordinators spend their time on patient contact, not on triaging a task list.
**Evidence anchor:** [Placeholder — pull coordinator time-on-admin before/after from onboarding surveys.]

The evidence anchors for the second and third are placeholders. Before these go on the website, fill them from your customer data."

> Structures the three-part value prop correctly; flags where evidence anchors are thin and need proof points

## PHASE 6: Value-prop-to-persona map

### User discovers a value prop with no primary audience

**User:** "The EHR integration one — I don't know who that leads with. IT cares, but IT doesn't buy."

**Advisor:** "That tells you something important: EHR integration is a technical objection-handler, not a lead value prop. It's the answer to 'will this work with our Epic environment?' — not the reason anyone buys. In the map, it shows up as 'supporting' for IT Director and maybe as implied context for VP Care Management ('we pull directly from your EHR — no manual data entry'). If it shows up as a lead for no persona, it belongs in an FAQ or a technical brief, not on the homepage. Here's the map:

| Persona | Predictive risk scoring | Automated care gap detection | Intelligent workflow routing |
|---------|-------------------------|------------------------------|------------------------------|
| CMO | Lead | Lead | Supporting |
| VP Care Management | Supporting | Lead | Lead |
| Care Manager | — | Supporting | Lead |

Review each row: does this match how your reps open conversations with each audience?"

> Catches the 'no-primary-audience' failure for a feature value prop; produces the table and frames its usage

---

## PHASE 7: Key phrases & rhetorical hooks

### User selects phrases they haven't earned yet

**User:** "We want to own 'AI care coordination' and 'the OS for care management' and 'eliminate readmissions.'"

**Advisor:** "'AI care coordination' is earnable — if you use it consistently and build the category, it attaches to you. 'The OS for care management' is a metaphor that requires the market to accept it, and right now you're not dominant enough for 'OS' to feel accurate rather than aspirational. 'Eliminate readmissions' is a claim you can't make — your evidence says reduce by 18%, which is strong; 'eliminate' sets an expectation you'll miss in every sales call. Start with phrases you've already earned or can earn in the next 12 months."

> Challenges overreach claims and metaphors the company hasn't yet made credible; ties phrase choice to current evidence

### User identifies phrases competitors have poisoned

**User:** "We've stopped saying 'population health' because every vendor says it and nobody knows what it means anymore. Also avoiding 'care gaps' as a noun because it sounds clinical."

**Advisor:** "'Population health' is exactly right to avoid — it's category jargon that signals nothing and everything simultaneously. 'Care gaps' as a noun is a useful signal to remove from headlines while keeping it in technical documentation where it's precise. Add both to the 'do not use' list with the reason: 'population health' for category fatigue, 'care gaps' (standalone) for clinical register that distances non-clinical buyers. Your 'own' list: 'predictive care coordination,' 'catch every patient before the gap,' 'the readmission window,' 'patient-by-patient risk,' 'coordinator capacity.' Each of these is either unique to your mechanism or ties directly to a phrase your buyers already use when describing their problem."

> Validates the user's existing language discipline; produces owned phrases tied to positioning logic and a 'do not use' list with rationale

---

## PHASE 8: Pricing language

### User opts out of pricing language

**User:** "We're fully sales-negotiated. No public pricing, no per-seat model we want to talk about yet."

**Advisor:** "Marking this as a placeholder. That's correct for your stage — anchoring a pricing frame before you've standardized packaging creates more problems than it solves. One flag: if prospects are asking 'how do you charge?' in early calls, your reps need a posture, even if it's not public. A simple answer is 'we price based on the size of the patient population you're coordinating' — that frames value before negotiation begins and avoids the 'per-seat' frame you'll want to avoid as you grow. Add this to the placeholder note so it's ready when packaging stabilizes."

> Accepts the opt-out without judgment; surfaces the rep-posture gap that still needs an answer even without public pricing

---

## PHASE 9: File assembly

### Standalone mode hand-off

**Advisor:** "The messaging file is written to `brand/language/messaging.md`. Review it and flag any value claims where the evidence anchor is weaker than the claim warrants — specifically the care-gap detection and workflow routing value props, which I marked as placeholders. Those are the claims most likely to cause friction in a sales call if a prospect asks for proof and you have to say 'we'll follow up.'"

**User:** "The readmission stat is solid. The coordinator time-savings one — we have the number from onboarding surveys but haven't published it. Can we still use it?"

**Advisor:** "Use it internally — in sales decks and training. Don't put it on the website until you've validated it across multiple customers and it's been through legal. 'Our customers report X% reduction in coordinator admin time' is the formulation that's honest about the source without overstating it. The evidence anchor in the messaging file should read: 'Customer onboarding surveys — to be validated at scale before public use.' That creates the pressure to close the evidence gap rather than paper over it."

> Models the standalone file delivery and the post-assembly conversation about evidence quality; treats evidence gaps as a process issue, not a messaging problem
