# Messaging Distillation Anti-Examples

*Running example: CareSync, an AI care coordination platform for health systems. Replaces care managers working from EHR task lists, email chains, and spreadsheets.*

---

### Anti-Example 1: Re-doing positioning under the messaging label

**User:** "I want to distill our messaging. We're targeting health systems, focused on care coordination. Here's our current tagline: 'Better care, better outcomes.'"

**Advisor (wrong):** "Before we touch messaging, let's make sure your positioning is solid. Who is your target customer exactly? What's the market you're in? What's the unique value you provide? What are your proof points? And how do you want to categorize your product? Let's build that out first."

> This is re-running the positioning framework (Components 1–5) inside a messaging session. The positioning conversation is already done — the messaging-distillation framework exists to work *from* positioning, not to relitigate it. The correct move at PHASE 1 is to surface the existing five-component positioning, verify it with the user, and move on. If positioning is genuinely missing, flag it explicitly and end the session — don't silently reconduct the positioning exercise under a different label.

---

### Anti-Example 2: Tagline as feature list

**User:** "Here's a tagline we've been using: 'AI-powered care management for health systems.'"

**Advisor (wrong):** "That works well. It clearly communicates what you do — AI-powered, care management, and who it's for. Let's keep it as the primary tagline and build the elevator pitches from there."

> "AI-powered care management" names the technology and the category but signals zero differentiation. Every competitor in the space could use this phrase. A tagline's job is to imply the differentiated value in language a buyer would actually feel. The advisor should have named the structural failure — mechanism label, not outcome — and pushed the user toward what outcome their best customers feel most strongly. Accepting a feature-label tagline skips the hardest part of PHASE 3 and sends a generic phrase into every downstream artifact.

---

### Anti-Example 3: Single elevator pitch for all audiences

**User:** "Can you give us one universal elevator pitch we can use across sales, marketing, and the website?"

**Advisor (wrong):** "Of course. Here's a universal pitch: 'CareSync is the AI care coordination platform that helps health systems reduce readmissions, improve patient outcomes, and empower care coordinators to do their best work.' This works for executives, clinical champions, and frontline users."

> A pitch that addresses everyone addresses no one. The CMO is accountable for readmission rates and CMS penalties. The VP Care Management is accountable for coordinator capacity. The frontline care manager is trying to figure out which patient to call next. One sentence cannot carry all three felt problems simultaneously. The advisor should have asked which audiences are in scope, then produced separate pitches anchored to each audience's problem — even if the user asked for a single output. Producing a universal pitch is a delivery shortcut that degrades every use case it's applied to.

---

### Anti-Example 4: Value-prop-to-persona map missing when personas are incomplete

**User:** "We don't have formal personas documented yet. Can we skip the value-prop map?"

**Advisor (wrong):** "No problem — if your personas aren't formalized, we'll skip PHASE 6 and revisit it once you've built out your persona documentation. Let's move to key phrases."

> Skipping PHASE 6 because personas aren't documented leaves the most important output of the session missing. The value-prop-to-persona map is precisely the artifact that exposes which value propositions have no primary audience — a structural flaw that will propagate into every sales deck and website section. The correct move: produce the map using the audiences already implied by the positioning (economic buyer, champion, user) and mark it `confidence: low — validate against persona documentation when available`. An incomplete map with known confidence is more useful than no map.

---

### Anti-Example 5: Confusing pricing language with pricing

**User:** "We charge $120,000 per year for health systems up to 1,000 beds, and $180,000 for larger systems."

**Advisor (wrong):** "Got it. I'll document that pricing in the messaging file: '$120K/year for health systems under 1,000 beds, $180K/year for larger systems.' That way the sales team has the numbers in one place."

> PHASE 8 is about *how the organization talks about price* — the frame, the value anchor, the posture reps use when prospects ask "how do you charge?" — not a record of the actual contract numbers. Recording dollar figures in the messaging file conflates a pricing conversation with a pricing language conversation and creates a document that will be wrong the moment pricing changes. The correct output is the posture: "We price based on the size of the patient population you're coordinating" — a frame that signals value orientation before negotiation begins, without committing to a number the messaging file shouldn't hold.
