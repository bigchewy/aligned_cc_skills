# Leopard Movement Hierarchy Examples

## Phase 1: Identify the Movement

### User learning a new lift

**User:** "I want to work on my overhead press. I can do it but it doesn't feel right."

**Advisor:** "Let's apply the hierarchy to your press. 'Doesn't feel right' is your body telling you something's off in the chain. We'll start with position - can you actually get into a proper overhead position? - then test whether it holds when you add movement and load. Might be a position issue disguising itself as a pressing problem."

> Frames the investigation through the hierarchy lens

### User returning from injury

**User:** "I'm coming back from a knee injury and want to get back to squatting."

**Advisor:** "Perfect situation for the hierarchy. After injury, you've got to re-earn every level. Position first - can you hold a squat position without the knee complaining? Then movement - can you move through the range with control? Speed and load come last. Your knee will tell us exactly where your current ceiling is."

> Applies hierarchy specifically to post-injury context

### User with a sport-specific movement

**User:** "I'm a pitcher and my throwing mechanics feel off."

**Advisor:** "Throwing is the ultimate speed movement - but the hierarchy still applies. Before we look at your throw, let's check your positions. Can you get into and hold the key checkpoints - the cocking position, the arm slot, the follow-through? If any of those positions are compromised, your body is finding workarounds at high speed. That's how arms get hurt."

> Applies hierarchy to high-velocity sport skill

## Phase 2: Test Position (Level 1)

### User reports position feels solid

**User:** "I got into the bottom squat position. I can hold it and breathe fine. Feels pretty good."

**Advisor:** "Good start. Now let's stress-test it. Close your eyes and hold it - still stable? Shift your weight slightly forward, then back - do you have control? Turn your head side to side - does anything collapse? Position isn't just 'can you get there' - it's 'do you own it from every angle?' What happens when you add those challenges?"

> Adds progressive position tests beyond the basic hold

### User can't achieve the position

**User:** "When I try to get into the start position for deadlift, my lower back rounds before I even touch the bar."

**Advisor:** "There's your answer. You can't own a movement you can't get into. The hierarchy says we don't add movement until you own position - and right now, you don't own the hip hinge. Could be hip mobility, could be hamstring length, could be thoracic stiffness making your lumbar compensate. Let's figure out what's blocking the position. Can you hip hinge with your hands on a box above knee height?"

> Identifies position failure and begins diagnosing the cause

### User has partial position

**User:** "I can get into the overhead position but I have to arch my back a lot to get my arms straight."

**Advisor:** "That's a compensation, not a position. Your back is doing the job your shoulders can't. Here's the test: flatten your back against a wall. Now try to get your arms overhead without letting your back leave the wall. How far can you get? That's your true shoulder range. The arch is your nervous system finding a workaround - and workarounds under load get expensive."

> Distinguishes true position from compensated position

## Phase 3: Test Movement (Level 2)

### User passes movement test

**User:** "I did 5 slow reps. Pretty consistent throughout, no sticking points I noticed."

**Advisor:** "Good movement quality. You own the range at controlled tempo. Now the real question: can you stop anywhere in that range and hold for 3 seconds? Pick a random point mid-rep on the next one. If you can stop and hold without falling forward, back, or collapsing, you truly own that movement pattern."

> Adds pause test to verify movement mastery

### User identifies compensation

**User:** "On the way down my knees cave in around the midpoint, then straighten out at the bottom."

**Advisor:** "You just described the hierarchy in action. You own the top position and the bottom position - but you don't own the transition. That knee valgus at the midpoint is your body finding the path of least resistance. The question is why: are your hip external rotators weak, or are they locked up? Let's test - in that midpoint position, can you consciously push your knees out and hold?"

> Uses movement breakdown to diagnose specific limitation

### User reports form degrades with reps

**User:** "First few reps look good but by rep 4 or 5 I feel myself rushing through the middle part."

**Advisor:** "Your body is telling you exactly where it gets tired. That 'rushing through' is avoiding the position you can't sustain. You own 3-4 reps of quality movement - that's your current capacity. The training protocol is clear: work at 3 quality reps, rest, repeat. Build density at quality before building to 5 reps."

> Identifies fatigue limit within movement test

## Phase 4: Test Speed (Level 3)

### User maintains form under speed

**User:** "I did 10 regular-speed reps and filmed myself. Form looks the same throughout."

**Advisor:** "Now you've got data. You own the movement at speed through 10 reps. Let's find your actual edge - where does it start to break? Try 15 reps. Or add 10-20% more load. Or do a set, rest 30 seconds, do another set. Somewhere there's a failure point - that's what we're hunting. The goal isn't to fail; it's to know where failure lives so you can train right below it."

> Pushes to find actual capacity limit

### User breaks down under load

**User:** "Bodyweight was fine but when I added weight, my back rounded coming out of the hole."

**Advisor:** "Load revealed your true position capacity. You have the movement pattern but not the positional strength to maintain it under external resistance. That's actually good news - the pattern is there, we just need to build strength at that position. Pause squats with lighter load, tempo work where you hold the bottom. Build the strength to maintain what you already know how to do."

> Distinguishes pattern mastery from positional strength

### User breaks down under fatigue

**User:** "First 5 reps were solid. By rep 8 my knees were caving and I was pitched forward."

**Advisor:** "There's your honest limit - 5-6 quality reps. Reps 7-8 were compensated movement, and compensated movement under load is how you get hurt. Your training zone is clear: sets of 5 with full quality. When 5 feels easy, test 6. Build capacity at quality, not by grinding through bad reps. The body learns what you practice - practice good movement."

> Sets clear training parameters based on fatigue threshold

## Phase 5: Create the Training Protocol

### Position-limited user

**User:** "So I need to work on the bottom squat position before I add load?"

**Advisor:** "Exactly. Here's your protocol: spend 2 minutes daily accumulating time in the bottom squat position. Use a doorframe or pole for balance at first. Work up to holding it unsupported while breathing normally. Add goblet holds with light weight to build positional strength. When you can hang out in the bottom for 2 minutes without thinking about it, you own the position. Then we test movement."

> Provides specific position-building protocol

### Movement-limited user

**User:** "I break down at the middle of the press. What do I do?"

**Advisor:** "That sticking point is your training target. Here's the protocol: pause presses where you hold at the sticking point for 3 seconds. Light load, but perfect position. Also practice the position without the movement - hold an empty bar or light weight at that exact height. Build familiarity and strength at the specific point of failure. When you can pause there without position loss, the movement quality will follow."

> Targets specific movement limitation with position holds

### Speed-limited user

**User:** "I've got the movement but I fall apart past 8 reps or when I add much weight."

**Advisor:** "You own position and movement - now we're building capacity. Protocol: work in the 5-6 rep range where quality is guaranteed. Build volume there - multiple sets, more sessions per week. When 5 reps at a weight feels automatic, add one rep. When 8 reps at that weight feels automatic, add 5-10% and drop back to 5 reps. Slow, patient progress. Quality is the governor."

> Provides progressive overload within quality constraints
