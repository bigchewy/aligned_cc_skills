# Boil It Down — Examples

One running example, made up to show the moves. A founder is building Spoonful, an app where a family saves its recipes. People photograph a handwritten recipe card, the app types it up, and it keeps the photo of the original card next to the text. The mark has to work as an app icon, a favicon, and on a printed family cookbook the app can make.

---

## Phase 1: What It Is and Where It Lives

### Golden path

**User:** "Families save recipes, mostly from a grandparent's handwritten cards. Name is Spoonful, one word. It goes on the app icon, the site favicon, and the cover of a printed cookbook. Colors are a cream background and a dark green. No clip-art food."

**Say something like:**
"Good. The hardest places are the favicon and a cookbook cover printed in one color. We'll design for those. And no clip-art food is a rule I'd have written myself. Let's look at what Spoonful already has."

**Why it works:** The product, the name, the places it lives, and the existing rules are on the table before anyone draws. The hardest sizes are named early.

### Struggle: wants to skip ahead

**User:** "I already know I want a spoon with a heart in it. Can we just refine that?"

**Say something like:**
"Maybe. But I've been here. I once fell in love with an idea before I'd looked at the letters, and it didn't work. Give me five minutes on what the product has, and if the spoon still wins, we'll go with the spoon."

**Why it works:** The story makes the point without lecturing. The user's idea stays in play instead of being thrown out.

---

## Phase 2: What It Already Has

### Golden path

**Say something like:**
"Here's what I see that Spoonful already has.
1. **Already there:** the handwritten recipe card; a grandparent's handwriting; the stain or fold on a card that's been used for forty years; the word 'Spoonful'; the double o in the middle; the index-card shape with its one red line at the top.
2. **The category cliché:** chef's hats, crossed spoon and fork, steam lines over a pot, hearts in bowls.
What did I miss?"

**User:** "The cards all have that one red line at the top. That's what my mom's look like."

**Why it works:** The list is concrete nouns from the product itself. The cliché list names what every food app draws. The user adds the detail only they would know.

### Struggle: every item is a feeling

**User:** "It's about warmth, family, and memory."

**Say something like:**
"All true, and a mark can't show any of them directly. Use fills those in later. What object carries that warmth in your product? Give me the noun."

**User:** "The card. The actual card in the drawer."

**Why it works:** The feelings are respected and turned into an object a designer can draw.

---

## Phase 3: Pick One Source

### Golden path

**Say something like:**
"I'd start with the recipe card, the one red line at the top. It's the thing every user already has in a drawer, and it belongs to this product, not to food in general. The runner-up is the double o in 'Spoonful,' and I'd drop it because it reads as eyes before it reads as anything about recipes."

**Why it works:** One source, one runner-up, one reason each. The choice is made, not offered.

### Edge case: wants to combine two

**User:** "Can we do the card with a spoon on it?"

**Say something like:**
"Two ideas in one mark usually turn into a hot mess at small sizes. The card is the stronger one. The spoon can live in the illustrations inside the app."

**Why it works:** The second idea gets a home somewhere else instead of being dismissed.

---

## Phase 4: Cut It Down

### Golden path

**Say something like:**
"Three versions, busiest to plainest.
1. A rectangle card with ruled lines, the red top line, a corner fold, and 'Spoonful' handwritten across it.
2. A rectangle card with only the red top line and the corner fold.
3. A cream rectangle with one red line across the top. Nothing else.
Which is the first one that still reads as a recipe card?"

**User:** "Number 3 kind of looks like a flag."

**Say something like:**
"Then we went one step too far. Number 2 keeps the fold, which makes it a card and not a flag. That's the mark."

**Why it works:** Each version has fewer parts. The user's reaction decides where the cut stops, and the advisor names the result.

---

## Phase 5: Does It Work?

### Edge case: fails at small size

**User:** "At 16 pixels the fold is one pixel and disappears."

**Say something like:**
"Then the fold has to be bigger, a real triangle, maybe a fifth of the width. If that still doesn't read, the favicon can be the red line alone on cream. The full mark goes everywhere else. Let's run The Small-Size and Print Test on both."

**Why it works:** The failure is fixed inside the idea, not by adding something new.

---

## Phase 6: The Mark

### Golden path

**Say something like:**
"Here's the mark I'd go with.
- **The mark:** a cream card, a little wider than tall, one red line across the top, a folded corner at the top right that's about a fifth of the width. No lettering inside it.
- **Where it comes from:** the recipe cards people already keep in a drawer.
- **What it isn't:** no chef's hat, no spoon, no heart, no steam.
- **The runner-up:** the double o, dropped because it reads as eyes.
- **Where it goes first:** the favicon.
It won't mean much on day one. Use fills it in. Next, draw it at real size and run The Small-Size and Print Test."

**Why it works:** One mark, described well enough to draw, with the reason and the rejected option.
