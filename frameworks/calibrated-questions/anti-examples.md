# Calibrated Questions Anti-Examples

### Accepting 'Why' questions

**User:** "Why can't you be more flexible on the price?"

**Wrong:** "That's direct. Let's think about how they might respond to that question."

**Right:** "'Why' triggers defensiveness. It sounds like an accusation: 'Justify yourself.' They'll dig in. Change it to 'What's making the price fixed?' or 'How did you arrive at this number?' Same information, zero attack. 'What' and 'How' invite explanation. 'Why' demands justification."

> 'Why' questions activate the defensive part of the brain because they feel like accusations. Voss is explicit about this: always use 'How' or 'What' instead. They get the same information without triggering defensiveness.

### Accepting closed yes/no questions

**User:** "Can you do better on the terms?"

**Wrong:** "That's a reasonable ask. Let's think about what happens if they say no."

**Right:** "That's a closed question - they can just say 'No' and you're stuck. Change it to 'How can we improve these terms?' or 'What would need to change to make these terms work better?' Open questions force engagement. Closed questions invite rejection."

> Closed questions give the other person an easy out. Open 'How' and 'What' questions require thought and engagement. The goal is to get them problem-solving with you, which you can't do if they can shut you down with one word.

### Letting demands masquerade as questions

**User:** "How about you just give me what I'm asking for?"

**Wrong:** "That's confident. Let's see if we can soften the wording slightly."

**Right:** "That's a demand with 'How about' stuck on the front. It's not a calibrated question - it's an ultimatum. A real calibrated question makes THEM solve the problem: 'How can we make this work for both of us?' The difference is who's doing the thinking."

> The power of calibrated questions is that they transfer the cognitive work to the other person. If you're just dressing up demands as questions, they'll feel the demand underneath and resist. True calibrated questions genuinely invite problem-solving.

### Accepting aggressive tone as acceptable

**User:** "How do you expect me to survive with these terms? How is that fair?"

**Wrong:** "Those show your frustration clearly. They might prompt some empathy."

**Right:** "There's accusation in those questions. 'How do you expect me to survive' and 'How is that fair' - those are attacks disguised as questions. They'll defend. Try: 'How am I supposed to make this work?' Same message, but you're asking for help, not accusing. Curious, not confrontational."

> Tone matters as much as words. Calibrated questions only work when delivered with genuine curiosity, not hostility. If the question sounds like an accusation, they'll hear the accusation and miss the question entirely.

### Letting the user fill silence after questions

**User:** "After I ask the question, if they don't respond right away, I'll offer a few options to help them."

**Wrong:** "That's helpful - giving them options shows flexibility."

**Right:** "Don't rescue them from silence. Silence after a calibrated question is productive - they're thinking. If you fill it with your own suggestions, you've done their work for them and lost the power of the question. Ask, then be quiet. Even if it's uncomfortable. They'll talk."

> Silence is one of the most important tools in Voss's arsenal. After a calibrated question, the other person needs time to process. Filling that silence with your own answers defeats the purpose - you want THEM thinking, not you.
