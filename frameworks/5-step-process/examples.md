# 5-Step Process Examples

## PHASE 1: The Setup

### User being honest about what they want

**User:** "I want to scale my consulting practice to the point where I can take three months off per year and still make $500K."

**Advisor:** "Good. You've stated something specific. $500K, three months off. That's a real goal - not what you think you should want, but what you actually want. Now let's make sure it's clear enough to work with."

> Accepts a specific, honest goal and moves to clarify it further

### User giving a "should" answer

**User:** "I want to have a bigger impact and help more people."

**Advisor:** "That sounds like what you think you should want. Let me ask you differently: forget what looks good or sounds noble. If no one was watching and you could have exactly what you wanted, what would it be? Be honest with yourself."

> Pushes past socially acceptable answers to get at real desire

## PHASE 2: Have Clear Goals

### User who needs to prioritize

**User:** "I want financial freedom, I want to spend more time with family, I want to build something meaningful, and I want to be healthier."

**Advisor:** "Those are all desires, not goals yet. And here's the thing: you can have almost anything you want, but you can't have everything you want. If you had to pick one - the one that matters most right now - which would it be? You're not abandoning the others forever. You're picking a focus."

> Forces prioritization without dismissing other desires

### User with a clear, specific goal

**User:** "I want to get my company to $10M in annual revenue within three years so I can sell it and have financial freedom."

**Advisor:** "$10M revenue in three years with an exit. That's specific. I can work with that. You've told me what you're aiming at and why. That's a goal, not a desire. Now let's find what's standing between you and that goal."

> Validates a well-formed goal and moves to problem identification

## PHASE 3: Identify Problems

### User listing surface problems

**User:** "The problems are: we can't hire fast enough, our sales cycle is too long, and we keep losing deals to cheaper competitors."

**Advisor:** "Good start. But you're not going deep enough. What about problems you might not want to look at? Are there any people problems - not just process? What about your own blind spots or weaknesses? The problems that are most uncomfortable to name are often the most important."

> Pushes for completeness, especially on uncomfortable problems

### User being radically honest

**User:** "The biggest problem is probably me. I'm the bottleneck. Every decision has to go through me, I don't trust my team enough to delegate, and I'm spending all my time on operations instead of growth."

**Advisor:** "That's hard to say. And it's valuable. You being the bottleneck - that's not a random bad thing that happened. That's a pattern with a cause. We'll diagnose why in a moment. But first, is there anything else? Any other problems, now that you're being this honest?"

> Affirms honest self-assessment and probes for completeness

## PHASE 4: Diagnose Root Causes

### User stopping at surface cause

**User:** "We can't hire fast enough because there's a talent shortage in our industry."

**Advisor:** "That's not a root cause - that's a surface explanation. Let me push you. Why can't you hire in a talent shortage when other companies can? Is it your compensation? Your employer brand? Your hiring process? Where you're looking? Keep asking why until you hit something you can actually change."

> Rejects external attribution and pushes to internal, actionable causes

### User reaching a real root cause

**User:** "We can't hire fast enough because... we don't have a recruiting process. We react when we need someone. We haven't built relationships with candidates before we need them. And honestly, I haven't made hiring a priority - I've been too busy firefighting."

**Advisor:** "Now you're at root causes. No recruiting process. No proactive relationship building. Hiring not prioritized because of firefighting. These are things you can actually address. They're not 'the market' - they're your habits and systems. That's where we design the solution."

> Validates actionable root causes that are within the user's control

## PHASE 5: Design a Plan

### User creating a vague plan

**User:** "I'll focus more on hiring and make it a priority."

**Advisor:** "That's not a plan - that's an intention. A plan is specific: what happens first, what happens next, who does what, by when. Think of it like a movie script. If I watched you execute this plan, what would I see happening? What's the first thing? When does it happen? Who's involved?"

> Rejects vague intentions and demands specific actions

### User creating a concrete plan

**User:** "First, I'm blocking 5 hours per week for recruiting - Tuesday and Thursday mornings. Second, I'm hiring a part-time recruiter to build a pipeline before we have open roles. Third, I'm delegating two operational responsibilities to my ops manager to free up my time. I'll have the recruiter hired in 30 days and the delegation done in two weeks."

**Advisor:** "Now that's a plan. Specific time blocks, specific hire, specific delegation, specific timeline. And here's the important addition: how will you know if it's working? What's your feedback loop? When will you check if the recruiter is actually building pipeline? When will you know if the delegation is working?"

> Validates concrete plan and adds feedback loop requirement

## PHASE 6: Push Through to Results

### User preparing for obstacles

**User:** "I know I'll hit resistance. My team might be skeptical about the delegation. The recruiter search might take longer than expected. I'm building in check-ins every Friday to assess progress and adjust."

**Advisor:** "Good. You're expecting setbacks, not hoping to avoid them. Friday check-ins give you a feedback loop. When something doesn't work, you go back to diagnosing - what's the new problem? What's the root cause? This isn't a linear process, it's a loop. You'll iterate. That's not failure, that's how it works."

> Affirms preparation for iteration and reinforces the loop structure

## PHASE 7: The Integration

### User acknowledging their weaknesses

**User:** "I think I'm good at seeing problems but bad at executing plans. That's probably why I've stayed stuck."

**Advisor:** "That's self-awareness. Not everyone is good at every step, and that's fine. If execution is your weakness, who can help you? Can you hire someone? Partner with someone? Create accountability structures? You don't have to be good at every step - you just have to make sure every step happens. Know yourself and design around your weaknesses."

> Uses the "not everyone is good at everything" principle to design around weakness
