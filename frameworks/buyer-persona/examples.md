# Buyer-Persona Examples

Running example: **Chief Medical Officer (payer-side)** — a regional health plan evaluating a digital health platform for high-risk member management.

---

## PHASE 1: Role anchoring

### User giving title without accountability context

**User:** "I want to profile our Chief Medical Officer — they're always in these deals."

**Advisor:** "Chief Medical Officer is a broad title. A CMO at a health system is accountable for clinical quality and physician credentialing. A CMO at a payer — a health plan or insurance company — is accountable for medical cost ratio, clinical program effectiveness, and regulatory compliance. Those are fundamentally different accountability structures. Which CMO are we profiling, and what are they actually accountable for at the board level?"

> Forces distinction between title and accountability — the critical first cut before building any profile

### User clarifying the role

**User:** "Payer-side. Chief Medical Officer at a large regional health plan. She owns medical management — utilization review, case management, that kind of thing."

**Advisor:** "Payer-side CMO, owns medical management. Her core accountability is probably medical loss ratio — the percent of premium revenue that goes to claims. She holds that number down while maintaining HEDIS and quality scores that regulators and large employers require. Where does she typically sit in your buying committee — early evaluator, internal champion, or late-stage blocker?"

> Anchors the role in its financial accountability metric before mapping committee position

### User confirming dual committee position

**User:** "She's often the internal champion — she sees the clinical need — but she's also a potential blocker if the implementation looks risky."

**Advisor:** "Champion-with-blocker-potential. Common pattern for clinical executives in regulated industries. They want the outcome but they're risk-averse about implementation because their name is on the protocol changes. We'll build the profile to address both sides — the champion who needs to sell this internally, and the blocker who's evaluating whether she can defend it if it fails."

> Captures dual committee role and frames what the profile needs to do for each side

---

## PHASE 2: Evaluation criteria

### User defaulting to vague outcomes

**User:** "She cares about improving member outcomes and reducing unnecessary utilization."

**Advisor:** "'Reducing unnecessary utilization' — what's her measure? Emergency Department visit rates per thousand members? Avoidable readmission rates? HEDIS scores on specific chronic condition management? Each is a different budget line and a different evidence standard. What would she show her board in 18 months if this purchase worked?"

> Pushes from category-level goals to concrete, reportable metrics with a time horizon

### User identifying specific criteria

**User:** "She'd show ER visit rates going down, HEDIS improvement on diabetes and hypertension measures, and she'd need to show it happened faster than it would have otherwise — not just correlated with the program."

**Advisor:** "ER rates, HEDIS diabetes/hypertension, and counterfactual attribution. The attribution question is the hardest — she needs to defend that it was your intervention, not seasonal variation or her other programs. What evidence type satisfies her? Internal pilot data? Peer health plan references with matched populations? Published outcomes research? The evidence type determines what you need to bring into the first meeting."

> Validates specifics and probes the evidence standard — not just what she measures but what proves causation

---

## PHASE 3: Skepticism triggers

### User naming the category scar

**User:** "She's seen a lot of vendors promise clinical outcomes they can never actually prove. She's gotten burned on population health platforms that generate dashboards but don't change anything."

**Advisor:** "'Dashboards that don't change behavior' — that's the category scar. The implicit belief she carries into your pitch: 'You're going to show me a portal, claim engagement metrics, and then attribute every outcome improvement to your tool in a retrospective that ignores every confound.' What's her internal credibility risk if she sponsors this and the MLR doesn't move?"

> Names the category baggage and pivots immediately to the internal credibility risk that makes it structural

### User describing the failure mode she fears

**User:** "If she sponsors it and ER rates don't move, her CFO will use it as an exhibit in the 'we spent money on wellness theater' argument."

**Advisor:** "'Wellness theater' — that phrase tells you exactly what frame she's defending against. She's not just evaluating your platform; she's evaluating whether she can defend the decision to a skeptical CFO in 18 months. That's a structural skepticism trigger. Any pitch that sounds like a consumer wellness app — engagement scores, behavior journeys, empowerment — will activate it immediately. Your pitch needs to sound like a medical management upgrade, not a wellness program."

> Translates the failure mode into a structural trigger and gives a specific pitch-register implication

---

## PHASE 4: Language resonance

### User offering surface vocabulary

**User:** "She talks about 'value-based care' and 'whole-person health' a lot."

**Advisor:** "'Value-based care' is regulatory vocabulary in this space — it's lost signal value because everyone uses it. 'Whole-person health' is more interesting but ambiguous. The language that lands with payer CMOs operationally tends to be specific: MLR, PMPM, care gap closure rates, risk stratification tiers, avoidable utilization. Which register does she actually live in day-to-day versus what she says in external presentations?"

> Distinguishes regulatory vocabulary from operational language where credibility gets established

### User clarifying her register

**User:** "Day-to-day she's in the operational register — PMPM, risk scores, stratification tiers. The value-based care language is for external audiences and board decks."

**Advisor:** "Then your pitch register should match her internal language. 'Whole-person health' in your executive summary reads as external audience; 'reduce unnecessary utilization among your top-decile risk-stratified members' reads as someone who understands her operation. Phrases that backfire: anything that sounds like a consumer app — 'engagement journeys,' 'empowerment,' 'behavior change coaching.' She's running a medical management program, not a lifestyle brand. Those phrases activate the wellness-theater skepticism before you've made a single claim."

> Gives specific phrases to use and specific phrases to avoid, with the mechanism that makes each land or fail

---

## PHASE 5: Common objections

### User working through the standard three

**User:** "She usually raises: 'We've tried this before and didn't move the needle,' 'My team doesn't have the bandwidth to manage another vendor,' and 'How do I know your outcomes data isn't cherry-picked?'"

**Advisor:** "Three different objection types. 'We've tried this before' is a Challenger problem — her mental model of the category is shaped by a prior failure and you haven't disrupted it yet. 'No bandwidth' is JOLT-Lack-of-Information — she doesn't know what your implementation burden actually looks like, so she's pattern-matching to the last vendor's integration chaos. 'Cherry-picked data' is Outcome Uncertainty — she knows outcomes data can be gamed and she doesn't have a framework for evaluating yours. Each needs a different response. 'We've tried this before' can't be answered with evidence; it needs a reframe of how this is different from what she tried. The other two can be answered with the right proof points."

> Categorizes objections by type and gives different response strategies for each

### User identifying the commercial insight

**User:** "The real insight is that she's managing high-cost members reactively — her stratification models identify who's at risk, but there's no reliable mechanism to reach them before the crisis."

**Advisor:** "That's the commercial insight. She knows who's at risk — her data is good. What she doesn't have is a mechanism to close the gap between 'identified' and 'reached before the ER visit.' The reframe: 'Your risk stratification model is working. The gap is what happens between flagging a member and changing their trajectory — and that gap is where your MLR bleeds.' Start with her data, reveal the operational gap, never mention your product. That's the Challenger teach."

> Confirms insight structure — starts from her known situation, reveals the unseen gap, never leads with the product
