# Competitive Battle-Card Examples

*Running example: Fieldpath, a field service dispatch and scheduling platform for HVAC, plumbing, and electrical contractors. Sells to independent service companies with 5–50 technicians. Positioning.md is complete; canonical alternatives have been established.*

---

## PHASE 1: Load canonical alternatives

### User provides the canonical list — calibration on new competitor name

**User:** "Running standalone. Here's the competitive alternatives section from positioning.md:

Component 1 — Competitive Alternatives:
1. Jobber
2. ServiceTitan
3. Google Sheets + phone coordination (no software)
4. Housecall Pro"

**Advisor:** "Confirmed. We are working standalone — I'll write to `brand/market/competitive.md` at the end.

The four alternatives I'll use throughout this session:
1. Jobber
2. ServiceTitan
3. Google Sheets + phone (no software)
4. Housecall Pro

If a name comes up during our conversation that isn't on this list, I'll stop and ask you to resolve it in positioning first. That's not procedural — it's because a battle card built on an informal competitor list will drift. The list in positioning is the decision about who you actually compete against. This session enforces that decision."

**User:** "One more to add — I keep hearing about FieldEdge from reps. Should we add it?"

**Advisor:** "That's the right instinct, but not something we resolve here. If FieldEdge is a real alternative — prospects are actually choosing it over you — it belongs in `strategy/positioning.md` under Component 1, not in this session. Here's why: if we add it here, the battle card becomes the source of truth for who you compete with, and positioning becomes stale. That's backwards.

Two questions to decide where FieldEdge goes:
1. Have prospects mentioned FieldEdge by name in active deals in the last 90 days?
2. Have you lost deals where a rep believes the prospect chose FieldEdge?

If yes to either, stop this session, add FieldEdge to positioning, and come back. If no, it's noise — prospects mention tools they've heard of, not tools they actually choose. We don't put noise on the battle card.

Which is it?"

**User:** "Honestly, I think it's noise. We've never lost a deal to FieldEdge. Reps hear the name on industry forums."

**Advisor:** "Then we leave it off. The four alternatives on your canonical list are the ones we'll work through. Proceeding to the competitive landscape."

> PHASE 1 calibration: advisor refuses to add a new competitor mid-session; surfaces the two questions that determine whether it belongs in positioning; accepts the user's decision and moves forward without re-litigating

## PHASE 2: Competitive landscape narrative

### User describes each alternative; advisor pushes back on a one-sided portrait

**User:** "Jobber is the mid-market friendly one — small to medium shops, clean UX, good mobile app. They win on ease of use. ServiceTitan is the enterprise gorilla — very powerful, very expensive, huge implementation cost. They win on large multi-location shops that need deep reporting. Google Sheets and phone is what everyone is moving away from — it's not really a competitor, it's a starting point. Housecall Pro is basically Jobber but with better marketing in the home services space."

**Advisor:** "Good starting portrait. Pushing back on two of these before I draft:

ServiceTitan — 'very powerful, very expensive' is a fine description of their position, but 'they win on large multi-location shops' undersells their competitive strength in the 10–20 technician range. Their marketing has moved down-market. If you haven't lost deals in that range where ServiceTitan was the finalist, you're either not seeing them there yet or your reps are filtering them out before it becomes a competition. Which is it?

Google Sheets + phone — calling it 'not really a competitor' is a mistake. It's the alternative that loses you deals before a rep is ever involved. If a prospect is running on spreadsheets and they decide to keep running on spreadsheets after seeing Fieldpath, that's a loss to inertia. Inertia is a real alternative with real win conditions: zero cost, zero behavior change, no implementation risk. We should treat it that way in the battle card.

Housecall Pro — 'basically Jobber but with better marketing' — if that's accurate, what do their customers actually value that Jobber customers don't? Is there a real product difference or just a go-to-market difference?"

**User:** "On ServiceTitan — you're right. We've seen them in deals with 12–15 tech shops, usually when the owner has ambitions to scale. On Housecall Pro — honestly, their financing integration is better. HVAC and plumbing contractors like the buy-now-pay-later option for customers. Jobber doesn't have that."

**Advisor:** "That's a real difference. Housecall Pro landscape:

*Housecall Pro is a home services platform with a strong position in HVAC, plumbing, and electrical. Their consumer financing integration — buy-now-pay-later checkout — is a genuine product differentiator in high-ticket service categories. They win with contractors whose average ticket size is high enough that financing converts deals that would otherwise stall. Their marketing targets the same buyer as Fieldpath, which means prospects frequently evaluate both.*

Revised ServiceTitan landscape:

*ServiceTitan is the high-capability incumbent. They market to enterprise multi-location shops but have expanded into the 10–20 technician range, usually with owners who have near-term growth ambitions. Their win condition is depth: complex reporting, multi-location management, deep QuickBooks and accounting integrations. They lose to implementation friction — typical onboarding takes 90–120 days and requires a dedicated implementation manager. Prospects who need to go live fast do not choose ServiceTitan.*

Confirm or correct these before I draft the other two."

**User:** "Both accurate. Proceed."

> Landscape phase: advisor challenges the 'not a competitor' framing for inertia; draws out a real product difference the user understated; presents draft narratives for confirmation before moving forward

## PHASE 3: Differentiators per alternative

### User identifies advantages and disadvantages; advisor refuses a vague claim

**User:** "Against Jobber: we're faster to implement — 2 weeks vs their 4–6 weeks. We have better route optimization built in. Against ServiceTitan: we're cheaper and faster. Against Housecall Pro: we don't have their financing integration, but our dispatch board is better. Against spreadsheets: every dimension — we're software, they're not."

**Advisor:** "'Better route optimization built in' — I'm going to push on that. Better how? Better is a comparison, and Jobber has a routing feature. What specifically does Fieldpath's route optimization do that Jobber's doesn't? I'll accept any of the following:

- Specific algorithm difference (e.g., live traffic, multi-stop optimization)
- Measurable outcome difference (e.g., avg drive time reduced by X%)
- Feature present in Fieldpath that is absent in Jobber
- A deal where a prospect tested both and chose Fieldpath specifically because of routing

Which of these can you back up?"

**User:** "Our routing does live traffic and multi-stop optimization in one pass. Jobber requires you to run optimization in segments manually if you have more than 6 stops. We had a deal with a 20-tech plumbing company where the owner timed both systems — ours built the route in 40 seconds, Jobber took them 8 minutes of manual adjustment."

**Advisor:** "That's a real differentiator. Recorded: 'Fieldpath runs multi-stop live-traffic optimization in a single pass. Jobber requires manual segment adjustment for routes exceeding 6 stops — one customer timed 40 seconds vs 8 minutes for a 20-technician route.'

Now on disadvantages: you mentioned you don't have Housecall Pro's financing integration. What else? Any advantage Jobber has over you that a rep hears about in deals?"

**User:** "Jobber has better QuickBooks integration. We sync but it's not as deep — we don't sync attachments or PDF invoices into QuickBooks. Also, Jobber's customer-facing portal is more polished than ours."

**Advisor:** "Recorded as genuine disadvantages — critical that reps know these before a prospect surfaces them. If a rep finds out mid-demo that a prospect is a heavy QuickBooks attachment user, they need to be able to say 'here's what that looks like in our sync' rather than being caught off guard.

Perceived-but-not-real difference worth noting: implementation speed against ServiceTitan. Prospects often assume ServiceTitan is the safer choice because of their track record with large shops. That safety perception is real, but the 90–120 day onboarding cost is frequently underestimated by buyers at evaluation. Reps should surface the implementation timeline question early — 'When do you need to go live?' — because the answer often closes ServiceTitan out of the conversation without Fieldpath ever having to attack them directly."

> Differentiators phase: advisor demands specificity on a vague claim before accepting it; accepts concrete evidence; draws out disadvantages without being asked; surfaces a perceived difference that is really an implementation-cost trap

## PHASE 4: Common objections + responses

### User surfaces top objections; advisor adds underlying concern and a deal-advancing question

**User:** "Most common objections by competitor:

Against Jobber: 'We already know Jobber, switching costs feel high.' 'Jobber is cheaper.' Against ServiceTitan: 'We want to grow, and we've heard ServiceTitan is better for larger operations.' Against Housecall Pro: 'We need the financing integration for our customers.' Against spreadsheets: 'We've been doing it this way for ten years and the guys are used to it.'"

**Advisor:** "Good list. Drafting responses. Open `language/messaging.md` before I do — I'll pull from your value-prop phrasings where possible rather than inventing new language.

**Jobber — 'Switching costs feel high'**
- Surface claim: moving systems is disruptive
- Underlying concern: the risk of a failed implementation is worse than the cost of staying put
- Response: 'You're right that any migration has friction. Here's what ours looks like in practice: two weeks to go live, your historical jobs imported, your technicians trained in a two-hour group session. The question we've found matters most isn't the switching cost — it's whether the cost of staying compounds. What does a day look like when your dispatcher is manually juggling routes by text?' 
- Deal-advancing question: 'If we could show you the exact migration checklist and let you talk to a customer who switched from Jobber, would that change the risk calculation?'

**Jobber — 'Cheaper'**
- Surface claim: Jobber costs less
- Underlying concern: the price difference isn't justified by the value
- Response: 'Jobber is priced lower at entry. Where the math changes is routing — one of our customers documented that the multi-stop optimization alone saved each technician 45 minutes a day across a 12-person crew. That's 9 hours of labor recaptured daily. At your loaded labor rate, what does 9 hours a day cost you right now?' 
- Deal-advancing question: 'Would it be worth running a routing comparison — your real jobs, both systems, same morning — so you have an actual number to put against the price delta?'

**ServiceTitan — 'Better for larger operations'**
- Surface claim: ServiceTitan scales better
- Underlying concern: choosing Fieldpath locks them into a smaller-shop product and they'll have to migrate again
- Response: 'That's exactly what we hear from growth-oriented owners, and it's worth being honest about. ServiceTitan does things at 50+ technicians that we don't. The question is timeline: ServiceTitan averages 90–120 days to go live after contract. If you're planning to add three technicians in the next year, you're buying capability you can't use yet, at a price point and implementation drag that affects you now. We work with shops up to 40 technicians who haven't outgrown us.'
- Deal-advancing question: 'What does your headcount look like in 18 months? Let's map what you'd actually need from each system at that size.'

**Housecall Pro — 'We need the financing integration'**
- Surface claim: Housecall Pro has financing, we don't
- Underlying concern: consumer financing converts high-ticket jobs that would otherwise stall
- Response: 'We don't have native financing and I won't pretend otherwise. What I'd ask is: what percentage of your jobs actually close on financing? If it's the primary close mechanism for a large portion of your book, Housecall Pro is the right call. If it's occasionally offered but rarely used, the question is whether you're building your operations platform around an edge case.'
- Deal-advancing question: 'Can you pull your last three months of jobs and tell me how many used financing? That number should drive the decision.'

**Spreadsheets — 'We've been doing it this way for ten years'**
- Surface claim: inertia; the current system works
- Underlying concern: changing will disrupt the team, and the team's resistance will fall on the owner
- Response: 'Ten years is a long time and I'm not going to tell you it's broken. What I'd ask is: in the last year, have you missed a job because of a scheduling miscommunication? Lost a technician an hour to a wrong address? Had a dispatcher day where everything fell apart because one call went sideways? If yes — how many times? Each of those costs real money. We're not offering change for change's sake, we're offering a system where the dispatcher isn't the single point of failure.'
- Deal-advancing question: 'Is there a week in the last month you'd describe as a bad week for dispatch? Tell me what that looked like — I want to show you what that same week looks like in Fieldpath.'

Pull from messaging.md phrasings where your language already captures the routing and labor-recovery angle — those are your sharpest proof points across competitors."

> PHASE 4 output: five objection/response maps, each naming the underlying concern separately from the surface claim; responses pull from internal proof points; every response closes with a deal-advancing question, not a product defense
