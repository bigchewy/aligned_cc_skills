# Competitive Battle-Card Anti-Examples

*Running example: Fieldpath, a field service dispatch and scheduling platform for HVAC, plumbing, and electrical contractors. Sells to independent service companies with 5–50 technicians. Positioning.md is complete; canonical alternatives have been established.*

---

### Anti-Example 1: Introducing a new competitor mid-session instead of updating positioning first

**User:** "Actually, can we add FieldEdge to the battle card? I keep hearing the name from reps."

**Advisor (wrong):** "Sure — FieldEdge is a common name in the HVAC dispatch space. I'll add them as a fifth alternative alongside the four on your canonical list. I'll draft a landscape narrative now:

*FieldEdge is a field service management platform focused on HVAC and refrigeration contractors. They are owned by GPS Insight and have strong integrations with service agreements and maintenance contracts. They win with shops that rely heavily on recurring revenue from maintenance agreements.*

Continuing to PHASE 3 — I'll include FieldEdge in the differentiators table."

> Positioning is the canonical source of truth for who you compete against. Adding a fifth competitor inside this framework session — without updating positioning.md first — creates two competing sources of truth: the battle card says five alternatives, positioning says four. The next time someone runs this framework, they will not know which list to trust. Reps who read the battle card will cite FieldEdge; your positioning will not mention it. The correct response at PHASE 1 is to stop and surface the question: "FieldEdge is not in your canonical alternatives list. If reps are hearing the name in active deals, it may belong in positioning — but that decision gets made there, not here. Add it to `strategy/positioning.md` under Component 1 and come back. If you're confident it's noise, we leave it off." The battle card inherits the alternatives list from positioning. It does not extend it.

### Anti-Example 2: Writing objection responses that invent language instead of pulling from messaging

**User:** "What's the response when a prospect says we're priced higher than Jobber?"

**Advisor (wrong):** "Here's the response for the Jobber pricing objection:

*Fieldpath delivers more value per dollar — our routing optimization alone gives technicians back time they were spending on manual scheduling. When you factor in efficiency gains, the math shifts quickly. Reps can close this with: 'You're investing in software that works harder than your current system — that's not a cost, that's leverage.'*"

> The objection response was written from scratch, inventing phrasings that may conflict with or undercut language the brand has already validated in `language/messaging.md`. The competitive battle card composes with messaging by design — value-prop phrasings, proof-point language, and differentiation framing belong in messaging.md and should be pulled into the battle card, not reinvented here. Inventing copy inside this framework creates two versions of how the brand talks about routing and efficiency: one in messaging, one in the battle card. When the messaging is updated, the battle card won't be. The correct response: "Before I draft this response, open your `language/messaging.md` — specifically the value-prop phrasings for routing and labor recovery. I'll anchor the objection response to that language rather than invent new phrasing." If messaging doesn't yet capture this angle, the advisor should flag the gap rather than fill it unilaterally.

### Anti-Example 3: Listing status-quo and in-house alternatives in competitive.md instead of alternatives.md

**User:** "What about prospects who just decide to keep using spreadsheets, or who try to build something in-house? Should I include those?"

**Advisor (wrong):** "Absolutely — those are real competitive threats and belong in the battle card. I'll add two more sections:

**Status quo (spreadsheets + phone):** Win condition — zero cost, zero behavior change. Fieldpath loses here when the prospect has no pain trigger and no internal champion pushing for change.

**Build in-house:** Win condition — the prospect believes a custom build will fit their process better. Common in larger shops with a technical co-founder or IT staff.

Including these gives reps a complete picture of everything they might lose a deal to."

> Status quo and in-house build are non-market alternatives — they are not products the prospect chooses instead of Fieldpath, they are decisions to not buy any software (or to build their own). These belong in `brand/market/alternatives.md`, which documents the full landscape of how the prospect can solve their problem, including doing nothing. `brand/market/competitive.md` — the file this framework produces — is scoped to named market competitors that appear as alternatives in active deals. Mixing non-market alternatives into the competitive file creates a category error: reps reading "competitive.md" expect to find named competitors they will encounter during evaluation. Finding "status quo" and "in-house" there conflates selling skill (handling inertia, building urgency) with competitive positioning (handling named product comparisons). The correct response: "Status quo and in-house are real things to prepare reps for, but they live in `alternatives.md`, not here. This framework produces `competitive.md` — named market competitors only. If you don't have an `alternatives.md` yet, that's a separate conversation."

### Anti-Example 4: Skipping the PHASE 6 sync check because the session felt clean

**User:** "We covered everything thoroughly — I'm confident the battle card is accurate. Can we skip the sync check and just finish?"

**Advisor (wrong):** "Makes sense — we've been rigorous throughout the session and I've been careful to only reference the four alternatives from your canonical list. The sync check would be redundant at this point. The file is assembled and ready."

> The sync check is not a verification of the advisor's attentiveness — it is a structural guard against the specific failure mode this framework was designed to prevent: competitive.md drifting from positioning.md. Drift happens in ways that feel invisible during a session. A prospect name mentioned in an objection response. A shorthand reference in a differentiator. A legacy term from a previous version of positioning that crept in through the user's language. "We've been careful" is not equivalent to "the scan passed." The correct response to a request to skip PHASE 6: "The scan takes two minutes and it is not optional. Its value is not confirming what I already know — it is catching the things neither of us noticed. Running it now." If the sync check passes cleanly, that confirmation is itself useful: it is the artifact that certifies the file is ready. Without it, the session closes without a certification — and the next time positioning changes, there is no baseline to diff against.

### Anti-Example 5: Writing competitor narratives as marketing copy instead of operational intelligence

**User:** "Can you write the landscape section for all four competitors?"

**Advisor (wrong):** "Here's the competitive landscape for all four alternatives:

**Jobber:** Jobber is an excellent choice for small service businesses looking for a clean, intuitive platform. Their beautiful mobile app and thoughtful UX make them a beloved tool in the home services community. They have built a loyal customer base through consistent product quality and outstanding customer support.

**ServiceTitan:** ServiceTitan is the gold standard for enterprise field service — a powerhouse platform trusted by the largest and most sophisticated operations in the industry. Their deep feature set and robust integrations make them the aspirational choice for shops with ambitions to scale.

**Housecall Pro:** Housecall Pro has built one of the most respected brands in home services, with a thriving community of contractors who love their platform. Their consumer financing integration is a standout feature that has won raves from customers in high-ticket service categories.

**Spreadsheets + phone:** The classic approach — low cost, zero learning curve, and a system your team already knows. While it lacks the sophistication of modern platforms, many contractors have made it work for years."

> Landscape narratives are operational intelligence for reps, not marketing copy for competitors. A laudatory description of Jobber's "beautiful mobile app" does not help a rep understand when they will lose to Jobber, who Jobber's best customer is, or what Jobber's genuine weaknesses are in a competitive evaluation. The purpose of the landscape section is to answer three questions for each alternative: Who is their best customer? How do they win? Where do they lose? "A beloved tool in the home services community" answers none of these. The correct approach: write from the rep's perspective, not the marketing team's. "Jobber wins with shops under 15 technicians where ease of onboarding is the primary purchase criterion. Their best customer already uses QuickBooks and values the integration depth. They lose when a prospect needs multi-stop route optimization or has a dispatcher managing more than 8 simultaneous calls." Describing a competitor favorably does not make reps more prepared — it makes them less able to have an honest conversation with a prospect who has already seen the competitor's demo.
