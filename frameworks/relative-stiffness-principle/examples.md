# Relative Stiffness Principle Examples

## PHASE 1: Establish the Problem

### User with recurring back pain

**User:** "I keep throwing out my back when I bend over. It doesn't matter how strong I get - it keeps happening."

**Advisor:** "That pattern tells me everything. Recurring injury during the same movement means the movement pattern itself is the problem, not your strength. Your body is taking the path of least resistance, and that path goes through your lumbar spine. Let's find out why."

> Directly addresses the "strength isn't working" complaint with the relative stiffness principle

### User attributing pain to aging

**User:** "My lower back hurts after standing all day. I'm 45 and I guess things just start to break down."

**Advisor:** "No. This isn't age. At 45, your tissues should be perfectly capable of standing. What's happening is that standing is loading your lumbar spine excessively because of relative stiffness somewhere in the chain. Let's find where movement is defaulting and why."

> Rejects the age explanation and reframes as movement dysfunction

### User focused on the pain location

**User:** "I have shoulder pain, right in the front of my shoulder when I reach overhead."

**Advisor:** "Where you feel pain is where stress accumulates - but that's not necessarily where the movement problem is. Your shoulder might be a victim of compensation. When you reach overhead, is your shoulder actually moving fully, or is your back arching to create the appearance of reach? Let's examine the relative stiffness in that chain."

> Redirects from symptom location to movement pattern analysis

## PHASE 2: Map the Kinetic Chain

### User with forward bending problem

**User:** "Bending over to pick things up. Even small things. I feel it in my lower back every time."

**Advisor:** "Forward bending involves a kinetic chain: your hips should hinge, your spine should stay stable. If your lower back is taking the load, something in that chain is stiffer than your lumbar spine - probably your hips. Let's test this. When you bend, where does the movement happen first?"

> Maps the chain and sets up relative stiffness testing

### User with rotational problem

**User:** "Twisting to reach something behind me, or playing golf - my lower back always hurts after."

**Advisor:** "Rotation should come from your hips and thoracic spine. Your lumbar spine is designed for only about 13 degrees of rotation total - it's not a rotation joint. If you're feeling it in your lower back during rotation, that segment is rotating when it shouldn't be. Something else in the chain - probably your hips or thoracic spine - is too stiff."

> Provides specific biomechanical context about lumbar rotation limits

### User unsure what makes it worse

**User:** "It just hurts during the day. I'm not sure what makes it worse."

**Advisor:** "Then let's find out systematically. Stand up and try three movements: bend forward like you're touching your toes, arch backward slightly, and twist to look behind you each direction. Which one increases your symptoms?"

> Provides systematic testing when user can't identify trigger

## PHASE 3: Identify the Stiffness Relationship

### User discovering hip stiffness pattern

**User:** "My back definitely rounds before my hips move. I can feel that now that you pointed it out."

**Advisor:** "There it is. Your hamstrings or hip flexors are stiffer than your lumbar extensors. When you bend, your body takes the path of least resistance - and that path goes through your spine. Your hips should be moving 60-70 degrees before your spine contributes much. Yours aren't. That's the relative stiffness problem."

> Confirms the diagnosis and provides specific range-of-motion context

### User who stretches but nothing changes

**User:** "But I stretch my hamstrings every day. They're still tight."

**Advisor:** "Stretching your hamstrings doesn't change relative stiffness if your lumbar spine is still less stiff. Your hamstrings might be slightly more mobile now, but your spine is still the path of least resistance. You've been treating one side of the equation. We need to address the other side - your lumbar stability."

> Explains why stretching alone doesn't work

### User noticing asymmetry

**User:** "Twisting to the right is fine, but twisting to the left makes my back hurt."

**Advisor:** "Asymmetry is diagnostic. Your left hip or thoracic spine is stiffer than the right. When you twist left, those segments don't move adequately, so your lumbar spine compensates. The pattern is so consistent that I can predict which side is the problem from where you feel pain."

> Uses asymmetry to pinpoint the stiffness location

## PHASE 4: The Key Insight

### User having the "aha" moment

**User:** "So all that hip stretching I did... it didn't help because my core wasn't stabilizing?"

**Advisor:** "Exactly. You were loosening one area without changing the relative relationship. Your hips got slightly more mobile, but your spine was still the easier path. The intervention needs both: mobilize what should move AND stabilize what shouldn't. Then the path of least resistance actually changes."

> Reinforces the dual intervention principle

### User resistant to the concept

**User:** "But my PT said I just need to strengthen my back."

**Advisor:** "Strengthening alone doesn't change movement patterns. You can have the strongest back in the world, but if your hips are stiffer relative to your spine, movement will still default to your spine during functional activities. Strength is one variable. Relative stiffness is another. Both matter."

> Addresses the common "just strengthen it" approach

### User connecting past failed treatments

**User:** "I've had massage, acupuncture, adjustments... nothing lasts more than a few days."

**Advisor:** "Those interventions treat the symptom location - the tissue that's being stressed. But they don't change the movement pattern that's creating the stress. The tissue calms down, then you go back to moving the same way, and the stress returns. You have to change the movement to change the outcome."

> Explains why passive treatments don't produce lasting results

## PHASE 5: Design the Intervention

### User ready to implement

**User:** "So I need to stretch my hips AND stabilize my core. What specifically should I do?"

**Advisor:** "Here's your prescription: First, hip mobility work - hip flexor stretches, hip hinge practice. Second, lumbar stability work - dead bugs, bird-dogs, but ONLY if you can maintain neutral spine. Third - and this is the most important - practice the corrected movement pattern during every forward bend in your day. The exercises teach the pattern. Daily life is where you practice it."

> Provides specific, hierarchical intervention plan

### User wanting to test progress

**User:** "How do I know if it's working?"

**Advisor:** "Test the movement. Can you now forward bend with your spine staying neutral while your hips hinge? Try it against a wall - keep your back flat against the wall while you hinge forward. If you can do that with control, you've changed the path of least resistance. Your spine is now stable enough that your hips become the easier path."

> Provides concrete, testable outcome measure

### User with complex pattern

**User:** "I have both the bending problem AND the overhead problem."

**Advisor:** "Then we address both kinetic chains. For bending: hip mobility plus lumbar anti-flexion stability. For overhead: lat mobility plus lumbar anti-extension stability. The common element is your lumbar spine - it's becoming the path of least resistance in multiple directions. That tells me your global core stability needs work alongside addressing the specific stiffness patterns."

> Integrates multiple patterns into coherent intervention
