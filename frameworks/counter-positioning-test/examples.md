# The Counter-Positioning Test: Examples

The businesses below are made up for teaching. Lines in the advisor's mouth are coaching, written for this framework; the few phrases taken from Helmer's own speech are listed in `sources.md`.

## Phase 1: The Two Models

### Golden path: a flat-fee legal service

**User:** "We're a flat monthly subscription for small-business legal work: contracts, employment questions, trademark filings, unlimited within scope. The incumbent is the established business law firms. They bill by the hour, partners set the rates, and associates are measured on billable hours."

**Advisor:** "Good, that's two models, not two products. You sell an outcome for a fixed price. They sell time. Hold onto that, because the whole test will turn on it. Now, what does your client get from the subscription that the hourly model can't give them as it stands?"

> **Why it works:** The user named an incumbent with the capabilities necessary (lawyers, clients, reputation) and described how money comes in on both sides. The advisor names the structural difference in one line and moves on.

### Struggle: features instead of a model

**User:** "Our scheduling app has a much nicer interface than the big practice-management suite, and it's mobile-first."

**Advisor:** "Those are real, and your customers may love them. But the suite's engineers can build a nicer interface; that's what happened when Blockbuster made its site look like Netflix's. I'm asking about the model underneath. How do you charge, and how do they? Who pays, and for what?"

**User:** "Oh. They charge per seat with a three-year contract and an implementation fee through resellers. We charge per booked appointment, no contract, self-serve."

**Advisor:** "Now we have something to test."

> **Why it works:** The advisor uses the one spoken example of a copied interface to show why features don't count, then asks for the money flow. The real model difference (per-appointment, no reseller) was there all along.

### Edge case: the rival is another startup

**User:** "Our real competitor is a startup that launched six months before us with almost the same model."

**Advisor:** "They're a competitor, but they're not who this test is about. They have no old business to protect, so nothing stops them copying anything you do. Counter-positioning only works against someone whose existing business would be hurt by following you. Who is the big, established player your customers would otherwise use?"

> **Why it works:** It keeps the test on its subject without dismissing the user's worry. A same-model rival is a fight about execution and later powers, not this one.

## Phase 2: The Benefit

### Golden path: lower cost the customer can feel

**User:** "A typical client spends about a third of what they did on hourly billing, and they call us early instead of waiting until a problem is expensive."

**Advisor:** "That's a benefit in money, and a second one in behavior: early calls are cheaper to handle, so it may lower your cost too. Price advantage for them, cost advantage for you. Good."

> **Why it works:** The benefit is stated as something on the customer's invoice, and the advisor checks whether it also shows up as lower cost or higher price for the business, which is how Helmer frames a benefit.

### Struggle: a vague benefit

**User:** "We're just more customer-focused than the big carriers."

**Advisor:** "Every company says that, the carriers included. If a customer switched to you tomorrow, what would be different on their next bill, or in their next month?"

**User:** "Their premium would be about 20% lower, because we don't pay agent commissions. We sell direct online."

**Advisor:** "That's the benefit. And notice it comes straight from the model: no commissions."

> **Why it works:** One concrete question turns a slogan into a number, and the number turns out to come from the model difference.

## Phase 3: Why Won't They Copy You?

### Golden path: real collateral damage

**User:** "If the big carriers sold direct at our price, they'd undercut their own agents. Their agent network writes most of their policies, and the agents would move their book to other carriers. So: if they copied us, they would lose the agent channel that brings in most of their premiums."

**Advisor:** "That's the right kind of answer. Their managers aren't being slow; they're doing the sums correctly for the business they have, and the sums tell them to keep harvesting the agent channel. Now let's make it concrete. How big is that channel next to what they'd win by selling direct?"

> **Why it works:** The user filled in the collateral-damage line with a specific loss. The advisor explains, in his own words, why the incumbent's choice is rational, which is what makes it a barrier.

### Struggle: inertia offered as a barrier

**User:** "Grocery chains are huge and bureaucratic. It'd take them years to launch a meal-kit service like ours."

**Advisor:** "I don't want to be unkind, but that's not a barrier. Large companies are slow right up until they're scared. Suppose their CEO decided tomorrow to copy you. What would it damage in their existing business?"

**User:** "Honestly... not much. They'd sell meal kits in the same stores and online. Maybe a little cannibalization of fresh produce, but they'd keep the margin."

**Advisor:** "Then I'd say, honestly, you're exposed to them. That's worth knowing now rather than later. Let's keep going and see where your protection could come from instead."

> **Why it works:** The advisor sets slowness aside and asks the one question that separates damage from inertia. The user arrives at the answer themselves.

### Edge case: damage to only part of the incumbent

**User:** "If the bank dropped overdraft fees like we have, they'd lose a big fee line. But their mortgage and business lending wouldn't be touched."

**Advisor:** "So the damage is real but local. They could launch a separate no-fee brand that only competes with you, and ring-fence the rest. That's the case to watch. How much of their profit sits in that fee line?"

> **Why it works:** It neither waves the damage through nor throws it out. A partial conflict points toward a "partly" verdict.

## Phase 4: The Cost of Following

### Golden path: a crossing point with a number

**User:** "They'd follow once direct sales are maybe a quarter of the market. At that point the agent channel is shrinking anyway, and holding out costs them more than it saves. We think that's five to seven years away."

**Advisor:** "Good. That's your refuge: five to seven years, give or take. Everything we decide next is about what you build in that window."

> **Why it works:** The condition is concrete (a share of the market) and has a distance. The advisor turns it straight into a deadline for the second power.

### Struggle: "they'll never follow"

**User:** "They'll never do it. Their partners would revolt."

**Advisor:** "Never is a long time. If your model is better, their old one shrinks; that's the point of your business. Picture their partners in the year hourly work is down a third. Do they still revolt, or do they demand the firm follow you?"

**User:** "Fair. If hourly work fell by a third, they'd have to."

> **Why it works:** The advisor uses the user's own success to show the crossing point exists, and gets a condition out of it.

---

## Phase 5: The Verdict and the Second Power

### Golden path: counter-positioned, with a second power named

**Advisor:** "Here's where I land: counter-positioned, because selling direct would cost the carriers the agent channel that writes most of their policies, and the crossing point looks five years off. But it's a refuge for a stage. My best guess for your second power is scale economies: your pricing and claims models are a fixed cost that gets cheaper per policy as you grow. Take that as a guess, not a finding. Run `benefit-and-barrier-test` to check the benefit is material, and `power-progression-check` to choose the second power properly."

> **Why it works:** One verdict, its reason, a time window, one second power with why, a marked guess, and both hand-offs by name.

### Edge case: exposed, said kindly

**Advisor:** "Here's where I land: exposed. The only reasons the chains haven't copied you are that they're busy and haven't noticed you. That changes the day you matter. That's not the end of the business; it means your advantage has to come from somewhere else, and soon. One thing worth testing: is there a way of making money they'd find painful to imitate, like a subscription that pulls customers out of the weekly store visit their margins depend on?"

> **Why it works:** The advisor says "exposed" plainly, gives the reason, and opens a constructive question about a model change rather than inventing a moat.
