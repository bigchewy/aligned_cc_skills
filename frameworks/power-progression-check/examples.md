# Power Progression Check — Examples

Three made-up businesses, to show the moves at different phases:

- **Origination:** a two-founder company selling scheduling and billing software to independent physiotherapy clinics. 30 clinics, nine months of sales. Clinics switch from a paper diary plus the large practice-management suite most of them already pay for.
- **Takeoff:** an online marketplace for renting used construction equipment between contractors in three states. Growing 8% a month, about 1,400 active renters, two funded rivals.
- **Stability:** a regional chain of 60 hardware stores, 40 years old, flat sales, competing with two national chains.

The coaching lines are written for this framework. Lines quoted as Helmer's own words are listed in `sources.md` with their source.

---

## Phase 1: Which Phase Are You In?

### Golden path

**User:** "We sell scheduling and billing to physio clinics. Before us they used a paper diary and the big practice suite for billing. 30 clinics, nine months, about $9k a month. I'd say origination: half our clinics still run the paper diary alongside us."

**Say something like:**
"I agree, and that last detail is the evidence. If clinics still keep the paper diary, the substitution isn't finished. So: origination, because half your clinics haven't let go of the old way yet. That's the line I'll write down."

**Why it works:** The phase is tied to one piece of evidence the founder already had, and it's stated back as the line that goes on the page.

### Struggle: wants to be further along

**User:** "We're 8% month-on-month, so we're basically in stability, right? We're the market leader in our three states."

**Say something like:**
"That's real growth, and congratulations on it. But stability is when growth has slowed and the shape of the market is settled. At 8% a month with two funded rivals, the shape isn't settled. I'd say takeoff, and in takeoff, operational excellence is everything. Does that sound right?"

**User:** "Fair. Takeoff."

**Why it works:** The growth is acknowledged before the finer line is drawn. The definition does the correcting, not the advisor's opinion.

### Edge case: a new business inside an old one

**User:** "We're the 40-year-old hardware chain. Stable, sure. But we've just started a tool-rental subscription for contractors. Which phase is that?"

**Say something like:**
"Two answers. The chain is in stability. The subscription is in origination. It's a second act, and second acts start the cycle again. Let's run the check on the subscription, because that's where the open questions are. We can do the chain another day."

**Why it works:** One business per run. The new line gets its own phase instead of borrowing the parent's.

## Phase 2: Strike What Isn't Available Yet

### Golden path

**Say something like:**
"So you're in origination. Branding and process power only become available at stability, so those come off the table. A brand that's Power takes many years of consistent behavior. Process power is really operational excellence on steroids, and that takes a long history too. Scale, switching costs and network economies aren't yours yet either, because they depend on your scale relative to competitors; we'll pick one to build toward. Does anything there feel like a loss?"

**User:** "Not really. We never said brand. We say 'easy to set up.'"

**Why it works:** The strikes come with reasons, and the user is invited to object instead of being told what they think.

### Struggle: claims a brand

**User:** "But physios love us. Our brand is our moat. We just spent $20k on a conference sponsorship."

**Say something like:**
"I don't want to be unkind, and founders have to be optimistic. People liking you is worth a lot. But recognition you bought isn't Power: that's not power, that you paid for it. Would a clinic pay you more than a rival offering the identical product, just because it's you? If you think so, let's test it later with `benefit-and-barrier-test`. For today I'll write branding as struck, because at nine months it can't have had time to become Power."

**Why it works:** The finer line (liking versus a price premium) does the work, and the hand-off gives the claim a fair hearing without stalling the check.

### Edge case: stability, and the old advantage is fading

**User:** "We're the hardware chain. We've always said our advantage is that our staff know everything."

**Say something like:**
"In stability nothing is struck on timing, so let's take that seriously. Could the national chains hire and train staff to the same level? If yes, it's operational excellence: you're on a treadmill, and if you stop running you get creamed, but it isn't Power. If there's something in how your stores run that rivals couldn't copy even while watching, that's a process power claim, and it needs `benefit-and-barrier-test`."

**Why it works:** Stability opens the late powers, so they're tested rather than struck, and the user's story is sorted into treadmill or Power.

## Phase 3: The Origination Question - Counter-Positioning

### Golden path

**User:** "Clinics are substituting away from the big practice suite. That company could build our scheduling easily. Why don't they? They charge per seat, and we charge a flat fee per clinic. If they matched us, their per-seat revenue from every large clinic would drop."

**Say something like:**
"That's the shape I look for. They have the capabilities, and copying you damages something they make money on today. Counter positioning is the refuge from that. I'll mark it plausible, with the per-seat pricing as the reason. If you want it tested properly, `counter-positioning-test` goes through it in detail."

**Why it works:** All three questions are answered with specifics, and the reason the incumbent won't copy is a cost to its existing business, not a lack of attention.

### Struggle: the reason is inertia

**User:** "The big suite won't copy us because they're slow and bureaucratic."

**Say something like:**
"They may well be slow. But that's a reason for today, not a barrier. Big companies get fast when a market gets big enough. If they built your product tomorrow, would it cost them anything they earn now? If not, you're pretty high risk from an incumbent who already has the capabilities, and I'd mark counter-positioning 'not shown yet.'"

**Why it works:** The difference between inertia and a real cost to the incumbent is drawn plainly, and the mark on the page reflects it.

### Edge case: takeoff, checking whether the old counter-position still holds

**User:** "We're the equipment marketplace. We started against rental yards, who couldn't list their competitors' equipment."

**Say something like:**
"Good. That's a classic origination story. Now that you're in takeoff, ask whether it still holds. Have any rental yards started listing other people's machines? If not, keep it as plausible. Either way, the bigger question for you now is the next phase."

**Why it works:** The origination question is still asked in takeoff, but briefly, so the session moves to the powers that matter for the current phase.

## Phase 4: The Takeoff Question

### Golden path

**User:** "Network economies, I think. Each new contractor who lists a machine makes the marketplace more useful to renters in that county, and renters bring more listers."

**Say something like:**
"Yes, that one is plausible for a local marketplace. The key thing here is materiality, not whether the flywheel exists. So: in a county where you have twice your rival's listings, do renters pick you almost every time, or only a bit more often? If it's almost every time, the benefit is real: you can charge a higher fee and still win."

**Why it works:** The power is tied to scale relative to the rival, county by county, and materiality is asked as a countable question.

### Struggle: claims all three

**User:** "All three, honestly. Scale, switching costs, and network effects."

**Say something like:**
"I'd be surprised. Usually one is real and the others are stories. What would a renter lose by leaving you for a rival tomorrow? If it's nothing but an app, switching costs are thin. Which one would hurt a rival most if you had it today?"

**Why it works:** The user narrows it themselves when asked what a customer would actually lose.

### Edge case: no takeoff power fits yet

**User:** "We're the physio software. Switching costs maybe? Once their bookings and billing history are in us, moving is a pain."

**Say something like:**
"That's plausible to build toward. But at 30 clinics, it isn't material yet. Let's write it as the takeoff power to aim for, with counter-positioning as what protects you meanwhile. 'No Power yet' is a fine answer at nine months. You're tilting probabilities."

**Why it works:** The advisor doesn't invent a moat to be helpful, and the user leaves with a direction rather than a verdict.

## Phase 5: Name the One and What Would Show It's Forming

### Golden path

**User:** "Network economies. We'll watch the share of rentals in our top five counties. Next check when we pass 3,000 renters or in six months, whichever comes first."

**Say something like:**
"Good. Let's make the condition countable: in counties where you have twice the listings of your nearest rival, you win at least 80% of rentals. That's the line. Next check: 3,000 active renters or May, whichever comes first."

**Why it works:** The sign is something they can count, it's tied to relative scale, and the next check has both a trigger and a date.

### Struggle: wants to declare victory

**User:** "Honestly, I think we already have network economies. Can we just put that?"

**Say something like:**
"Maybe you do. If you think you already have it, run `benefit-and-barrier-test` on that claim. I'd want to know why it's a castle and not a shack before it goes in a deck. On this page, I'll write it as the power to aim for, with the sign that would confirm it."

**Why it works:** The claim is taken seriously and sent to the framework built to test it, without overstating it on this page.

### Edge case: can't name a condition

**User:** "I don't know what we'd measure for switching costs."

**Say something like:**
"Let me offer one, and you tell me if it's countable: clinics with more than a year of billing history in your system cancel at less than a quarter of the rate of clinics in their first year. Can you pull that number?"

**Why it works:** A specific condition gets offered for the user to accept or fix, instead of leaving the page with a vague goal.
