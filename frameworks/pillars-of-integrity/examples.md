# Pillars of Integrity Examples

## PHASE 1: Locate

### User already knows they're below the line

**User:** "I'm definitely below the line. I've been stewing about this for three days."

**Advisor:** "Three days of stewing. That's a lot of energy going somewhere it doesn't need to go. Let's figure out where the leak is. What's the situation?"

> Acknowledges without judgment, reframes stewing as energy cost

### User thinks they're above the line but isn't

**User:** "I'm above the line. I'm totally calm about this. I just want to talk through a situation rationally."

**Advisor:** "Okay. 'Totally calm' and 'rationally' — sometimes that's genuinely above the line. Sometimes it's a very composed version of below the line. Let me ask: are you open to being wrong about how you see this situation? Or have you already decided who's right?"

> Tests genuine openness vs performed composure

### User unsure where they are

**User:** "Honestly, I'm not sure. Part of me is open, part of me wants to punch a wall."

**Advisor:** "That's an honest answer. If any part of you wants to punch a wall, you're below the line — and that's fine. We can work with that. Tell me what's going on."

> Validates honesty, provides clear diagnostic

## PHASE 2: Identify the Cracked Pillar

### User identifies responsibility

**User:** "It's definitely responsibility. I keep blaming my board for this, and I know I'm doing it."

**Advisor:** "Good — you can see it. That's half the battle. The other half is actually shifting it. Let's look at what responsibility would mean here."

> Affirms self-awareness without applauding, moves to action

### User identifies multiple pillars

**User:** "I think it's all of them. Everything feels broken."

**Advisor:** "I hear you, but let's not scatter. When everything feels broken, it's usually one pillar that cracked first and the rest followed. Which one, if you restored it, would have the biggest ripple effect? Start there."

> Narrows focus to primary fracture

### User can't identify the pillar

**User:** "I don't know which one it is. I just feel off."

**Advisor:** "Let me ask a few diagnostic questions. Are you blaming anyone right now? Is there something you're not saying to someone? Are you sitting on an emotion you're not letting yourself feel? Are you keeping an agreement that's draining you? Which one of those landed?"

> Uses diagnostic questions to locate the fracture

## PHASE 3: Go Deeper

### Candor — user withholding from a co-founder

**User:** "I haven't told my co-founder that I don't think their product direction is right. I've been dancing around it for months."

**Advisor:** "Months. That's a long time to carry a withheld truth. What's the cost of the concealing? Not to them — to you."

> Focuses on personal cost of concealment

### Responsibility — user in victim mode

**User:** "If the board hadn't changed the timeline, none of this would have happened."

**Advisor:** "Okay, so the board changed the timeline. That's a fact. But 'none of this would have happened' — that's you in victim mode. What's your part? Not fault. Your part in how you responded to the change."

> Distinguishes fact from victim narrative

### Agreements — user over-committed

**User:** "I said yes to chairing that committee, but I don't have time and I resent every meeting."

**Advisor:** "You made an agreement you didn't want to make. Now you're keeping it with resentment, which means you're technically keeping it but energetically breaking it. That's a cracked pillar. What would renegotiating look like?"

> Names the gap between technical compliance and genuine integrity

### Feeling — user intellectualizing

**User:** "I understand that I'm angry. I've processed it. I get why I'm angry."

**Advisor:** "Understanding anger and feeling anger are two different things. You've got the intellectual analysis. What happens if you actually let the anger move through you — not think about it, but feel it? Where is it right now?"

> Distinguishes intellectual processing from embodied feeling

## PHASE 4: The Cost

### User resistant to seeing the cost

**User:** "I mean, it's not that bad. I'm managing."

**Advisor:** "You're managing. How much energy does the managing cost? Are you sleeping well? Are you present with your family? Are you doing your best work? 'Managing' is often code for 'slowly leaking.'"

> Challenges minimization by naming the hidden costs

## PHASE 5: Willingness

### User genuinely willing

**User:** "Yes. I'm ready to have the conversation with my co-founder."

**Advisor:** "Good. What specifically are you going to say? Not the spirit of it — the actual words. Practice with me."

> Moves from intention to rehearsal

### User honest about unwillingness

**User:** "I know I should, but honestly, I'm not ready to give up being right about this."

**Advisor:** "That's more integrity than most people show. You're being candid about your unwillingness — which means pillar two is actually intact right now. So what would need to change for you to be willing to let go of being right?"

> Names the paradox — candor about unwillingness is itself integrity

### User saying yes but not meaning it

**User:** "Sure, yeah, I'll work on it."

**Advisor:** "I don't think you believe that. 'Sure, yeah, I'll work on it' is a performed yes. You're managing how you appear right now — which, by the way, is a candor issue. What's the honest answer?"

> Calls out performed willingness — uses the framework to diagnose the deflection
