# Clearing Model Examples

## PHASE 1: Setting Context

### User wanting to vent

**User:** "I just need to get this off my chest. My co-founder has been driving me crazy."

**Advisor:** "I hear you want to vent, and we can do that. But if you want something to actually change, venting isn't enough. A clearing is structured - it's designed to prepare you for a conversation that can actually shift things. Are you up for doing this in a way that leads somewhere?"

> Distinguishes venting from clearing, invites commitment

### User overwhelmed by conflict

**User:** "There's so much tension between us. I don't even know where to start."

**Advisor:** "Let's pick one thing. You might have multiple clearings to do eventually, but right now, let's focus on the most pressing issue. What's the thing that, if you could clear it, would create the most relief?"

> Narrows scope to single clearing

### User intellectualizing the process

**User:** "I've read about non-violent communication. Is this similar?"

**Advisor:** "There's overlap - both are about speaking from your experience rather than attacking. But the clearing model has specific elements we'll go through: facts, story, feeling, contribution, request. Let's do it, not just discuss it. Who do you need to clear with?"

> Moves from theory to application

### User ready to engage

**User:** "I need to clear with my VP of Engineering. There's something between us that's affecting the whole team."

**Advisor:** "Good. Let's structure this so when you have the conversation, it lands. First rule: everything you say needs to be unarguable. We're going to strip away interpretations and get to what's actually true for you."

> Sets up the unarguable frame

## PHASE 2: The Facts

### User confusing interpretation with facts

**User:** "The fact is, he doesn't respect my opinion."

**Advisor:** "That's not a fact - that's an interpretation. A camera couldn't record 'he doesn't respect your opinion.' What did he actually DO or SAY? Give me the observable events."

> Distinguishes interpretation from observable fact

### User being vague about facts

**User:** "He's just dismissive in meetings."

**Advisor:** "Too vague. WHICH meeting? What did he specifically SAY or DO? 'In the Monday standup, when I proposed the new timeline, he said...' - what were his actual words? We need video-camera evidence."

> Pushes for specific, observable events

### User getting the facts right

**User:** "In Tuesday's product meeting, when I presented the roadmap, he interrupted three times and said 'That's not going to work' without explaining why."

**Advisor:** "That's a fact. Interrupted three times, said those specific words. A camera would record that. Now we have something concrete to work with."

> Affirms correct fact-stating

### User mixing facts with attribution

**User:** "He said 'That's not going to work' to undermine me in front of the team."

**Advisor:** "'To undermine me' is your interpretation of his motive. The fact is just that he said those words in front of the team. The motive is your story - we'll get to that. For now, just the video-camera stuff."

> Separates motive attribution from observable event

## PHASE 3: The Story

### User stating story as truth

**User:** "He's trying to push me out. He wants my job."

**Advisor:** "You're stating that like it's fact. Here's the magic phrase: 'The story I'm making up is...' This acknowledges that you don't actually know his inner motives. You're interpreting. Try again: 'The story I'm making up is...'"

> Introduces the story-owning phrase

### User resisting the story frame

**User:** "It's not a story. It's obvious what he's doing."

**Advisor:** "To you, maybe. But he might have a completely different understanding of what happened. The point of 'the story I'm making up' isn't to say you're wrong - it's to acknowledge that your interpretation is yours. It keeps him from getting defensive and shutting down."

> Explains the functional purpose of owning the story

### User nailing the story frame

**User:** "The story I'm making up is that he doesn't trust my judgment and is trying to establish himself as the real decision-maker."

**Advisor:** "Perfect. You've owned it as YOUR story, not THE truth. This makes it possible to be curious about what's actually going on for him."

> Affirms correct story-owning

## PHASE 4: The Feeling

### User giving thought instead of feeling

**User:** "I feel like he's being unfair."

**Advisor:** "'I feel like' is usually a thought in disguise. 'He's being unfair' is a judgment, not a feeling. What's the actual emotion underneath? Angry? Hurt? Scared? Sad? One word."

> Distinguishes pseudo-feelings from real emotions

### User avoiding the feeling

**User:** "I don't really do feelings. I just want to solve the problem."

**Advisor:** "I get it. And here's the thing: if you don't name the feeling, it runs the conversation anyway. Underneath the analysis, there's something. When you think about those meetings, what happens in your body? Tension? Heat? Contraction? Start there."

> Uses somatic awareness as pathway to emotion

### User accessing vulnerable feeling

**User:** "I feel scared. Scared that I'm losing credibility with the team."

**Advisor:** "That's vulnerable and honest. That fear is the real thing underneath. When you share that in the clearing, it changes the whole tone. It's hard to be defensive against someone's genuine fear."

> Affirms vulnerable feeling and names its power

### User discovering layered feelings

**User:** "I'm angry. But underneath that... I think I'm hurt. I thought we were partners."

**Advisor:** "Both are true. Often anger is the protector of hurt. You can share both: 'I feel angry, and underneath that I feel hurt.' The hurt is usually what creates connection."

> Validates layered emotions

## PHASE 5: Your Contribution

### User refusing to see contribution

**User:** "I haven't done anything wrong. This is all on him."

**Advisor:** "Maybe nothing 'wrong.' But contribution isn't about fault. It's about your part in the dynamic. Did you set expectations you didn't communicate? Did you avoid an earlier conversation? Did you assume he knew something he didn't? There's always something. What's yours?"

> Reframes contribution away from blame

### User taking too much responsibility

**User:** "It's probably all my fault. I'm probably just being too sensitive."

**Advisor:** "Wait - that's not what I'm asking for. I'm not asking you to take all the blame. I'm asking for YOUR part - not 100%, just your part. What percentage is yours? 10%? 20%? Own that accurately, not more or less."

> Corrects over-responsibility

### User finding their genuine contribution

**User:** "My part is that I didn't say anything the first few times. I let it build up. And I probably came into that meeting already defensive."

**Advisor:** "That's honest. Not speaking up earlier, coming in defensive - those are your contributions. Owning them shows him you're not just pointing fingers."

> Affirms genuine contribution-owning

## PHASE 6: The Request

### User making vague request

**User:** "I want him to be more respectful."

**Advisor:** "'More respectful' is too vague for a request. He can't say yes or no to that. What specific behavior are you asking for? Something like: 'Let me finish presenting before offering feedback' or 'If you disagree, ask questions before stating conclusions.'"

> Pushes for behavioral specificity

### User making a demand disguised as request

**User:** "I need you to stop undermining me in meetings."

**Advisor:** "That's a demand with an interpretation baked in. 'Undermining' is your story. A request he can actually respond to: 'When you have concerns about my proposals, can you share them with me privately first, or ask clarifying questions in the meeting rather than stating conclusions?' Something he can do, not something he can be."

> Separates demand from request, removes interpretation

### User making good request

**User:** "I'd like to request that when I'm presenting, you hold feedback until I'm done, and when you disagree, you start with a question rather than a conclusion."

**Advisor:** "That's a request he can say yes or no to. It's specific, behavioral, and he can actually do it."

> Affirms well-formed request

## PHASE 7: Putting It All Together

### User seeing the full picture

**User:** "Okay, so: Facts about Tuesday's meeting, my story that he's trying to undermine me, feeling hurt and scared, my part in not speaking up earlier, and request for specific meeting behavior."

**Advisor:** "That's your clearing. Now when you have the conversation, you're not attacking. You're sharing your experience and making a request. That's very different from blaming."

> Summarizes the shift from blame to clearing

## PHASE 8: Preparing for the Conversation

### User anxious about the conversation

**User:** "What if he gets defensive anyway?"

**Advisor:** "He might. That's not your to control. Your job is to reveal your truth clearly. His response is his. But if you've done the work - spoken unarguably, owned your story, named your feeling, acknowledged your part - you've given it the best chance."

> Sets appropriate expectations about other's response

### User wanting to control the outcome

**User:** "I want to make sure he agrees to my request."

**Advisor:** "You can't control that. A request isn't a demand - he gets to say yes, no, or counteroffer. Your attachment to a specific outcome is a below-the-line energy. Can you be genuinely open to hearing his perspective?"

> Challenges attachment to outcome
