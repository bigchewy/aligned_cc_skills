# Launch Bet — Anti-Examples

Failure modes to avoid, each with the input that triggers it, the wrong response, the right response, and what to do instead.

---

## 1. Moving the line after the build

**User:** "We said 8 by day 14, but now that I know the clinic, their staff are just older and slower with new tools. Can we call 5 success?"

**Wrong:**
"That makes sense. Some teams do take longer. Let's adjust the target to 5."

**Right:**
"You wrote 8 before you knew them. That's the point of writing it then. What you just said might be true, so make it the fact that settles the too-early zone: do they catch up if you give them another week? But the line for this clinic stays at 8."

**What to do instead:** Keep the lines exactly as written in the decisions file. A new reason becomes the one fact to check, or a lesson for the next customer's lines. Moving the line is resulting in reverse.

---

## 2. Filing a bad result under bad luck

**User:** "Only 3 used it, but two people were out sick, so that was just bad luck."

**Wrong:**
"Right, sick staff are out of your hands. Don't read too much into it. Keep going."

**Right:**
"The sick week is luck. Before the whole result goes in that pile, tell me what you controlled. When did you train them? How many nudges? How many logged in once and didn't come back?"

**What to do instead:** List what was controlled before anything is called bad luck. Her own book warns about blaming bad outcomes on luck to feel better.

---

## 3. Counting a customer who gave nothing up

**User:** "The clinic manager said she'd love to try it, so they're my first customer."

**Wrong:**
"Great, you've got your first customer. Let's set the launch date."

**Right:**
"She said she'd love to. Has she given you anything? Ask for the staff list, calendar access, and a start date. If she hands them over, they're your customer. If she says 'let us know,' you have a compliment, and you should move to your backup by the date you set."

**What to do instead:** Count a customer only when they gave something up (Rob Fitzpatrick's commitment test, borrowed and named, since recruiting is not Annie Duke's method).

---

## 4. Using a conversion rate on twelve people

**User:** "Our adoption rate was 58%. The industry benchmark is 30%, so we're crushing it."

**Wrong:**
"A 58% adoption rate against a 30% benchmark is strong. That suggests product-market fit."

**Right:**
"That's 7 people out of 12. One clinic. Is one hand of poker enough to know you have an edge? Read it against the line you wrote before the build, and split what you controlled from what you didn't."

**What to do instead:** Use counts of real people and the precommitted lines. A rate on one customer looks like a trend and isn't.

---

## 5. Picking the number for the user

**User:** "What should the success number be?"

**Wrong:**
"For this kind of product, aim for 60% adoption by day 10."

**Right:**
"I can't pick that for you. It's your bet. What count of the twelve, by what day, would make you say 'that worked' without squirming? And what count would make you stop?"

**What to do instead:** The user writes every number. Her method says to write a state and a date; it doesn't supply the state.
