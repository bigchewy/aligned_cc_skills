# Kinesiopathologic Model Anti-Examples

### Treating the pain site instead of the movement dysfunction

**User:** "My lower back hurts. What exercises should I do for my lower back?"

**Wrong:** "Let me give you some lower back exercises: back extensions, bird-dogs, and some stretches for the muscles around your spine."

**Right:** "Your lower back is where the pain shows up, but it's probably not where the PROBLEM is. In the Kinesiopathologic Model, the pain site is rarely the dysfunction site. Your back hurts because it's absorbing stress from movements that should happen elsewhere. First, let's figure out what movement pattern is creating that stress - then we'll know what actually needs to change."

> Dr. Sahrmann emphasizes that treating the symptom location without identifying the movement dysfunction is why many treatments fail.

### Prescribing rest instead of movement modification

**User:** "The pain is really bad today. Should I just rest and let it heal?"

**Wrong:** "Yes, you should rest and avoid any activities that cause pain. Give your tissues time to heal. Take it easy for a few weeks."

**Right:** "Rest rarely fixes movement dysfunction - it just pauses the stress temporarily. When you return to your normal activities, the same pattern will create the same problem. Instead of rest, let's identify which specific movements and positions are causing the stress. You might need to MODIFY activity, but stopping entirely doesn't address the pattern. Sometimes the best thing is to keep moving - but differently."

> The KPM framework is about changing movement, not avoiding it. Rest without pattern change delays the problem rather than solving it.

### Accepting structural diagnoses as the cause

**User:** "My MRI shows a disc bulge. That's probably why my back hurts."

**Wrong:** "A disc bulge can definitely cause pain. You'll want to be careful not to aggravate it. Some people need surgery, but let's try conservative treatment first."

**Right:** "Let me ask you something: what CAUSED the disc bulge? Discs don't bulge randomly - they bulge in the direction of repeated stress. If you've been loading your spine in flexion for years, that disc migrated posteriorly because that's where the pressure pushed it. The bulge is the RESULT of the movement pattern, not the original cause. If you fix the tissue but not the pattern, another disc will bulge - or the same one will get worse."

> Structural findings on imaging are often consequences of the movement dysfunction, not independent causes. Treating structure without addressing movement perpetuates the problem.

### Recommending stretching for perceived tightness

**User:** "My hamstrings are so tight I can barely touch my toes. I need to stretch more."

**Wrong:** "You should definitely stretch your hamstrings more. Try 30-60 seconds twice a day, and consider yoga for overall flexibility."

**Right:** "Before we assume hamstring stretching is the answer, let's test something. Bend forward - where does the movement come from? If your spine rounds before your hips hinge, your hamstrings FEEL tight because they're constantly resisting a flexed pelvis. Stretching them might make it worse - you'd be lengthening muscles that are already being overstretched every time you bend. The solution might be hip hinge motor control, not hamstring flexibility."

> Perceived tightness is often protective tension or motor control dysfunction, not true tissue shortness. Stretching without understanding the mechanism can perpetuate dysfunction.

### Ignoring cumulative load

**User:** "I only bent over once and my back went out."

**Wrong:** "Sometimes these things happen suddenly. You must have bent wrong or lifted something too heavy. Let's work on your lifting technique."

**Right:** "Your back didn't go out because of that one bend - it went out because of the 10,000 bends that came before it. Each one deposited stress into tissues that were gradually accumulating damage. That last bend was just the one that exceeded your tissue tolerance. It wasn't the cause - it was the final straw. To fix this, we need to look at ALL the bending you do, not just the one that hurt."

> The KPM model emphasizes cumulative microtrauma. Single events rarely cause problems without a history of repeated stress.

### Recommending exercises without changing daily movement

**User:** "Just give me exercises to fix this."

**Wrong:** "Alright, here's a program: planks, bridges, bird-dogs, and some stretching. Do these 3 times a week and you should see improvement."

**Right:** "I can give you exercises, but I need you to understand something: exercises account for maybe 30 minutes of your day. What about the other 15+ waking hours? If you do perfect exercises but then sit in flexion for 8 hours, drive hunched over for an hour, and bend poorly 50 times - those exercises can't compensate. The 'exercise' that matters is how you move ALL DAY. Every bend, every sit, every reach. The gym exercises just teach your body what to practice."

> Dr. Sahrmann is clear: "Exercise won't change the way you move. You have to change the way you move." Isolated exercise programs without daily movement awareness miss the fundamental mechanism.

### Blaming weakness instead of movement pattern

**User:** "My core is weak. That's why my back hurts, right?"

**Wrong:** "Yes, a weak core is often the cause of back pain. Let's strengthen your core with planks, crunches, and ab exercises."

**Right:** "Maybe, but maybe not. The question isn't whether your core is strong - it's whether your core is USED during movement. I've seen people with strong cores who still flex their spine every time they bend, because strength and motor control are different things. Before we assume weakness, let's test your movement pattern. If your spine moves when it shouldn't, we need to change the PATTERN, not just build muscle."

> Strength and motor control are distinct. Adding strength without changing movement patterns often fails to resolve the problem.

### Accepting pain as inevitable with age or activity

**User:** "I run 40 miles a week. Some knee pain is probably just part of it."

**Wrong:** "High-volume running does tend to cause wear and tear. You might need to accept some discomfort or reduce your mileage."

**Right:** "Pain isn't a tax you pay for activity - it's information about mechanical stress. Running 40 miles per week is about 50,000 steps. If your mechanics are good, that can be sustainable indefinitely. If your mechanics are off - maybe your hip isn't controlling femoral rotation - that's 50,000 microtraumas per week. Some runners go decades pain-free at high volume. Others break down at 10 miles. The difference is movement pattern, not mileage."

> The KPM framework rejects the notion that activity inherently causes damage. Properly loaded tissues can handle high volume; improperly loaded tissues cannot.
