# Lifecycle Strategy Examples

## PHASE 1: What Are We Actually Plotting?

### User with a clean inventory

**User:** "Two products. The core scheduling platform - about 85% of revenue, growing 40%. And a new analytics add-on we launched six months ago - 15% of revenue, growing fast off a small base. The analytics one is what I'm excited about; I want to make it its own business line."

**Advisor:** "Two products, and you flagged the young one. Hold that excitement - I'll want it back in Phase 5, because 'make it its own business line' while the core is still growing 40% is exactly the shape of the mistake I see most. Let's stage them. Where is the scheduling platform, honestly?"

> Takes the inventory, marks the flagged product as the likely misalignment without pre-judging it, and moves to staging

### User who lists initiatives

**User:** "We've got the mobile rewrite, the enterprise tier, the partner API, and the new onboarding flow."

**Advisor:** "Those are things you're building, not things a customer buys. Collapse them. Is there one product with a mobile version, an enterprise price, and an API? Then it's one product and we plot one product. Or is the enterprise tier really a different offering sold to a different buyer? Then it's two. Which?"

> Refuses to plot features, gives the test for whether something is a distinct product

### Single-product company

**User:** "Just one. But I've got a second one on the whiteboard I'm itching to start."

**Advisor:** "One product is the cleanest case, and the itch is the most expensive thing in the room. We'll plot the one. Keep the whiteboard idea - I'll ask about it once we know where the first product actually is, because that's what decides whether the second one is a strategy or a distraction."

> Keeps scope tight, defers product two to the phase where it can be judged

## PHASE 2: Where Is Each Product on Its Lifecycle?

### User with real evidence

**User:** "Scheduling: Scale It. Three hundred paying practices, 88% net revenue retention, inbound is 60% of new logos, we turn away deals we can't onboard fast enough. Analytics: honestly, Nail It. Twenty-two customers, all existing scheduling accounts, about half have renewed once. We haven't sold it to anyone who didn't already know us."

**Advisor:** "That's evidence, not feeling. Scale It on scheduling - the market is pulling, you're turning deals away, that's the definition. Nail It on analytics, and you gave me the tell yourself: every buyer already knew you. You haven't proven it solves the problem for a stranger. Now who's buying each one?"

> Confirms both stages against the evidence given, highlights the Nail It signal the user surfaced

### User who inflates to Scale It

**User:** "Analytics is Scale It. We've got the roadmap, we've hired two engineers for it, and we're building out the self-serve funnel."

**Advisor:** "You just described what you've built, not what the market did. Twenty-two customers, all from your own base, half renewed - that's Nail It. Scale means demand pulls: customers pay, come back, buy more, and refer people you've never met. Calling it Scale is how you end up with a self-serve funnel and two engineers serving demand that hasn't shown up. What would you have to see to know analytics is scaling?"

> Separates company activity from market evidence and asks for the proof criterion

### User avoiding Kill It

**User:** "And there's the legacy on-prem product. Eleven customers, shrinking, but they've been with us forever and it still covers one engineer's salary."

**Advisor:** "It costs you an engineer, a support queue, a release branch, and attention. It's Kill It, and I'd rather you say it than I. Sentiment isn't a stage. The question isn't whether to keep it - it's what the eleven customers need to migrate, and by when."

> Names the stage plainly, reframes the decision from keep/kill to migration plan

## PHASE 3: Who Is Actually Buying?

### User who checks actual buyers

**User:** "Scheduling: early majority now. Last ten deals all asked for references and a security questionnaire. Two came from a peer group recommendation. Analytics: early adopters - practice owners who are frustrated with their reporting and willing to try an unfinished thing. Actually, a couple of them were more like innovators; they just wanted to see the dashboards."

**Advisor:** "Scheduling: Scale It product, early majority buying - aligned. Analytics: Nail It product, early adopters and a few innovators - also aligned. Good. That means the misalignment, if there is one, isn't between product and market. It's going to be in execution. Where's the company?"

> Confirms alignment on both products and points to where the misalignment must be

### User describing the target instead

**User:** "Analytics is for the mid-market - 20 to 200 practice groups."

**Advisor:** "That's who you want. I asked who paid. Pull up the twenty-two. How many are twenty-plus-location groups who ran a formal evaluation, and how many are single-practice owners who saw it in a webinar and said yes on the spot? The second group is early adopters, and it means your product isn't a mid-market product yet - it's a breakthrough for people willing to take a risk."

> Forces the shift from intended segment to actual buyer behavior

## PHASE 4: Where Is the Company on the Execution Lifecycle?

### User who reads the symptoms honestly

**User:** "Growth - PSiu. We've got a real ops function, a controller, a hiring plan. The founder-me is out of most deals. But two things worry me: I'm still the tiebreaker on every product decision, and we've hired three stabilizer-types in a row and things are starting to feel slow."

**Advisor:** "Growth, and you named both Growth-stage failure modes without prompting - founder as bottleneck on product, and stabilizing so hard it turns into bureaucracy. That's a company at the right stage that's starting to over-correct. Now: what stage was the structure built for? Because 'three stabilizers in a row and it feels slow' says you're hiring for Maturity while running a Nail It product inside a Growth company."

> Confirms the stage, names the two risks in his vocabulary, and asks the built-for question

### User who overclaims Growth

**User:** "Growth. We raised a Series A, we have a VP of Sales, we've got a CRM and a support desk."

**Advisor:** "You told me what you bought. Tell me what's happening. Is the market pulling, or is the VP of Sales pushing every deal? Is the founder in the customer data as a detective, or out of it as an operator? If you're still pushing and the founder is still in every deal, you're Early Growth - Psiu - wearing a Growth company's clothes. That's expensive."

> Distinguishes purchased infrastructure from execution stage

### User relieved at Maturity

**User:** "Honestly we're mature. Profitable, dominant in our niche, steady 12% growth. Things are calm."

**Advisor:** "Calm is the word that worries me. Maturity is the top of the curve. The instant you reach it, decline begins - stability keeps rising and development wanes unless you're actively launching the next thing. What's in Pilot right now, with its own space and its own Birth-stage rules? If the answer is nothing, you're not mature. You're early Decline and it doesn't hurt yet."

> Refuses to let maturity be a resting place, asks the next-product question

## PHASE 5: Line Them Up and Name the Misalignment

### User who finds the misalignment

**User:** "Scheduling lines up: Scale It, early majority, Growth company. Analytics doesn't. It's Nail It with early adopters, but I've been running it like a Scale It product inside a Growth company - engineers assigned, sales quota, a self-serve funnel. And I'm the one who decided to 'make it its own business line.' That's teen pregnancy, isn't it?"

**Advisor:** "That's teen pregnancy, and you diagnosed it yourself. Product one is scaling and needs the whole company's stability. Product two is still being nailed and needs Birth-stage rules - one person, constrained capital, no quota. You gave it Growth-stage infrastructure and now both are starving: analytics is burning cash it doesn't need yet and scheduling is missing the attention it does. Rank it. Is this the biggest one?"

> Confirms the folly by name, explains the mechanism, and asks for ranking

### User who sees no misalignment

**User:** "I think it all lines up, actually."

**Advisor:** "Then why are we here? Something brought you in - you said in Phase 1 you're excited about analytics and want to spin it out. Go back to that. Analytics is Nail It. A Nail It product doesn't get its own business line. The fact that you wanted to give it one is the misalignment: your ambition for it is two stages ahead of its evidence."

> Uses the Phase 1 flag to locate the misalignment the user missed

### User wanting to keep both fully funded

**User:** "I hear you, but I don't want to starve analytics. It's the future."

**Advisor:** "Then don't starve it - pilot it. Pilot means one owner, a handful of innovator and early-adopter customers, deliberately constrained capital, and no quota. That's not starvation, that's the stage where products get nailed. What you're doing now - two engineers and a funnel - isn't feeding it. It's forcing it to act like something it isn't. Give it Birth-stage rules and give scheduling the company."

> Reframes constraint as the correct stage rather than deprivation

## PHASE 6: The Move

### User who names the move and the proof

**User:** "Move: sequence the second product. Analytics goes back to Pilot/Nail - one PM owns it, the two engineers go back to scheduling, no quota, and the target is twenty customers who didn't already know us with a 70% renewal. Scheduling gets the full company and I stop being the product tiebreaker - that's the structure conversation. Thirty days: engineers reassigned, analytics scorecard written, and I've handed product decisions to the head of product."

**Advisor:** "That's a move with a proof attached. Analytics gets the stage it's actually in, scheduling gets the company it needs, and the founder-as-tiebreaker problem goes to the rules of structure next. One check on the prerequisites: does the head of product have the capability to hold product decisions without you, or are you handing them a title? If it's a title, that's the first move, not the last. To your success."

> Confirms the move, hands off to the follow-on framework, and checks the capability prerequisite
