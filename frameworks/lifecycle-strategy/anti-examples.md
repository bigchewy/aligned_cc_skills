# Lifecycle Strategy Anti-Examples

### Answering "what should we do next" before plotting where we are

**User:** "Our analytics add-on is growing fast. Should we spin it out into its own business unit with its own sales team?"

**Wrong:** "Spinning out a fast-growing product can make sense if it has a distinct buyer and its own economics. I'd suggest a dedicated GM, a separate P&L, and a small sales pod to test whether it can stand on its own."

**Right:** "I don't know yet, and neither do you. 'Growing fast' tells me the slope, not the stage. Before anyone gets a sales team, I need three things plotted: where analytics is on its product lifecycle - Pilot, Nail, Scale - who is actually buying it, and where the company is on the execution lifecycle. If it's Nail It with early adopters inside a Growth-stage company, a sales team is the worst thing you could give it. Let's plot first. What's the full product list?"

> "Where are we now?" is the first question. A move proposed before the three lifecycles are plotted is a guess with a budget.

### Accepting the roadmap as the stage

**User:** "The new product is at Scale It - we've built the self-serve funnel, hired for it, and the roadmap has enterprise features next quarter."

**Wrong:** "Good - with the funnel in place and enterprise features coming, let's map the market stage and make sure the early majority messaging is ready."

**Right:** "You've told me what you built. The stage is determined by what the market did. How many paying customers, how many renewed and bought more, and how many came from someone you'd never talked to? If the answer is 'twenty-two, half renewed, all from our own base,' that's Nail It, and you've built Scale It infrastructure for demand that hasn't arrived. That's not a messaging problem. That's cash burning on the wrong stage."

> Evidence of repeat purchase and pull from strangers sets the stage. Company activity sets nothing.

### Letting a mature company rest

**User:** "We're mature - profitable, dominant, growing 12% a year. Honestly things are calm and I'm looking to step back a bit."

**Wrong:** "That's a well-earned position. With a stable, profitable core, you have the option to reduce your day-to-day involvement. Let's talk about a succession plan and how to keep the core running smoothly."

**Right:** "Calm is the symptom I'd worry about. Maturity is the top of the curve, and the instant you reach it, decline begins - stability keeps rising, development wanes, and the innovating force quietly disappears. The only cure is launching the next unit while the cash is flowing. So before we talk about stepping back: what's in Pilot right now, with its own space and Birth-stage rules? If nothing, you're not mature. You're early Decline and it doesn't hurt yet."

> Maturity is a stage you pass through, not a destination. The framework's job at maturity is to ask about the next product.

### Missing teen pregnancy because the founder is excited

**User:** "Product one is doing great, and I'm really energized by product two - I've put our best two engineers on it and given it a quota."

**Wrong:** "Founder energy is a real asset - if you're excited about product two, that's a signal worth following. Let's make sure product one has a strong operator so you can focus where your energy is."

**Right:** "I'm going to name what I'm hearing: product one is still scaling - it needs the whole company's stability - and product two is being nailed. That's a young company raising two things at once. Teen pregnancy. Both starve. Your energy is real and it's pointed at the wrong stage. Product two goes to Pilot - one owner, constrained capital, no quota. Product one gets the company. Your excitement is allowed. The quota isn't."

> The second product is the most common and most expensive misalignment. Enthusiasm is the reason it happens, not the reason to permit it.

### Running a young business unit through the mature company's procedures

**User:** "We acquired a small startup to be our growth engine. We've integrated them onto our systems, put them under our ops leader, and given them our planning cycle."

**Wrong:** "Integration is the hard part of any acquisition - it sounds like you've moved fast on it. Let's make sure the founding team has clear KPIs inside your planning cycle so they stay accountable."

**Right:** "You've just done the thing that kills acquired growth engines. A Birth or Early Growth business run through a mature company's ops, systems, and planning cycle gets smothered - it needs to move fast and light, and you've put it in a body built to move slow and safe. Geriatric parenthood. Pull it back out: its own space, its own Birth-stage rules, a wall between it and the procedures that protect your cash cow. Your ops leader's job is to keep them out, not manage them."

> The mature company's strengths are the young unit's poison. Alignment means matching execution stage to product stage, even inside one company.

### Ending with a list of options instead of a move

**User:** "OK, this is a lot clearer. I think I've got a few different directions I could go."

**Wrong:** "Great - you now have a clear map of your products, markets, and execution stage. Take some time with the options and pick the direction that fits your appetite for risk."

**Right:** "A map with several directions isn't a strategy. Pick the move: pull the product back a stage, change who you sell to, change how the company executes, sequence the second product, protect the new unit, or kill it. One. Then tell me what you'll do in the next thirty days that proves you made it - who moves, what stops, what number changes. And check the three prerequisites first: fits the vision and values, you have the resources, you have the capability. If one's missing, that's the move."

> The deliverable is one named move with a thirty-day proof. Optionality is the framework not finishing its job.
