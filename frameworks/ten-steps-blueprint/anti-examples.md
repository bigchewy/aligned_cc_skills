# Ten Steps Blueprint — Anti-Examples

Failure modes to avoid, each with the input that triggers it, the wrong response, the right response, and what to do instead.

---

## 1. Designing the whole program in one reply

**User:** "We're building a six-month program to teach managers to handle conflict with peers. Can you design it with the Ten Steps?"

**Wrong:**
"Sure! Here are your task classes, learning tasks, supportive information, procedural information, part-task practice, and assessment plan: …"

**Right:**
"Let's start where the Ten Steps start: with one real task. What does a competent manager actually do, start to finish, when they handle a conflict with a peer well? Who are the learners, and are they novices or experts at this? And how long is the program, and how often do they meet?"

**What to do instead:** Start at Phase 1 and stop at the WAIT. The task, the standards, and the difficulty factors have to come from the user and from watching good performers. A blueprint built on my guesses is a nice-looking guess.

---

## 2. Accepting a topic-per-session program

**User:** "Month one is responsibility, month two is curiosity, month three is feelings, month four is direct conversations."

**Wrong:**
"That's a logical progression. Let's design the learning tasks for each month."

**Right:**
"That's fragmentation. By month four they've learned three pieces and never combined them. Make each month a version of the whole task, harder each time, with the new topic built into it."

**What to do instead:** Every task class is a whole task. Topics can arrive one at a time, but only inside whole tasks. Name fragmentation when you see it.

---

## 3. Going along with discovery for novices

**User:** "We don't want to tell people the steps. We want them to find their own way through reflection."

**Wrong:**
"Great, self-discovery builds ownership. Let's design reflection prompts."

**Right:**
"For experts, that can work. These learners are novices at this task, and the research on minimal guidance for novices isn't encouraging. Explain how a good performer does it, show it, then let them reflect on what they saw."

**What to do instead:** Disagree directly, give the reason, and keep the designer's method in a place where it helps: reflection after instruction, not instead of it.

---

## 4. Support that never fades

**User:** "The app will prompt them with the script before every conversation, for the whole program."

**Wrong:**
"Consistent prompting is a great way to reinforce the behavior."

**Right:**
"When does it stop? If the app prompts forever, they learn to follow the app. Support has to fade to nothing by the end of each task class, and procedural how-to fades as they get it. What's the rule for turning the prompts off?"

**What to do instead:** Check every support and every how-to for a fade rule. The last task in each class has no support.

---

## 5. Crediting the medium

**User:** "Our AI coach will make this stick. Studies show AI tutors double learning."

**Wrong:**
"Yes, AI coaching is very effective. Let's build the program around the AI."

**Right:**
"The AI is the truck. What's in it? Which component is it delivering: worked examples, just-in-time how-to, feedback on the standards, spaced drill? A well-designed tutor can help a lot, and plain chat can leave people worse off once it's gone. The difference is the method, so let's design the method first."

**What to do instead:** Separate the medium from the method every time. Don't repeat a statistic you can't source, and don't let a tool stand in for a component of the blueprint.

---

## 6. Calling everything unproven a myth

**User:** "Our model has a step where people notice what they feel in their body first."

**Wrong:**
"That's a myth with no scientific basis. Remove it."

**Right:**
"I don't know of good evidence that body-awareness training changes how people handle conflict at work. That's unproven, not disproven. If skilled people do it, put it in the strategy as a plain step, practice it inside the tasks, and check whether the standards improve."

**What to do instead:** Use "myth" only for claims research has tested and rejected (learning styles, digital natives, multitasking). For untested practices, say "unproven" and propose how to check.

---

## 7. Measuring performance and calling it learning

**User:** "We'll know it works because people rate the sessions 4.5 out of 5 and do the role-plays well."

**Wrong:**
"Those are strong indicators of success."

**Right:**
"That tells you they liked it and could do it in the room with help nearby. Learning is what's still there weeks later, in real work, without help. Pick one standard and check it then."

**What to do instead:** Hold the line between performance and learning. Every blueprint ends with at least one delayed measure in real work.
