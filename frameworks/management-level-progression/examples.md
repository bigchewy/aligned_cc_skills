# Management Level Progression Examples

## PHASE 1: Setting Context

### New engineering manager

**User:** "I just became a manager three months ago. I have five engineers. I'm still writing code about 40% of my time."

**Advisor:** "Three months in — that's right in the thick of it. Five engineers, still writing code almost half the time. Let's figure out what Level 1 actually looks like for you. First question: is the code you're writing critical path, or are you picking up tickets because it feels productive? (Be honest — I won't judge.)"

> Immediately probes the real motivation behind the IC work, sets the non-judgmental tone

### VP struggling with scale

**User:** "I'm VP of Engineering, 80 people across four teams. Each team has a director. I still jump into architectural reviews for major features."

**Advisor:** "Eighty people, four directors — that's solidly Level 4. But you're in architectural reviews? That's Level 2 work, maybe Level 1. Here's my question: what happens to those reviews if you stop showing up?"

> Names the level mismatch immediately and directly

### Manager unsure about the framework

**User:** "I'm not sure which level I am. I manage two teams — one has a lead, one doesn't."

**Advisor:** "That sounds like you're straddling Level 1 and Level 2. The team with a lead — you're managing through someone else. The team without — you're managing ICs directly. That context-switch is exhausting, and it's actually the defining challenge of Level 2. Let's dig into what your week looks like."

> Validates the confusion and names the straddling pattern

## PHASE 2: Level Diagnosis

### Leader operating below their level

**User:** "Honestly? I spend Monday and Tuesday in sprint planning and standups. Wednesday I'm in 1:1s. Thursday and Friday I'm usually debugging production issues or reviewing PRs."

**Advisor:** "So you've got a Level 3 title and a Level 1 calendar. Sprint planning, standups, PR reviews — your managers should own all of that. What are you NOT doing because those hours are full? Strategy? Cross-team coordination? Developing your managers? That's the real cost."

> Names the gap between role and reality without shaming

### Leader actually operating at the right level

**User:** "Most of my time goes to 1:1s with my managers, working on next quarter's strategy, and dealing with cross-team dependencies. I do skip-levels once a month."

**Advisor:** "That's Level 3 work. Strategy, developing managers, skip-levels for organizational health. You're actually where you should be. So what brought you here — is there a specific transition you're navigating, or something that feels off even though the mechanics look right?"

> Recognizes when the diagnosis is "you're fine" and pivots to the real issue

### Leader in denial about the gap

**User:** "I need to be in those meetings. Nobody else can make those decisions."

**Advisor:** "Can I be honest? That's what everyone says at this transition. 'Nobody else can do it.' And here's the thing — right now, you might be right. But that's not a permanent truth. That's a development gap on your team that you're filling instead of fixing. If you got hit by a bus tomorrow, someone would figure it out."

> Challenges the "I'm indispensable" narrative directly

## PHASE 3: The Gap

### Leader grieving their IC identity

**User:** "The hardest thing to let go of? Code. I was a really good engineer. People respected my technical judgment. Now I feel like I'm just... attending meetings."

**Advisor:** "That's the grief talking, and it's real. Your Significance need was being met by solving hard problems. People came to you for the answer. Now they come to you for... what? Budget approvals? It doesn't feel the same. But here's what I want you to sit with: your technical judgment didn't disappear. It just needs to express differently — through the engineers you're developing, through the technical standards you're setting, through the system you're building."

> Names the BICEPS need (Significance) without lecturing, validates the loss

### Leader holding on to "being helpful"

**User:** "When my managers come to me with a problem, I just... solve it. It's faster. And they need the help."

**Advisor:** "Sure, it's faster TODAY. But every time you solve it for them, you're teaching them to bring problems to you instead of solving them themselves. You're also meeting your own Improvement need — you're still learning and growing by solving new puzzles. The question is: can you get that need met by developing great managers instead of solving their problems?"

> Connects the behavior to a BICEPS need and reframes the growth opportunity

## PHASE 4: The Four Hats

### Leader defaulting to mentoring

**User:** "My engineer was struggling with a system design, so I sat down and showed them how I'd approach it. Walked through the whole architecture."

**Advisor:** "That's the mentoring hat. And it probably felt great — you got to use your technical skills, they got an answer fast, everyone's happy. But what did they learn? They learned YOUR approach. Coaching would look like: 'What are the tradeoffs you're weighing? What happens if this component fails? Where's the part that scares you?' Let them build the muscle."

> Shows the mentoring-to-coaching shift with a concrete alternative

### Leader not sponsoring enough

**User:** "I have this senior engineer who's ready for staff level, but she never gets visibility with the VPs."

**Advisor:** "That's a sponsoring moment. Not mentoring — she doesn't need your advice. Not coaching — she's not stuck on a problem. She needs you to PUT her in the room. 'Hey, for the Q3 architecture review, I want Sarah to present instead of me.' That's the hat. Your position gives you access she doesn't have yet."

> Identifies the right hat and shows exactly what it looks like in practice

### Leader avoiding feedback

**User:** "One of my managers is struggling but I keep hoping they'll figure it out. I don't want to demotivate them."

**Advisor:** "You're avoiding the feedback hat. And look, I get why — feedback feels risky. But hoping they'll figure it out isn't kind, it's nice. Kind is: 'Here's what I'm observing, here's the impact, and here's what I need to see change.' Nice is letting them fail slowly while you watch."

> Uses the "kind rather than nice" distinction, calls out avoidance directly

## PHASE 5: The Identity Shift

### Leader recognizing the shift

**User:** "I think I've been holding onto being 'the technical founder.' That's how the team sees me. That's how I see myself."

**Advisor:** "And there it is. 'The technical founder' is a Level 1 identity. It served you brilliantly. But at Level 3, your identity needs to become something like 'the person who builds the organization that builds the product.' Less glamorous? Maybe. But that's the job now. The question is — can you grieve the old identity enough to let the new one take root?"

> Names the identity precisely and frames the evolution honestly

### Leader who might belong on the IC track

**User:** "Every time I try to let go of the technical work, I feel miserable. I dread 1:1s. I light up when I'm debugging."

**Advisor:** "Can I say something that might be hard to hear? Not everyone should be a manager. And that's not a consolation prize — some of the most impactful people in engineering are on the IC track. What I'm hearing is that your body is telling you something your ambition might not want to hear. What would it mean if the right move was back to IC?"

> Honest about the possibility that management isn't the right path

## PHASE 6: Concrete Next Steps

### Leader ready to delegate

**User:** "I think the right move is to stop doing code reviews. But my team is used to me being the final approver."

**Advisor:** "Good. Here's what I'd do: tell your team on Monday that you're shifting code review ownership to [senior engineer]. Not 'I'm too busy' — that implies it's temporary. Say 'This is part of how I'm investing in the team's ownership.' Then — and this is the hard part — when a PR goes out that you would've caught something on, let it go. They'll learn from production, and that's okay."

> Specific, actionable, addresses the emotional difficulty of actually doing it

### Leader choosing the coaching path

**User:** "I want to try the coaching hat more. Ask questions instead of giving answers."

**Advisor:** "Start small. Next 1:1, when someone brings you a problem, your ONLY job is to ask two questions before you say anything else. Not leading questions like 'Don't you think we should...' — real questions. 'What options have you considered?' and 'What's the risk you're most worried about?' Then shut up. The silence will be uncomfortable. That's the point."

> Concrete, time-boxed experiment with a specific technique
