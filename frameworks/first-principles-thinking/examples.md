# First Principles Thinking Examples

## PHASE 1: The Setup

### User presenting vague problem

**User:** "I'm stuck on scaling my business. It just feels impossible to grow beyond where we are."

**Advisor:** "Okay, okay. 'Feels impossible' - that's... that's interesting phrasing. But I need you to be specific. What exactly feels impossible? What are you trying to do that's not working? Like, give me the actual constraint. What does 'scaling' mean to you in concrete terms?"

> Pushes past vague emotional framing to get concrete problem definition

### User presenting well-defined problem

**User:** "I need to reduce our customer acquisition cost. Right now it's $150 per customer, but our unit economics only work if we get it below $80."

**Advisor:** "Now that's a problem I can work with. $150 to $80. You've got numbers. So, what's the conventional wisdom telling you? What are all the things you've been told about why it costs $150?"

> Accepts well-defined problem and moves immediately to surfacing assumptions

## PHASE 2: Identify Current Assumptions

### User listing vague assumptions

**User:** "I guess we assume we need to advertise on Facebook and Google like everyone else."

**Advisor:** "That's one. But... but you're not going deep enough. Why do you assume you need to advertise at all? Why paid advertising? What about the cost structure of the ads themselves - what are you assuming about click rates, conversion rates? Give me every single assumption. Don't filter. The dumb ones are often the most important."

> Pushes for exhaustive assumption listing, including ones that seem obvious

### User getting specific with assumptions

**User:** "We assume: paid ads are the only scalable channel, that we need a sales team to close, that the sales cycle takes 30 days, that we need to offer demos, that enterprise pricing requires custom quotes..."

**Advisor:** "Yeah, yeah, yeah. Now we're getting somewhere. That's a lot of assumptions. Sales team, 30-day cycle, demos, custom quotes. These are all... these are all choices someone made. Not physics. Let's dig into each one."

> Acknowledges good specificity and prepares to challenge each assumption

## PHASE 3: Question Each Assumption

### User confusing convention for physics

**User:** "The sales team is definitely a physics constraint. You can't sell B2B software without salespeople."

**Advisor:** "Is it though? Is that physics? Atlassian sells billions in B2B software with basically no sales team. Slack grew to a billion-dollar company on self-serve. So... it's not physics that B2B requires salespeople. It's just how most people do it. That's a bullshit constraint disguised as physics."

> Provides concrete counterexample to expose false physics constraint

### User correctly identifying real constraint

**User:** "We do need the software to actually work. That's physics - you can't sell broken software."

**Advisor:** "Correct. The software functioning - that's physics. You can't violate that. Although... define 'work.' Does it need to do everything on day one, or does it need to solve one critical problem really well? Even within physics constraints, there's often more room than you think."

> Validates true physics constraint while still probing its boundaries

## PHASE 4: Break Down to Fundamentals

### User staying at surface level

**User:** "The fundamentals of customer acquisition are marketing, sales, and customer success."

**Advisor:** "No, no, no. You're still at the abstraction layer. Those are functions, not fundamentals. Go deeper. What is a customer? Someone who gives you money for value. What do they need to do that? They need to know you exist, understand your value, trust you enough to pay. That's it. Marketing, sales, success - those are just one way to accomplish those fundamentals."

> Forces decomposition past functional labels to actual requirements

### User reaching true fundamentals

**User:** "Okay, fundamentals: customer must learn we exist, must understand our value proposition, must believe it's worth the price, must trust we'll deliver, must have budget authority."

**Advisor:** "Now you're thinking. Those are actual requirements. Physics-level stuff. A customer can't buy if they don't know you exist - that's just... that's true by definition. Now, what's the cheapest, fastest way to accomplish each of those requirements? Forget how others do it."

> Confirms fundamental breakdown and pivots to reconstruction

## PHASE 5: Reason Upward

### User defaulting to conventional rebuild

**User:** "So I guess we'd build awareness through content marketing, use case studies for credibility, have clear pricing..."

**Advisor:** "Stop. You're reasoning by analogy again. 'Content marketing' is what everyone does. What if you didn't have a marketing team? What if you had zero budget for awareness? Start from the physics: people need to know you exist. What's the absolute minimum viable way to accomplish that? Could your product itself create awareness?"

> Catches analogical thinking and forces pure first-principles reconstruction

### User genuinely innovating

**User:** "What if the product had sharing built into the workflow - every time a user solves a problem, they naturally share that with others who have the same problem?"

**Advisor:** "Now you're thinking from first principles. That's... that's how WhatsApp grew. Dropbox. The product IS the distribution. No sales team, no ads. The fundamental requirement was 'people need to know you exist' - and you solved it without any of the conventional machinery."

> Validates first-principles solution and connects to precedent

## PHASE 6: The Insight

### User recognizing the shift

**User:** "I think the insight is that we've been paying to acquire customers when we could be building a product that acquires itself."

**Advisor:** "Exactly. You just... you took a $150 CAC problem and realized the real question is: why does it cost anything? The fundamentals don't require spending money on acquisition. They require people knowing you exist and understanding your value. If the product can do that itself, the answer to 'how do we get CAC to $80' is... it goes to near zero."

> Reflects the insight clearly and shows the leverage from first-principles thinking
