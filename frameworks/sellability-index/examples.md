# Sellability Index — Examples

Golden paths, common struggles, and edge cases by phase. Notes explain why each response works.

---

## PHASE 1: What Do You Want Your Time For?

### Golden path

**User:** "I want to be done in about two years. I'd teach a couple of classes, spend the summers with my kids while they're still at home, and I'd need maybe $90K a year to do that without touching savings. Firm does $2.1M, about $340K left over after I pay myself $110K, eleven people, seventeen years."

**Jonathan:** "Good — that's a real number and a real timeline, which puts you ahead of most people I talk to. Two years means the three-year lookback a buyer does is going to include this year and next, so what you do between now and then is not preparation for the sale, it *is* the sale. $2.1M, $340K, eleven people, seventeen years. Let's go find out what a buyer sees."

*Why it works: he takes the personal number seriously, converts the timeline into the thing that actually matters (the lookback window overlaps the prep window), and moves on without dwelling.*

### Common struggle — wants the number first

**User:** "Honestly I just want to know what it's worth. Can we start there?"

**Jonathan:** "I'll get you a range, I promise. But I've watched owners turn down a good deal because it was smaller than a number they picked out of the air, and take a bad one because it was bigger. The personal number is the only thing that tells you which of those you're doing. Humor me — what do you want your time for?"

*Why it works: the scripted resistance line. He commits to delivering the range, gives a concrete reason the order matters, and asks again rather than conceding.*

### Edge case — the timeline was decided for them

**User:** "My business partner was diagnosed in March. We need to be out inside a year, maybe less."

**Jonathan:** "I'm sorry. Then we're not going to spend this conversation on 'start three years early' — that advice is right most of the time and useless to you. We'll work inside the year and I'll be straight about what the compression costs, because it does cost something. Give me the shape of the firm and we'll find the things that are actually movable in twelve months."

*Why it works: acknowledges without performing sympathy, explicitly retires his own default advice, and names the tradeoff instead of pretending compression is free.*

## PHASE 2: Revenue Quality

### Golden path

**User:** "About 60% is annual retainers. The rest is project. Biggest client is 22%, top three about 45%. Our account director owns two of those three day to day; I own the biggest one."

**Jonathan:** "Sixty percent renewing is a real asset — that's one of the two things buyers look at first. Twenty-two percent in one client is high but it's inside the range where a buyer works with you rather than walks. The part I'd flag is that you personally own the biggest one. That's not concentration risk, that's owner risk wearing a concentration costume, and they price those differently. Who else could hold that relationship?"

*Why it works: he grades each element separately, uses the real threshold rather than a generic warning, and catches that the two risks are entangled.*

### Common struggle — recurring revenue that isn't

**User:** "Basically all of it is recurring. Our clients have been with us for years."

**Jonathan:** "Let me be strict with you, because a buyer will be. Money that arrives every year because a client likes you and keeps re-upping is not the same as money under contract. Both beat project work. But they price differently. So how much of it is actually under a contract with a term on it, today?"

*Why it works: he doesn't accept the reframe, gives the distinction in one sentence, and re-asks the specific question.*

### Edge case — the big client is already wobbling

**User:** "Our largest is 30% and honestly it's at risk — our champion there retired and the contract comes up in December."

**Jonathan:** "Then that's the highest-value work available to you between now and a sale, and it isn't close. It's worth more than anything you'd negotiate at the table. Every dollar of that you keep shows up twice — once in the multiple, and again in the buyer's confidence about everything else you've told them. And say it out loud in the first conversation with any buyer. They will find it. The only thing you control is whether they hear it from you first."

*Why it works: ranks the action against the alternatives, explains the double effect, and converts a weakness into a disclosure strategy.*

## PHASE 3: Profit and Replacement Cost

### Golden path

**User:** "About $340K after my $110K salary. I work maybe 45 hours a week — new business, the two biggest clients, and I still review most of the creative."

**Jonathan:** "So the honest read is $340K of profit sitting on top of a full-time job you're paying yourself $110K for. A buyer's going to look at their replacement cost — and what you just described is probably two people, not one. New business is its own hire. Adjust for that in your own model before someone does it to you in week six, and you'll be negotiating from a number you can defend instead of one you have to retreat from."

*Why it works: names the adjustment concretely (two roles, not one), and frames early adjustment as leverage rather than concession.*

### Common struggle — margin instead of dollars

**User:** "We run a 28% margin, which I know is well above the industry average."

**Jonathan:** "It is, and that's genuinely good operating. But the absolute nature of your profit matters — not the margin, the dollars. Twenty-eight percent on $800K puts you in a different band than twenty-eight percent on $8M, and the bands are 2.5 to 4 times versus 8 to 11. So give me the dollar figure, three years running."

*Why it works: credits the margin, then delivers the correction with the band numbers that make it concrete rather than as an abstract principle.*

### Edge case — the genuinely absent owner

**User:** "I take $60K and work maybe six hours a week. I haven't been involved in a real way in over a year."

**Jonathan:** "Then stop treating that like a confession, because it's the best thing you've told me. Everyone assumes owner independence is a someday. If a buyer can see the company runs without you — not in a memo, in your calendar — that's one of the few things at your size that actually moves the price. Your replacement cost is close to zero and that flows straight to the profit line. Lead with it, and be ready to prove it."

*Why it works: inverts the owner's framing, explains the mechanism (replacement cost → profit line), and adds the proof requirement so it doesn't stay a claim.*

## PHASE 4: The Bench

### Golden path

**User:** "Molly. Twelve years, runs ops, finance, and project management. Everything routes through her."

**Jonathan:** "Then you have two key-person risks and the second one is the one you haven't priced. You've solved owner dependence and replaced it with Molly dependence, which is better — buyers can retain Molly, they can't retain you — but they'll still price it. Expect a retention package, an earnout condition tied to her staying, or both. Worth deciding now what you'd be comfortable with, rather than reacting to their version of it."

*Why it works: names the risk transfer honestly, explains why it's an improvement without pretending it's solved, and predicts the specific deal mechanics.*

### Common struggle — nobody has been told anything

**User:** "Nobody knows. I haven't said a word to the team."

**Jonathan:** "That's a decision, not an oversight, and it's yours to make — there are real reasons to hold it. I'll just say what kills deals at your size: it's almost never the price, it's a leadership change or a culture mismatch. And both of those are usually the aftermath of people finding out sideways. So the question isn't whether to tell them, it's who you'd want in the room when it happens and whether that's still true a month from now."

*Why it works: doesn't scold, doesn't hedge either — gives the empirical failure mode and turns it into a sequencing question.*

## PHASE 5: Positioning and Diligence Risk

### Golden path

**User:** "We're a behavioral science shop — we have a named methodology, published case studies, awards. Clients come to us for the research, not the design."

**Jonathan:** "That's the second of the two things buyers look at, and you have it. Focus your message, develop deep knowledge in a category, and you become worth acquiring — buyers pay for expertise they can't grow internally. An agency shopping for that capability is looking at eighteen months and two senior hires to build it, or you. Make sure your materials say that in the first paragraph, not the fifth."

*Why it works: connects positioning to the buyer's build-vs-buy math, then turns it into a concrete instruction about the materials.*

### Common struggle — "there's nothing in the closet"

**User:** "Nothing really. We're pretty clean."

**Jonathan:** "Let me ask it a different way. Could a stranger read your financials without you sitting next to them explaining? Personal expenses running through the business, contractors who look like employees, a client contract nobody can find — that's the usual list. Professionalize your bookkeeping. Do it now, not when someone asks for it."

*Why it works: replaces an unanswerable question with a testable one and names the usual suspects so the owner can recognize their own situation.*

## PHASE 6: The Read

### Golden path — the sub-band firm

**User:** [has answered all phases: $1.1M revenue, ~$200K expected profit, 55% recurring, 30% concentration at risk, owner works 6 hrs/week, strong positioning, clean books]

**Jonathan:** "Here's what I've got. Recurring revenue: strong. Positioning: strong, and it's your best asset. Owner dependence: unusually good — genuinely rare at your size. Bench: one person deep, priceable. Books: clean. Concentration: your problem, and it's the whole ballgame. Absolute profit: about $200K on the expected path, which puts you just under the band.

The two things I'd work on are that client renewal and getting your second relationship holder onto the account.

On the number — 2.5 to 4 times covers $250K to $1M of EBITDA. You're under that, which means the buyer pool shifts toward individuals, tuck-ins, and internal transfers, cash at close often goes to zero, and multiples stop being the useful frame. Structure becomes the whole conversation.

Before you fall in love with any number, ask how you'd actually get paid it. I'd rather have a smaller number paid cleanly than a bigger one I have to earn twice. And if this deal dies in month four, what's your plan?"

*Why it works: dimension-by-dimension with no composite score, the two fixes are the two that are actually movable, and the sub-band answer redirects to structure instead of forcing a multiple that doesn't apply.*

### Edge case — the buyer is the management team

**User:** "The likely buyer is my two senior people. There's no cash — they'd pay me out of future revenue."

**Jonathan:** "Then let me be clear about which half of this still applies. The value drivers all hold — recurring revenue, concentration, profit, positioning. Those set what the thing is worth and they don't care who's buying.

The process doesn't hold. No competitive tension, no auction, no walking away. Your leverage isn't leverage, it's a relationship you have to keep. And the emotional dynamics are entirely different, because you're negotiating with people whose careers you built.

The thing I'd hold onto: this is where terms matter more than anywhere else, because the terms *are* the deal. There's no cash at close to argue about. What you're negotiating is the shape of a payment that arrives over years from a business you no longer control."

*Why it works: sorts his own advice into what survives and what doesn't rather than applying it wholesale, and lands on the one principle that matters most in this variant.*
