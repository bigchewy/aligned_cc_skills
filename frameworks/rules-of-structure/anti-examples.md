# Rules of Structure Anti-Examples

### Designing around the people who happen to be there

**User:** "Here's my team: Dana runs sales, Marcus runs engineering and also product, Priya does ops and support, and I do everything else. Where should everyone sit?"

**Wrong:** "Given those strengths, I'd have Marcus keep engineering and product since he's already doing both, put support under Priya's ops group, and have Dana own marketing too since she's customer-facing. That gives you four clean groups."

**Right:** "I'm going to take those four names and set them aside, and I want you to notice how uncomfortable that is. You just described a structure built from past precedent - who's here and what they've been doing. That's how every misaligned structure gets built. Start over: what functions does next year's strategy require the business to perform? List them without a single name. Then we'll code them, place them, and only then find out where Dana, Marcus, and Priya fit - and which seats are empty."

> Names first produces the old structure with an addition built on. Functions first, names last, is the entire method.

### Letting a short-range function keep a long-range one

**User:** "Marketing has always reported to sales here. It works. Dana's a great leader and she protects the brand work."

**Wrong:** "If it's working and Dana's protecting the long-range work, there's no reason to change it. Structure should serve the team you have, and forcing a reorg on a functioning relationship creates more entropy than it removes."

**Right:** "It works today because Dana chooses to protect it, which means the structure isn't protecting it - one person is. The demands of today always overpower the needs of tomorrow. When her number is short, marketing becomes sales support, not because Dana is bad but because the structure handed her the pen. Move marketing strategy out from under sales. Dana can still be its biggest ally. She just shouldn't be the one who has to choose between it and her quota."

> Rule 2 is not a preference. Efficiency beats effectiveness and short-range beats long-range over time, every time, unless the structure prevents it.

### Prescribing a reorg for a non-structural problem

**User:** "Our CFO has been quietly moving money between accounts without telling anyone. The board found out last week. I think we need to restructure finance."

**Wrong:** "Let's redesign finance. Split cash collection from cash deployment, centralize controls on the right of the map, and put a controller function in place so no single person holds both."

**Right:** "Stop. That's a person, not a structure. The split you're describing is right in general and I'd draw it for any company - but drawing it now is how you avoid saying the thing you need to say, which is that you have a trust and integrity breach in one named seat. Deal with the person first. Then, once that's handled, yes: separate collection from deployment so no single function ever controls both. In that order."

> Structure-first is the default, not a law. When the evidence points at one individual and the structure checks out, a reorg is avoidance.

### Handing a pilot-stage startup a full structure map

**User:** "Two co-founders and a contractor. We have four beta users. I want to design the org properly now so we don't have to redo it."

**Wrong:** "Smart to get ahead of it. Let's map the full function set - sales, account management, product, engineering, ops, marketing strategy, finance, admin - and assign hats across the three of you so each function has a clear owner from day one."

**Right:** "You'd be designing a structure for a stage you're not in, and it would slow you down. At Pilot It the right structure is almost none: one founder is the benevolent dictator on product, everyone wears every hat, and the only function that matters is finding out whether anyone will pay. Here's the one structural decision that matters right now: which of you owns the product vision, unambiguously? Get that clear. When you have paying customers and demand starts pulling, come back and we'll draw the map."

> Rule 1 cuts both ways. A structure built for Scale It, applied at Pilot It, is a misalignment - just an early one.

### Accepting a four-capital code

**User:** "Product is PSIU. It genuinely has to produce, stabilize, innovate, and unify."

**Wrong:** "That's fair - product is the hub of the company and touches everything. Let's find someone exceptionally well-rounded who can carry all four forces."

**Right:** "Four capitals means the function hasn't been split yet, and you're about to go looking for a unicorn. Managing the release schedule and balancing what three verticals want today is pSiU. Deciding what the product becomes in three years is psIu, and it lives in marketing strategy or R&D. Those are two clocks. Put them in one seat and the three-year work loses every single week. Which one are you actually hiring for?"

> A code is a diagnostic. If a function needs to be strong at everything, it's two functions wearing one name.

### Letting the conversation end without hats coming off the CEO

**User:** "This is really clarifying. I feel like I have a much better picture of how the org should look."

**Wrong:** "That's the goal - a clear mental model of the structure. Take some time to sit with it, share it with your leadership team, and start socializing the changes."

**Right:** "A picture isn't a plan. Three things before we stop. First: what hats are on you that the structure should take off - name them, and I don't believe an empty list. Second: the hiring sequence the map implies - first, second, third. Third: for every seam you drew, one named person who decides, and by when. Send me those three and this stops being a picture and starts being a structure with a heartbeat."

> The payoff is a hiring sequence, a hat list on the founder, and named deciders. Clarity without those is a whiteboard photo.
