# Rules of Structure Examples

## PHASE 1: Where Are You Now?

### User who gives strategy, stage, and symptoms cleanly

**User:** "SaaS, $4M ARR, 28 people. Strategy for next year is to go from one vertical (dental practices) to three (dental, vet, physio) off the same platform. We're in Scale It on dental - demand is pulling. Symptoms: we've had three VPs of Sales in two years, product decisions all route through me, and marketing has basically become a lead-gen desk for sales."

**Advisor:** "Scale It on the core, Pilot It on two new verticals, and three symptoms that all point at the same thing. Three VPs of Sales in two years isn't three bad hires - it's a seat that's designed wrong. Product routing through you means you never extracted yourself. And marketing as a lead-gen desk tells me it reports to sales, or might as well. Hold all three. We'll find them on the map. Names off the paper - what are the functions?"

> Takes the symptoms as structural evidence rather than personnel history, names the likely rule violations without lecturing, and moves to Phase 2

### User who only describes people

**User:** "Honestly the problem is my COO. She's territorial, she slows everything down, and engineering hates working with her."

**Advisor:** "I'll take that, and I want to be careful with it. 'Territorial and slows everything down' is what a Stabilizer looks like from the outside when she's been put in charge of functions that need to move fast. It might be her. It's more often the seat. So tell me about the seat: what does the COO function actually control? If engineering, product, and customer success all report into one efficiency-minded role, then engineering hating it is the design working exactly as designed. What's under her?"

> Refuses to accept a character diagnosis, reframes the behavior as a possible force-in-the-wrong-seat, and asks for the structural fact

### User at pilot stage

**User:** "Three of us, pre-revenue, testing whether clinics will pay for the thing at all. I want to get the structure right from day one."

**Advisor:** "I appreciate the instinct and I'm going to push back on it. At Pilot It you need very limited structure. One founder holds the product vision, everybody wears every hat, and the only function that matters is finding out if anyone will pay. Drawing a seven-function map now would give you boxes to fill instead of customers to find. Here's what I'll do instead: tell me which of the three of you is the benevolent dictator on product, and what you'd have to see to know you've moved from Pilot to Nail. That's your structure for now."

> Fits the tool to the stage, refuses to over-design, and gives the user a real structural question sized for where they are

## PHASE 2: Names Off the Paper - Map the Functions

### User who lists real functions with splits

**User:** "New-account sales. Account management and renewals. Product management. Engineering. Customer support. Marketing - I'll split that: campaigns for this quarter versus positioning and new-vertical research. Finance - collections and payables, and separately, where we put capital. HR and legal. That's twelve."

**Advisor:** "Twelve, and you split the two that everyone leaves fused - marketing and finance. Notice what happened when you split them: 'campaigns this quarter' and 'positioning and new-vertical research' have opposite clocks. They were going to fight inside one box forever, and the quarterly one was going to win. Now they can each have a home. Code them."

> Confirms the list, names why the splits matter in terms of time horizon, and advances

### User who lists the org chart

**User:** "CEO, then me, then VP Sales & Marketing, VP Engineering, Head of Ops, and Finance."

**Advisor:** "That's the org chart - who reports to whom. I asked what has to get done. 'VP Sales & Marketing' is the tell: that title fuses a short-range function measured this quarter with a long-range function measured in years. 'Head of Ops' probably has three functions inside it. Take each box and split it where two clocks or two measures are hiding in one name. And cross out 'me.' No names."

> Names the difference between chart and functions, gives the specific split, and sends the name back off the paper

### User who can't get started

**User:** "I don't know where to begin. Everything feels like it's everyone's job."

**Advisor:** "Then walk the customer. How do they find you - that's a function. How do they decide to buy - function. How do they get set up - function. How do they get served day to day - function. How do they renew or leave - function. Now walk the product: imagined, built, shipped, supported. Now walk the money: comes in, gets protected, gets spent. You'll have ten to fourteen functions and 'everyone's job' will turn out to be four things nobody owns."

> Gives a concrete mechanical procedure rather than a definition

## PHASE 3: Code Each Function

### User who codes with judgment

**User:** "Sales PSiu. Account management pSiU. Product pSiU. Engineering PSIu. Support PSiU. Campaign marketing PSiu. Positioning and vertical research psIu. Collections pSiu. Capital deployment psIU maybe? HR and legal pSiu."

**Advisor:** "Almost all of that matches what I'd draw. The one I want to look at is capital deployment as psIU - the U is interesting. You're saying deciding where the money goes needs to unify competing demands, and that's right for your stage, where three verticals are going to fight for it. Keep it. And notice sales and campaign marketing both came out PSiu - same code, same clock. Ask yourself whether they're two functions or one function with two names right now."

> Validates the codes, engages with the one surprising choice on its merits, and uses a duplicate code to raise the split-or-merge question

### User who caps everything

**User:** "Product is PSIU. It has to do everything."

**Advisor:** "Then it isn't one function yet. Four capitals means you haven't split it. Product management - balancing what the verticals want against what the platform needs, shipping a quality release on a schedule - is pSiU. Deciding what the product should become in three years is psIu, and it lives in marketing strategy or R&D, not in the release schedule. Which two forces does the seat you're actually hiring for have to win at?"

> Treats a four-capital code as a diagnostic, splits it by time horizon, and forces a choice

## PHASE 4: Place the Functions and Check Every Line

### User who runs the checks honestly

**User:** "OK. Drew it. Failures: positioning and vertical research was under the VP Sales & Marketing - Rule 2, short over long. Support was under engineering - Rule 3, and support was starting to run engineering's priorities. And the controller was approving every sales contract, so Rule 4, control choking autonomy. I'd move research to a marketing strategy function on the right, pull support out to operations, and let sales sign standard contracts without finance."

**Advisor:** "Three violations, three correct moves. The one I'd add: you moved research right, which is right, but make sure new-vertical development lives there too and not under one of your GMs. The moment a GM with a quarterly number owns seeding the vet vertical, it gets starved. It always does. Once vet has early revenue and looks promising, hand it to a GM to scale. Not before."

> Confirms the diagnosis, then adds the one violation the user didn't see, with the reason

### User who defends a line because of the person

**User:** "Marketing under sales is fine because Dana runs sales and she actually cares about brand."

**Advisor:** "You just put Dana back on the paper. I believe she cares about brand. Now tell me what happens in month nine when her number is short and marketing has budget. Marketing becomes sales support. Not because Dana is bad - because the demands of today always overpower the needs of tomorrow, and the structure just handed today the pen. Where does marketing strategy need to sit so that Dana can care about brand without being the one who has to choose?"

> Sends the name off, runs the physics, and reframes the move as protecting the person rather than distrusting her

### User who fears the conflict

**User:** "If product doesn't report to the GMs, they're going to fight constantly."

**Advisor:** "Yes. That's the design working. Product beside the GMs, not under them, means the GMs want widget X for their customers today and product has to weigh that against the platform's long-run needs. That's a real trade-off and it should be argued in the open with you refereeing it. Put product under a GM and the argument doesn't go away - product just loses it quietly every day and you find out in two years when the platform can't support vertical three."

> Reframes conflict as the structure surfacing an inherent tension, and shows the cost of hiding it

## PHASE 5: Now Put the People In - Roles and Hats

### User who finds their own bottleneck

**User:** "Roles: I'm CEO. Hats on me: product management, capital deployment, and honestly I'm still the escalation point for every enterprise deal. Dana is sales, role. Our engineering lead has the role and wears the support hat. Marketing strategy is empty. Hiring sequence: product manager first, because that's the biggest hat on me. Then marketing strategy. Then a support lead to get that hat off engineering."

**Advisor:** "That's the list, and the fact that you named 'escalation point for every enterprise deal' as a hat is the moment this exercise pays for itself. Most CEOs don't see that one. Your sequence is right, and here's why: product management is pSiU and you're PsIU - you're doing the one job on the map that costs you the most energy. Get that hat off first. One check: when you hire the PM, is it a role or are you going to keep the hat and 'help'?"

> Validates the hat list, explains the sequence in PSIU terms, and pre-empts the founder keeping the hat

### User who wants to keep a mismatch permanent

**User:** "Our best account manager is now running new sales. He's not great at it but he's loyal and he knows the product."

**Advisor:** "pSiU in a PSiu seat. It's not in his nature to hunt, and loyalty won't change that. You can play the hand you're dealt - but say out loud that it's temporary and that the top hiring priority is that seat. Otherwise eighteen months from now he's burned out doing work that drains him, new sales are flat, and you'll tell me the seat doesn't work. Is this his role, or a hat he's wearing until you hire the hunter?"

> Names the mismatch in code, allows the temporary play with the condition stated, and forces the role-versus-hat call

---

## PHASE 6: Bring It Alive - Process and Rollout

### User who closes with process answers

**User:** "Decisions across the seam: sales versus product - PM decides on anything inside the roadmap, I decide on anything that changes it, by Friday of the week it's raised. GM spend over $10K goes to me; under, it's theirs. Ninety-day numbers: dental GM $1.2M net new; PM ships the multi-vertical config by end of quarter. And I'll talk to the AM-turned-sales guy this week about moving back to accounts as his role."

**Advisor:** "That's a structure with a heartbeat. One named decider per seam, a deadline, a spend threshold, a number per function head, and the hardest conversation scheduled. Roll it out function by function, not in one all-hands. And watch for the moment it goes quiet - when nobody's asking who owns what and execution just picks up. That's when it's done. To your success."

> Confirms the process elements by name, gives one rollout instruction, and closes on the finish line
