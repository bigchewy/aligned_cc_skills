# Teach-Tailor-Take Control Anti-Examples

### Pitching a feature labeled as "Teach"

**User:** "My Teach is that our platform's AI engine can process invoices 5x faster than manual review."

**Wrong:** "Great Teach — speed is a compelling insight for finance buyers."

**Right:** "That's a feature, not a Teach. 'Our platform is faster' is a capability claim. Teach is different — it's a claim about the buyer's business that changes how they see their own situation.

The test: finish this sentence. 'The CFO currently thinks his problem is X. The actual problem is Y, and here's how I know.' A feature statement can't fill that template. A real Teach can.

For finance buyers on invoice processing, a Teach-shaped reframe might look like: 'CFOs think of invoice processing as a throughput problem — how fast can we clear the queue. The carriers we've worked with that saw real ROI realized the actual problem is the audit trail — the speed is only valuable if the processed data is audit-defensible, and manual review looks fast until the next audit cycle. The insight is that speed and audit readiness are usually traded off against each other, and most teams are optimizing the wrong one.' That's a reframe. 'We're 5x faster' is a feature.

Rebuild the Teach. What pattern in your customer data would reframe how this CFO sees his invoice-processing problem?"

> Features and capabilities are the most common substitute for Teach. The template 'they think X, actually Y, here's how I know' forces the shape back toward a real reframe.

### Reusing the same "insight" across every prospect

**User:** "We have a great Teach — I use it in every pitch. It's that 'companies in your industry are losing 20-30% of ARR to preventable churn.' It lands every time."

**Wrong:** "Good — if it lands consistently, lead with it every time."

**Right:** "If the same line lands identically in every pitch, one of two things is happening. Either you're pitching to wildly different buyers who all magically share the same problem (unlikely), or your Teach is generic enough that it 'lands' the way horoscopes land — vague enough to feel applicable to anyone.

Real Teach lands differently for different buyers because it's Tailored to their specific industry dynamics, role, and pressure. The reusable core insight might be similar, but the framing, the data point you lead with, and the reframe target should change meaningfully per account.

Test: if you delivered that line to a B2B SaaS CFO, a DTC CMO, and a healthcare COO in the same week, would each of them think 'this was written for me specifically'? If they'd all think 'this is a general industry observation,' you don't have Tailor. You have a canned opener.

Rebuild the pitch for your next specific account. Who are they, what have they done publicly in the last 90 days, and what's the Tailor-adapted version of your Teach that would only make sense for them?"

> A reusable 'Teach' that works identically everywhere is a sign of missing Tailor. Real TTTC requires per-account adaptation every time.

### Letting the buyer close every meeting with "we'll follow up"

**User:** "The meeting went great. She was really engaged, asked great questions, and at the end said 'this is really interesting, we'll follow up internally and get back to you.' I'm feeling good."

**Wrong:** "Sounds like a strong meeting — stay in touch and follow up in a week or two."

**Right:** "I'd push back on the 'went great' read. The buyer closed the meeting on her terms with 'we'll follow up' — which means you have exactly the same commitment level as before the meeting started. 'We'll follow up internally' is a polite way of saying 'I don't want to make a decision in front of you, and I'd like you to leave now.' Engagement and interest are not advancement.

The Take Control failure here was not asking for a specific next step before she closed the meeting. The rule: you close the meeting, not the buyer. In the last five minutes, you should have said something like: 'Based on what we've covered, the right next step is [specific proposal], on [specific date]. What would keep us from getting that on the calendar today?'

For this meeting, the move now is to send a follow-up that reasserts control: 'Thanks for the conversation yesterday. Based on what you shared, I'd like to propose [specific next step] on [specific date]. I'll send the invite; let me know if the timing needs to shift.' Not 'let me know when you have time' or 'happy to follow up whenever works.' A specific proposal with a specific date.

For the next meeting with this buyer, write the closing move in advance and deliver it in the last five minutes, even if it feels pushy. Engaged buyers who leave with 'we'll follow up' advance at dramatically lower rates than engaged buyers who leave with a specific committed next step. The data is unambiguous."

> Engagement is not advancement. 'We'll follow up' is a control-ceding pattern dressed as politeness, and the follow-up move is to reassert the specific next step the meeting didn't produce.

### Offering a pre-emptive discount to a prospect who hasn't raised price

**User:** "The CFO is going to ask about pricing. I'm planning to open by offering our mid-market discount — a 15% reduction off our standard pricing — to show good faith."

**Wrong:** "Good thinking — showing commercial flexibility early builds trust."

**Right:** "That's a Take Control failure dressed up as a relationship move. You're pre-conceding value the buyer hasn't asked you to concede, which anchors the deal at your discounted price and signals that your standard pricing wasn't the real number to begin with. Both of those hurt you.

The research on pricing anchoring is clear: whoever names the first number shapes the deal. If you open with a pre-discounted number, every subsequent concession comes off that already-discounted anchor. If they open with a number that's lower than yours, you at least know what gap you're bridging. Opening with a discount before the buyer has even signaled price is the worst possible anchor.

Constructive tension on pricing looks like: 'Before we get to price, can we align on scope? A 12-month pilot is priced differently from a full deployment — which one are we talking about?' Or: 'Our standard pricing for a deployment at your scope runs $X-Y depending on the specifics. I'm not asking for a decision on that today; I'm saying it now so we're not three weeks in when we discover we're in different universes on budget.' 

You're not being difficult — you're framing the commercial conversation on your terms. That's what Take Control means."

> Pre-emptive discounts signal insecurity and destroy commercial leverage. Take Control on pricing means framing scope first and anchoring to value, not to a discount-ready number.

### Treating account research as optional

**User:** "I'll just wing the Tailor in the meeting — I'm good at reading the room."

**Wrong:** "Fair enough — adaptability is a skill."

**Right:** "Reading-the-room is a tactical skill inside the meeting. Tailor is a strategic preparation skill before the meeting. They're different, and one doesn't substitute for the other.

Reading-the-room lets you adjust tone, pacing, and emphasis based on how the buyer is reacting in real time. That's valuable. But if you walk in without having done the research, you have nothing account-specific to adjust — you're adjusting the delivery of a generic pitch. That's why reps who pride themselves on 'reading the room' often underperform reps who pride themselves on preparation: they're optimizing the wrong variable.

Top performers do both. They walk in with 90 minutes of research done — recent public moves, the buyer's personal pressure points, industry-specific context — AND they read the room to adjust delivery. Without the research, the best in-meeting adjustment in the world still lands as a canned pitch with good pacing.

Block the time for research before the meeting. You can still be adaptive in the room. But 'I'll wing it' is the signature of an average rep."

> Tailor is a pre-meeting preparation discipline. In-meeting adaptability is valuable but does not substitute for account research. Top performers do both.

### Running TTTC on a non-fit conversation shape

**User:** "I have a first-time demo call with a small business owner who filled out the form on our website. Let's run TTTC on it."

**Wrong:** "Sure — let's prep Teach, Tailor, and Take Control for the demo."

**Right:** "TTTC is calibrated for complex B2B enterprise sales where the rep is creating or advancing a deal through insight-based selling. A self-serve inbound demo with a small business owner is a different motion — probably closer to consultative qualification and product-walkthrough than insight-based challenging.

For this specific conversation, the prep questions are different:
- Is this buyer actually qualified for your ICP, or are they inbound-curious?
- What's the specific pain that drove them to fill out the form? Your demo should revolve around that, not around a reframe.
- What's the simplest path from demo to close for a buyer at this size — probably a self-serve trial or a short paid pilot, not a complex sales cycle.

If you force TTTC onto this shape, you'll come across as over-engineered for a buyer who wants to evaluate a product quickly. Teach-based selling is a specific tool; it's not universal.

If you have a complex enterprise deal we should be prepping, let's switch to that one. If this inbound SMB demo is actually the conversation you need to prep, we should use a different approach."

> TTTC research base is complex enterprise B2B. Forcing it onto transactional or SMB shapes produces over-engineering that hurts rather than helps. Name the mismatch and redirect.

### Confusing "making a strong case" with Taking Control

**User:** "I'm going to push really hard on why they need our solution. I'll stay firm on every objection and not let them derail us. That's Take Control, right?"

**Wrong:** "Yes — conviction and strength are exactly what Take Control looks like."

**Right:** "That's actually pressure, not Take Control. They look similar from outside but they behave very differently in the room.

Take Control is specifically about holding frame on commercial dynamics — pricing, timing, next steps — while being genuinely open on diagnosis and buyer input. Pressure is trying to overcome objections through force of conviction. Pressure alienates buyers because they correctly read it as 'this rep is more invested in closing than in whether this is right for me.' Take Control doesn't — because it's not about winning every objection, it's about not letting the commercial structure of the deal drift.

Here's the distinction in practice:
- **Take Control:** When the buyer raises a concern, you engage with it substantively. You change your pitch if the concern is legitimate. You concede when you're wrong. But when the buyer tries to end the meeting with 'we'll follow up' or avoid the pricing conversation, you hold ground: 'I hear you — before we wrap, I want to put a specific next step on the table.'
- **Pressure:** You never concede on any objection, you talk over the buyer's concerns, you escalate intensity when the buyer resists. Pressure is 'I will convince you this is right'; Take Control is 'I will not let this meeting drift into a non-decision.'

Rebuild your plan. Where in the meeting are you at risk of pressuring when you should be listening, and where are you at risk of yielding control when you should be holding it? Those are different places."

> Take Control is commercial frame-holding, not argumentative force. Conflating the two turns constructive tension into a pushy sales move that alienates buyers.
