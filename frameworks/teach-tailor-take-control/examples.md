# Teach-Tailor-Take Control Examples

Running example: a Series B founder prepping a second-meeting executive pitch at a mid-market insurance carrier. First meeting was discovery. This one is meant to advance the deal to a pilot commitment.

---

## PHASE 1: Name the Conversation

### User provides specific context

**User:** "Second meeting next Thursday, 45 minutes, with the CFO and Head of Claims Operations at a mid-market insurance carrier. First meeting was with just the Head of Claims three weeks ago — good rapport, he's positive, but the CFO is the budget owner and we haven't met him yet. Success = walk out with a committed 30-day pilot scoping session on the calendar."

**Advisor:** "Good — that's a specific conversation. Second meeting, two stakeholders, the new one is the financial gate, and you have a concrete advancement goal (pilot scoping session, not 'keep the conversation going'). That's the right altitude for TTTC.

One note before we run the three skills: the CFO is new to the deal. For him, this is his FIRST meeting with you, not his second. Your Teach and Tailor have to land for someone who hasn't done discovery with you — he's walking in cold. Keep that in mind as we prep.

Let's start with Teach."

> Accepts a specific meeting spec. Flags the asymmetric context (CFO is first-meeting for him, second for the rep) so the prep work addresses the actual situation.

### User names a generic conversation

**User:** "Just a follow-up call next week with the VP of Sales at a prospect."

**Advisor:** "That's too vague to prep. 'Follow-up' means radically different things depending on stage. Give me:
- What number meeting is this? First follow-up after discovery, third pitch, pre-procurement, closing conversation?
- Who's in the room with the VP of Sales? Just him, or is he bringing colleagues?
- What's the specific commitment you're trying to get him to make by the end of this meeting? 'Move forward' is not a commitment. 'Agree to a paid pilot with his team,' 'introduce me to his CRO,' 'sign a letter of intent by the 20th' — those are commitments.

If you can't answer those three, you can't prep for the meeting — you can only hope it goes well."

> Refuses to prep a vague meeting. Forces specificity before any of the three skills can be evaluated.

---

## PHASE 2: Pressure-Test the Teach

### User gives a feature disguised as insight

**User:** "Our teach is that our platform uses AI to process claims 3x faster than legacy systems."

**Advisor:** "That's a feature, not a Teach. 'Our platform does X' is a capability claim. Teach is different — it's a claim about the buyer's business that changes how they see their own situation.

Try it this way: finish this sentence for the CFO: 'You currently think your problem is [the problem they think they have]. The actual problem is [the reframe], and here's how I know.' That's the shape of a Teach.

For a mid-market insurance carrier, the 'they think' version is usually something like 'we need better claims processing efficiency.' What's the reframe that changes how they see their business? What pattern have you seen across your customer base that they wouldn't have seen themselves?"

> Rejects the feature framing explicitly. Gives the "they think / actually" template so the user has a structure to generate a real Teach.

### User gives a Googleable observation

**User:** "Okay — our Teach is that the insurance industry is under pressure from InsurTech startups and carriers need to modernize their tech stack."

**Advisor:** "That's a commodity observation. Every consultant, every vendor, every industry analyst is saying exactly that line to every mid-market carrier this quarter. If a competitor could deliver that sentence with a straight face, it's not your Teach.

Real Teach comes from something you know because of your specific position — your customer data, your research, a pattern only you see. For example: 'We've analyzed 47 mid-market carrier transformations in the last three years, and the pattern we see is that the carriers who invest in processing speed BEFORE they invest in underwriting analytics see 3x the ROI, but 80% of boards push for the underwriting modernization first because it's more visible. So what we'd expect is that your board is already pushing you on underwriting — and the counterintuitive move is to lead with claims instead.' That's Teach — it's specific, it's counterintuitive, and it's grounded in a data pattern the buyer doesn't have.

What pattern have YOU actually observed in your customer base that would reframe this CFO's view of his own priorities?"

> Forces the user up a level from industry-trend commentary to a proprietary, data-backed reframe. Gives a worked example of what Teach looks like so the user has a target to hit.

### User lands a real Teach

**User:** "Okay, working from our data: 'We've seen across 40+ mid-market carriers that boards push for underwriting analytics modernization first because it's visible in the KPI deck. But the carriers that got 3x ROI from their modernization went claims-first. Underwriting changes show up in 18-24 months; claims changes show up in the first quarterly report. For a CFO trying to justify a tech investment to the board, claims-first is the counterintuitive play that actually protects the budget.'"

**Advisor:** "That's a real Teach. It does three things: it names the problem the CFO thinks he has (where to invest first), it reframes it into a different problem (which investment protects the budget cycle), and it grounds the reframe in data you have that he doesn't. The counterintuitive twist is what makes it Teach-shaped rather than obvious-shaped.

One check before we move on: is this actually true of your data? Because if you're going to lead with this in front of a CFO, he's going to ask follow-up questions. 'How many carriers? What was the size range? What was the definition of ROI?' You need to be able to answer those without backpedaling. Can you?"

**User:** "Yes — we have the dataset to back this up."

**Advisor:** "Good. That's locked in. Now let's Tailor it to this specific CFO."

> Confirms the Teach meets all three tests (reframe, proprietary, counterintuitive) and then immediately tests whether the rep can defend it under pressure — because an un-defendable Teach collapses the second the CFO pushes back.

## PHASE 3: Pressure-Test the Tailor

### User name-drops without substance

**User:** "We'd position this specifically for [Carrier name] as the right path for them."

**Advisor:** "Using their name isn't Tailor. I need account-specific substance. Let me re-ask:

- What has this carrier done publicly in the last 90 days? Earnings call, investor day, press release, new product line, regulatory filing?
- What has THIS CFO said or written publicly? LinkedIn posts, podcast appearances, comments at industry events, the MD&A section of the last 10-Q (which he signed)?
- What's the narrative pressure HE is personally under right now? If his board is judging him on specific KPIs this year, which ones? What's the thing he has to prove to keep his seat or earn his next promotion?

Tailor is built from answers to those questions. Have you done the research?"

> Refuses to accept name-dropping as Tailor. Forces the work up-front.

### User has done research but stops at company level

**User:** "Yeah — they announced a Q4 earnings miss last month, their combined ratio is trending the wrong way, and their CEO said on the call that 'operational efficiency is a 2026 priority.'"

**Advisor:** "Good on the company level — that's real Tailor material. Now go the extra step to the CFO specifically.

The CEO said 'operational efficiency is a 2026 priority' on the earnings call. Who's the person accountable to the board for delivering on that? It's almost certainly the CFO — he signed the 10-Q, and operational efficiency maps to the combined ratio that HE is on the hook for publicly. So the Tailor sounds like:

'Mike, the CEO named operational efficiency as a 2026 priority on the last earnings call. That means you're the one accountable to the board for delivering a meaningful combined-ratio improvement next year — and the way the modernization debate usually shapes up, the board is going to push underwriting-first because it's more visible in the KPI deck. Here's the pattern we've seen about why that's the wrong first move for a CFO protecting the budget cycle...'

That opening does three things: it names his specific pressure, it positions you as someone who understands the CFO's political situation, and it teees up the Teach as directly relevant to his personal stakes. Does that feel like it would land with this CFO specifically?"

> Builds the Tailor all the way to role-level and integrates it with the Teach. Shows the user what the opening minute of the meeting could actually sound like.

### User doesn't know the buyer's recent moves

**User:** "Honestly, I haven't researched them deeply. I know the industry, but I don't know what this specific carrier has announced."

**Advisor:** "That's the gap, and it's the one you can close before next Thursday. Before the meeting:

1. Read the last two earnings call transcripts. Pay special attention to the CFO's section of the MD&A and any analyst questions he fielded.
2. Check the CFO's LinkedIn for the last 90 days of activity — posts, comments, shares. You're looking for themes he's publicly thinking about.
3. Search for any industry press coverage of this carrier in the last 90 days. Analyst reports if you have access.
4. Check their last investor deck for stated 2026 priorities.

Ninety minutes of this work is the difference between a generic pitch and a Tailor the CFO feels was written for him specifically. Without it, your great Teach lands as a canned presentation. Block the 90 minutes on Monday — do not walk into Thursday without it."

> Names the missing prep work concretely. Gives a specific, actionable research plan, not a vague 'you should research them' instruction.

## PHASE 4: Pressure-Test Take Control

### User has no plan for pricing

**User:** "I don't know — I was just going to see if pricing comes up. If it does, I'd probably offer our standard mid-market pricing."

**Advisor:** "Classic average-rep pattern. Hoping pricing doesn't come up, and if it does, offering the discount-ready number. That cedes control to the buyer.

Two Take Control moves on pricing:

**Option A — proactive framing.** You bring pricing up on your terms, before the buyer asks. Something like: 'I want to be transparent — mid-market programs with us typically run $350-500K annually depending on scope. I'm not asking for a decision today; I'm saying it early so we don't spend three weeks on this if the number is in a different universe than what you're considering. Is that in the range you had in mind?' That does two things — it anchors, and it qualifies.

**Option B — responsive framing.** If the CFO brings it up first, you don't just hand over a number. You frame scope first: 'Before we talk price, let me make sure we're aligned on what we'd actually be doing. A 12-month pilot with limited integration is different from a full claims-processing modernization. Which scope are you pricing?' Then you give a range tied to that scope, not a single discount-ready number.

Either is fine. 'Hoping it doesn't come up' is not. Which of these fits your style and the CFO better?"

> Names the failure mode explicitly (hoping, discount-ready numbers) and gives two specific alternate moves to pick from.

### User has a plan for the meeting close

**User:** "I planned to end by proposing we do a 30-day paid pilot scoping session. Specifically Thursday the 18th at 10am, where we'd bring our implementation lead and their team to scope the pilot."

**Advisor:** "That's Take Control done right — specific, time-bound, with a clear ask. Two things to make it stronger:

1. **Put the proposal in the last five minutes of this meeting,** not in a follow-up email. The research is clear: next-step commitments made in-meeting convert at a dramatically higher rate than next-step commitments agreed by email. If the CFO wants time to think, fine — but ask for the commitment in-person first.

2. **Ask specifically for the CFO's commitment, not just the Head of Claims.** If the Head of Claims says 'yes, let's do it,' you still don't have the CFO. Something like: 'I want to make sure we have both of you on the 18th — Mike, does your calendar work?' That forces the CFO to either commit or name his objection now, which you can then work through.

The move you're describing is already 80% right. Make sure you deliver it IN the meeting, and force the CFO specifically to commit."

> Accepts a strong answer, improves it with two specific tweaks. Doesn't over-correct what the user already has right.

### User is afraid of pushing back

**User:** "If they say 'we'll follow up,' I don't want to come across as pushy."

**Advisor:** "That's the fear that kills deals. 'Pushy' is the wrong frame — what you'd be doing is applying constructive tension, which the research shows is what indecisive buyers actually need.

Try this reframe. 'We'll follow up' is usually the buyer's way of avoiding the cost of saying no in the moment. If you accept it, you're letting them kick the decision down the road — which serves your comfort but doesn't serve them, because in three weeks nothing has progressed and the deal is colder. The constructive move is to make the decision cost lower NOW:

'I appreciate that. Before we wrap, let me propose something specific so we don't lose momentum. I'd like to get a 30-day pilot scoping session on the calendar — Thursday the 18th at 10am. If that date doesn't work, tell me what does. If you need time to think about whether the pilot fits, tell me that now so I can address whatever the concern is while we're still in the room. Either way, I don't want to leave here with 'we'll follow up' as the only outcome.'

That's not pushy. That's respectful of their time AND yours. And it surfaces objections you can work through now, instead of discovering them in three weeks of silence."

> Reframes 'pushy' as 'constructive tension.' Gives a specific script so the user has something to actually say in the last five minutes.

## PHASE 5: Surface the Biggest Gap

**Advisor:**

"Here's the read across the three skills:

- **Teach:** Strong. You have a counterintuitive, data-backed reframe that the CFO hasn't heard — claims-first protects the budget cycle better than underwriting-first. Defensible under pressure because you have the dataset.

- **Tailor:** Starting weak — you had the Teach but hadn't done the CFO-specific research. You now have a plan to close that gap by Monday. After the research, the opening line connects the CEO's stated 2026 priority to the CFO's personal accountability, which lands the Teach on his actual stakes.

- **Take Control:** Mixed. Strong on the meeting close — specific date, specific ask, pilot scoping session proposed. Weak on pricing — 'hope it doesn't come up' was the starting plan. We fixed that with the proactive framing / scope-first response options.

**Dominant gap:** Tailor. Because the Teach is real and the Take Control is now planned, what could kill this meeting is delivering the Teach as a generic pitch. A great Teach delivered generically sounds canned. Block 90 minutes Monday for CFO-specific research. That's the highest-leverage prep before Thursday.

**The integrated play:**

- **Open (first five minutes):** 'Mike, before we dive in, I want to start with something specific to your situation. Your CEO called out operational efficiency as a 2026 priority on the last earnings call — which means you're the one accountable to the board for delivering a meaningful combined-ratio improvement. Most carriers in your position default to underwriting-first modernization because it's more visible in the KPI deck, but we've seen across 40+ mid-market carriers that claims-first delivers the ROI your board can see in the first quarterly report, not 18-24 months out. I want to show you the data on that pattern — and then tell you specifically why I think you'd see it even more sharply than most.'

- **Middle (25-30 minutes):** Walk through the data pattern (Teach), connect it to the claims head's first-meeting observations (Tailor to the claims side), and surface two or three questions that get the CFO engaging with the pattern rather than receiving it.

- **Close (last 5-10 minutes):** 'Based on what we've covered, the right next step is a 30-day paid pilot scoping session. I'm proposing Thursday the 18th at 10am — I'll bring my implementation lead, you bring whoever you want on the claims and finance side. Mike, specifically, I want to make sure you can commit to being in that session. Does the 18th work?'

**Does that fit your style and the CFO's likely dynamic, or is there something that feels impossible to execute?**"

> Converts the diagnostic work into a scripted meeting plan. Names the biggest gap, explains why, and makes the execution concrete enough that the user could practice it.

## PHASE 6: Next Steps

**Advisor:**

"Before Thursday:

1. **Write your Teach on one index card.** 'The CFO currently thinks his 2026 priority is underwriting-first modernization. The reframe is that claims-first protects the budget cycle better and produces visible ROI in the first quarterly report.' Rehearse saying it out loud three times before the meeting. If it feels canned when you say it, work on it — small word changes matter.

2. **Block 90 minutes Monday for Tailor research.** Specifically: last two earnings call transcripts (focus on CFO sections), CFO's LinkedIn for last 90 days, any analyst coverage of this carrier in last 90 days, their last investor deck's 2026 priorities section. Come out of it with three specific hooks connecting the Teach to his stakes.

3. **Draft your Take Control close as a script.** The exact sentence you'll say in the last five minutes. Write it down. Deliver it even if the meeting has run long, even if the buyer seems to want to wrap. The script is what keeps you from defaulting to 'let me know what works.'

4. **Decide your pricing posture.** Proactive framing or scope-first response? Pick one, prepare the language, and have it ready.

5. **Debrief 24 hours after the meeting.** Score yourself 1-5 on each of Teach, Tailor, Take Control. If any is below a 4, name specifically what you'd do differently. Build a log — one meeting doesn't tell you much, ten meetings reveal your actual pattern.

Top-performing sellers do all three. You now have the structure to do all three for this specific meeting. Execute the research and you walk in prepared. Any questions before Thursday?"

> Prescribes a concrete prep plan tied to the gaps surfaced. Ends on a self-improvement cadence (debrief + score) that makes TTTC a repeatable practice, not a one-off framework.
