# Leadership Pipeline Passages Examples

## STEP 1: Locate the Current Role

### IC recently promoted to manager

**User:** "I got promoted to engineering manager three months ago. I have five direct reports. Honestly, most of my week is still spent coding because I'm the most experienced on the team."

**Advisor:** "How much of your week — give me a percentage — is spent doing the technical work yourself versus planning, coaching, and reviewing your team's work?"

**User:** "Probably 70% coding, 30% management stuff."

**Advisor:** "And when something breaks in production, who fixes it?"

**User:** "Usually me. I'm the fastest."

> Locates the leader at Passage 1 transition — still operating as an IC with a manager title.

### Manager who manages managers but skips levels

**User:** "I run the platform team. I have three engineering managers reporting to me, and about 25 engineers total. My weeks are mostly spent in architecture reviews and helping engineers with technical decisions."

**Advisor:** "When you say you help engineers with technical decisions — are these the engineers who report to your managers? Not your managers themselves?"

**User:** "Yeah, I jump into their Slack channels, do code reviews, sometimes pair with them on hard problems."

**Advisor:** "And your three managers — when was the last time you spent an hour developing one of them as a leader?"

**User:** "...I give them feedback in our one-on-ones."

> Locates the leader at Passage 2 — role requires managing managers, but they're still managing ICs directly.

## STEP 2: Diagnose the Gap

### Passage 1 diagnosis

**Advisor:** "Your role requires Passage 1 leadership — managing others. But you're still operating at Passage 0 — managing self. Here's the evidence:

Skills: You're the one doing the hardest technical work. Your team doesn't get the reps.
Time: 70% doing, 30% managing. For your role, that needs to be closer to the inverse.
Values: You still measure yourself by code quality. Your role measures you by team output.

You're performing one level below where you sit."

**User:** "But if I stop coding, things will slow down. My team isn't ready."

**Advisor:** "Your team isn't ready because you haven't made them ready. That's the Passage 1 trap — you keep doing the work because the team can't, and the team can't because you keep doing the work."

> Names the gap across all three dimensions. Doesn't accept the excuse.

### Passage 2 diagnosis

**Advisor:** "Your role requires Passage 2 leadership — managing managers. But you're skipping your managers and managing their ICs. That's Passage 1 behavior in a Passage 2 role.

Skills: You're reviewing code and making architecture decisions that your managers should own.
Time: You're in engineer Slack channels instead of developing your three managers.
Values: You value technical excellence. Your role values the quality of leaders you develop.

Your managers have stopped growing because you do their job for them."

**User:** "I don't want to lose touch with the technical work."

**Advisor:** "That's every Passage 2 leader's fear. Here's the reality: your job isn't to stay technical. Your job is to develop managers who ensure technical excellence. Different job."

> Directly addresses the common Passage 2 resistance — fear of losing technical identity.

## STEP 3: Name What Must Stop

### Passage 1 — leader resists letting go

**Advisor:** "You must stop doing these things: coding production features, being the first responder for production issues, reviewing every PR, and measuring yourself by lines of code or features shipped. Which of these are you still doing?"

**User:** "All of them, honestly. But the PR reviews — I catch things nobody else catches."

**Advisor:** "Of course you do. You have more experience. But every PR you review is a coaching opportunity you skip. Instead of catching the bug, teach someone to see the pattern. That's Passage 1 work."

> Reframes the leader's strength as the obstacle.

### Passage 2 — leader won't stop managing ICs

**Advisor:** "You must stop managing individual contributors directly. Stop jumping into their Slack channels. Stop doing code reviews on their PRs. Stop making architecture decisions that your managers should own. Which of these will be hardest to let go of?"

**User:** "The architecture decisions. I've been making those for eight years."

**Advisor:** "Eight years of building that skill. Now you need to build that skill in three other people. If you keep making those decisions, your managers will never develop the judgment. You're the bottleneck."

> Names the specific identity loss and reframes the new work.

## STEP 4: Name What Must Start

### Passage 1 — concrete next actions

**Advisor:** "Here's what replaces the coding: Planning work so your team can execute without you. Weekly one-on-ones focused on development, not status. Giving feedback on HOW someone works, not just WHAT they produce. Measuring your success by whether your team ships without your hands on the keyboard. What's the one thing on this list that would change the most if you started tomorrow?"

**User:** "Development-focused one-on-ones. Mine are all status updates right now."

**Advisor:** "Then start there. Next one-on-one, don't ask 'what's the status?' Ask 'what's the hardest decision you made this week, and what did you learn from it?' That's Passage 1 leadership."

> Gives a concrete, immediate action.

## STEP 5: The Identity Shift

### Naming the core shift

**Advisor:** "Every passage requires an identity shift. You were the best engineer on the team. That's why they promoted you. Now you're a leader of engineers. Those are different jobs. The question isn't whether you can learn to manage — you can. The question is whether you'll let go of being the best engineer. That identity served you for a decade. It's now in your way."

**User:** "That's... uncomfortable to hear."

**Advisor:** "It should be. Letting go of what made you successful is genuinely hard. But holding on to it is what makes leaders fail at their next passage. What's one thing you'll do this week to practice being a leader of engineers instead of the best engineer?"

> Names the emotional reality without dwelling on it. Moves to action.

### Staff engineer clarification

**User:** "What if I don't want to manage? I want to stay technical."

**Advisor:** "Then don't manage. The IC leadership track is real — Staff Engineer, Principal Engineer. That's a parallel pipeline with its own transitions: from solving problems to defining which problems matter, from individual output to technical leverage. Not everyone needs to go through the management passages. But if you're IN a management role, the passage transitions apply fully. What do you actually want?"

> Acknowledges the dual track without forcing management.
