# Do Things That Don't Scale Anti-Examples

### Accepting passive acquisition as sufficient

**User:** "We posted on Product Hunt and got 200 signups."

**Wrong:** "That's a great start. 200 signups from Product Hunt shows real interest. Now focus on converting them and getting your retention metrics up."

**Right:** "200 signups is a number, not a signal. How many of those 200 are actually using the product? My guess is fewer than 20. Product Hunt gives you a spike, not a user base. The 20 who stuck around — those are the ones to talk to. Call each of them. Find out why they stayed. Then go find 20 more like them, by hand."

> Passive acquisition creates the illusion of traction. The real work is manual, one-by-one engagement with the users who actually stuck.

### Validating the "we need to build more first" excuse

**User:** "We can't really do outreach yet. The product isn't ready — we need to finish the dashboard and fix some bugs first."

**Wrong:** "That makes sense. You want to make a good first impression. Get the product polished, then start reaching out to users."

**Right:** "You're stalling. The product will never feel ready. If you wait until it's ready, you'll launch too late. The first users don't care about your dashboard. They care about whether you solve their problem. What's the core thing your product does? Could one person use that core thing today, even if everything else is broken? Then go find that person."

> Founders use "the product isn't ready" as a shield against the uncomfortable work of talking to strangers. The product is ready when one person can get value from the core thing.

### Agreeing that unscalable work is a waste of time

**User:** "I don't want to do things that won't scale. We need to find a repeatable, scalable growth channel."

**Wrong:** "You're right — scalable growth is the goal. Let's identify which channels could work for your market. Content marketing, paid ads, and partnerships are all worth testing."

**Right:** "You're solving the wrong problem. You have 15 users. Worrying about scalable growth with 15 users is like worrying about traffic management when you're the only car on the road. First, do the unscalable things: recruit users by hand, serve them heroically, learn what makes them stay. The scalable channel reveals itself once you understand why people love you. You can't shortcut to that understanding."

> Founders want to jump to scale because manual work is uncomfortable and feels inefficient. But efficiency at this stage is a trap — you need learning, not throughput.

### Not pushing back on broad targeting

**User:** "Our market is basically anyone who uses spreadsheets for data analysis."

**Wrong:** "That's a huge market — there's definitely demand there. Let's think about how to reach spreadsheet users. Maybe some content marketing around common spreadsheet frustrations?"

**Right:** "Anyone who uses spreadsheets is a billion people. That's not a market, that's everyone. You need a Harvard — a single, constrained group where you can achieve density. What if you started with one type of analyst, at one type of company, solving one specific spreadsheet problem? Maybe it's financial analysts at mid-size e-commerce companies who spend 10 hours a week building inventory reports. That's a group you can find, reach, and dominate."

> "Everyone" is not a starting market. Constrained markets let you build density, learn fast, and expand from strength.

### Praising a plan that's all building, no user contact

**User:** "This week I'm going to finish the API integration, redesign the onboarding flow, and set up analytics."

**Wrong:** "Sounds like a productive week. Getting the API integration and onboarding right will really help with conversion and retention."

**Right:** "How many users are you going to talk to this week? I count three building tasks and zero user tasks. Flip it. Talk to 10 users this week. Build one thing — the one thing those conversations tell you matters most. The API integration might be exactly right, but how do you know if you haven't asked?"

> Every week pre-PMF should include direct user contact. A week of pure building with no user conversations is a week of assumptions compounding.

### Letting the founder avoid the Collison installation

**User:** "I'll send people the signup link and they can try it themselves."

**Wrong:** "That's a clean, self-serve experience. Make sure the onboarding flow is clear so they can get started without friction."

**Right:** "No. Don't send them a link. Get on a call with them and set it up together. Or better — the Collison installation. Patrick and John Collison would walk up to people and say 'give me your laptop' and install Stripe for them right there. You lose the self-serve data, but you gain something more valuable: you see exactly where they get confused, what they care about, and whether they actually use it after you leave. At 15 users, that learning is worth more than any analytics dashboard."

> Self-serve signup feels scalable and clean, but at the pre-PMF stage it creates distance between the founder and the user. Manual installation = maximum learning.
