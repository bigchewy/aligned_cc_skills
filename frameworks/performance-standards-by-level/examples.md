# Performance Standards by Level Examples

## STEP 1: Gather the Evidence

### Self-diagnosis — engineering manager

**User:** "I'm an engineering manager with six direct reports. I'd say my biggest wins this quarter were shipping the new auth system and catching a critical performance regression before it hit production."

**Advisor:** "Those are technical wins. When you say 'I shipped the auth system' — did you write the code, or did your team?"

**User:** "I wrote about 40% of it. The core architecture was mine."

**Advisor:** "And the performance regression — you caught that personally?"

**User:** "Yeah, I was reviewing the benchmarks and noticed the spike."

**Advisor:** "Tell me about your team's growth this quarter. Who got better at what?"

**User:** "...I haven't really thought about it that way."

> Gathers evidence: wins are personal technical achievements, not team development. Leader is operating at Passage 0 in a Passage 1 role.

### Diagnosing a direct report — VP assessing a director

**User:** "I have a director who manages three engineering managers. She's great — her teams consistently ship on time and quality is high. But something feels off."

**Advisor:** "What does she spend her time on?"

**User:** "She's in a lot of the technical architecture discussions. She runs the sprint retrospectives for all three teams. She does skip-levels with every IC quarterly."

**Advisor:** "And her three managers — how are they developing as leaders?"

**User:** "That's the thing. They're... fine? They do what she asks but they don't seem to be growing."

**Advisor:** "They're not growing because she's doing their job. She runs their retros, makes their architecture decisions, and manages their ICs through skip-levels. What's left for them to own?"

> Evidence shows a Passage 2 leader (Director) operating at Passage 1 — managing ICs instead of developing managers.

## STEP 2: Name the Gap

### Clear gap diagnosis

**Advisor:** "Here's what I see. The role requires Passage 2 — managing managers. She's operating at Passage 1 — managing others directly.

| Dimension | Required (Passage 2) | Actual (Passage 1) |
|-----------|---------------------|-------------------|
| Skills | Selecting and developing managers | Reviewing IC work, running team ceremonies |
| Time | Evaluating manager effectiveness | Architecture reviews, skip-level meetings |
| Work Values | 'My managers develop great teams' | 'My teams ship great work' |

This isn't a performance problem. Her teams ship on time — she's doing Passage 1 work well. But her role needs Passage 2 work, and her managers aren't growing because she does their job for them."

**User:** "So she's not underperforming, she's just performing at the wrong level?"

**Advisor:** "Exactly. And it'll look fine until the org scales. Then the managers she hasn't developed will fail, and she'll try to compensate by doing even more Passage 1 work. It compounds."

> Frames the diagnosis without judgment. Names the compounding risk.

## STEP 3: Readiness Assessment

### Coachable — missing awareness

**Advisor:** "Three questions. First: does she know she's operating at Passage 1?"

**User:** "No. She thinks she's doing great. The teams are shipping."

**Advisor:** "That's common. Passage 1 success looks like organizational success until it doesn't. Second: if she understood the gap, would she be willing to let go of the technical work?"

**User:** "I think so. She wants to grow. She just doesn't know what 'up' looks like."

**Advisor:** "Third: does your organization support the transition? Do you model Passage 3 behavior? Or do you reward her Passage 1 work?"

**User:** "Honestly, I probably reinforce it. I praise the technical wins because they're visible."

**Advisor:** "So she's coachable — she's willing but not yet aware, and the system reinforces the wrong passage. You need to fix both: have the conversation with her AND change what you celebrate."

> Identifies that the system (the VP's behavior) contributes to the passage problem.

### Misplaced — not willing

**Advisor:** "Does he know he's operating at the wrong level?"

**User:** "I've told him several times. He knows."

**Advisor:** "And is he willing to let go?"

**User:** "He says he is, but then he's back in the code reviews the next week."

**Advisor:** "That's not willingness — that's compliance without commitment. Third question: does the culture support the shift?"

**User:** "Yes, we've invested in manager development."

**Advisor:** "Then you have someone who's aware, supported, but not willing. His identity is attached to being technical. The honest conversation is: 'This role requires letting go of the technical work. If that's not something you want, a Staff Engineer role might be a better fit.' That's not demotion — it's alignment."

> Honest about when the right answer is a different role, not more development.

## STEP 4: Build the Development Plan

### Passage 1 → Passage 2 transition plan

**Advisor:** "Here's the plan.

**Stop:**
- Stop running sprint retrospectives for your managers' teams
- Stop attending architecture reviews that your managers should own
- Stop doing quarterly skip-levels with every IC — your managers should know their people
- Stop making hiring decisions for your managers' teams

**Start:**
- Hold weekly one-on-ones with each manager focused on THEIR leadership development, not team status
- Evaluate each manager by how well their team performs WITHOUT your intervention
- Ask each manager to present their team's development plan quarterly
- Observe your managers' one-on-ones twice a quarter — coach them on how they coach

**Progress indicators:**
- Her calendar shifts from team-level meetings to manager-level conversations
- Her managers start making architecture decisions she would have made
- She describes wins as 'my managers handled X' instead of 'my teams shipped Y'
- At least one manager gets promoted or takes on expanded scope within 12 months

**Timeline:** Check in at 30, 60, and 90 days. The first month will feel like she's doing less. She is. That's the point."

> Concrete, specific, measurable. No generic leadership advice.

## STEP 5: The Honest Conversation

### Preparing a VP to have the conversation

**Advisor:** "The conversation sounds like this: 'You're doing Passage 1 work in a Passage 2 role. Your teams ship on time — that's not the issue. The issue is that your managers aren't growing because you do their job. Here's what needs to change: you stop running retros, you stop doing architecture reviews, you stop skip-levels. You start developing your managers as leaders. I'll check in at 30, 60, and 90 days.'

Lead with respect — she's good at what she does. Be specific about what changes. And stop praising the Passage 1 wins — start asking 'how are your managers developing?' instead of 'how's the team shipping?'"

**User:** "What if she pushes back?"

**Advisor:** "Then ask: 'What happens when we double in size next year? Can you personally run retros for six teams? Review architecture for twelve squads? The model doesn't scale. Your managers need to be ready. That's your job now.'"

> Provides the exact language. Anticipates resistance with a scaling argument.
